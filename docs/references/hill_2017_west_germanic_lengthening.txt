The paper deals with two Germanic sound changes which are traditionally be-
lieved to postdate the disintegration of the Proto-Germanic parent language. The
lengthening in several monosyllables, attested in West Germanic languages, is
usually believed to be an innovation of this branch. The so-called Gothic break-
ing is similarly thought of as belonging exclusively to East Germanic. The paper
shows that there is evidence suggesting a Proto-Germanic age for parts of both
sound changes, in particular for a lengthening in monosyllabic words ending
in PGmc *-r and for a lowering of PGmc *i if followed by *r. Proto-Germanic
possessed at least three pronoun-based place adverbs formed with PGmc *-r, cf.
Goth ƕar ‘where’ from ƕa- ‘what’, þar ‘there’ from þa- ‘that’ and hēr ‘here’ from
hi- ‘this here’. The vocalism of these adverbs did not match that of the corre-
sponding pronouns on two points. First, the vowels of the adverbs were probably
long. Second, the close PGmc *ẹ̄ (Goth ē, OHG ia) of ‘here’ did not match PGmc
*i in the corresponding pronoun. The paper assumes that the long vowels of the
place adverbs emerged by a lengthening of etymologically short vowels in mon-
osyllablic words ending in PGmc *-r. The timbre difference between PGmc *ẹ̄
in ‘here’ and PGmc *i in the corresponding pronoun for ‘this here’ is accounted
for by a lowering of PGmc *i before *r. Both postulated developments must have
been operating already in Proto-Germanic times but the lowering must have
chronologically preceded the lengthening. The paper introduces the data sup-
porting the assumptions made and discusses the apparent counterevidence.

    1.   Introduction

    The paper deals with two sound changes which are traditionally described as com-
    paratively recent developments in the Germanic daughter-languages. First, the
    lengthening in several monosyllabic words ending in r, which is attested in West
    Germanic dialects such as Old High German and Old English, is usually believed to
    be an innovation of Proto-West-Germanic. Second, the so-called Gothic breaking –
    the lowering of i und u before r, h and ƕ – is commonly seen as a recent Gothic or
    Proto-East-Germanic development. I will argue that there is evidence suggesting a
    Proto-Germanic age for parts of both sound changes.
         This evidence is provided by the phonology of the Proto-Germanic adverb for
    ‘here’ in different daughter-languages. In Section 2 I will describe the system of
    Proto-Germanic pronoun-based place adverbs, to which the adverb ‘here’ belongs,
    and point out the peculiar features of this particular member of the group, the un-
    expected length and the unexpected timbre of its vowel. In Section 3 the previous
    attempts at explaining these unexpected features of ‘here’ will be sketched. I will
    demonstrate that none of these attempts satisfactorily resolves the problem. In the
    following Section 4 I will discuss the quantity of the vowel in the Germanic place
    adverbs ‘where’ and ‘there’. I will show that these adverbs, derivationally reminiscent
    of ‘here’, probably underlie the same secondary lengthening of the root vowel due
    to their monosyllabic pronunciation. The parallelism in the phonological develop-
    ment of ‘here’ on the one hand and ‘where’ and ‘there’ on the other suggests a Proto-
    Germanic date for this sound change. Section 5 will deal with the timbre of the root
    vowel of ‘here’. I will introduce evidence suggesting that the lowering of high vowels
    traditionally known as Gothic breaking may partly, i.e. as far as PGmc *i before
    Goth r is concerned, reflect a Proto-Germanic development. In Section 6, I will
    summarize how the assumptions reached in the previous sections of the paper, i.e.
    the Proto-Germanic age of parts of the two sound changes traditionally attributed
    to later times, explain the unexpected phonology of Germanic ‘here’ in a coherent
    and non-aprioristic way. In the last section of the paper, Section 7, I will discuss
    the Gothic imperative hiri ‘come here!’, which is traditionally assumed somehow
    to derive from the place adverb ‘here’. I will specify the exact relation between the
    imperative and the place adverb and demonstrate that the shape of Goth hiri is best
    explained without direct reference to the development of Germanic ‘here’.

3. Explaining Proto-Germanic ‘here’

The vocalism of Goth hēr ‘here’ and its counterparts in the other Germanic lan-
guages is a firmly established problem of Germanic historical phonology. However,
this problem does not seem to have received much attention during roughly two
centuries of research into Germanic. In the older literature, the matter is often
mentioned but not investigated in a satisfactory, non-aprioristic way. This was un-
doubtedly caused by the obvious uniqueness of the problem. The lack of other
Proto-Germanic lexemes with comparable unexpected features prevented scholars
from applying the most efficient method of investigation, i.e. from looking for re-
current correlations in the data. For this reason, most of the opinions found in the
literature are necessarily ad hoc and, therefore, of a merely historical interest today.3
     In this section, I shall only discuss the two most recent attempts to resolve
the problem in a less aprioristic manner. Both theories try to find the way out by
re-examining the morphological assumptions on which the traditional derivation
of ‘here’ is based. The attempts seem to be equally unsuccessful precisely in their
morphology.
     According to Ringe (1984: 139–145), the adverb Goth hēr etc. was not nec-
essarily derived from PGmc *hi- ‘this here’ (cf. Goth dat.sg. masc. hi-mma) in
the same way in which Goth ƕar ‘where’ and þar ‘there’ were derived from PGmc
*hwa- ‘who, what’ (cf. Goth dat.sg. masc. ƕa-mma) and PGmc *þa- ‘that’ (cf. Goth
dat.sg. masc. þa-mma). Ringe assumes that Goth hēr and its peers in the other
Germanic languages reflect the hypothetical instrumental singular of the pronoun,
PGmc *hī, 4 secondarily enlarged by the element *-r taken over from the adverbs
for ‘where’ and ‘there’. The long *ī of PGmc *hīr is assumed to have been regularly
lowered to PGmc *ẹ̄ due to the influence of the following tautosyllabic *r.

     Unlike several other monosyllabic indeclinables, PIE *ḱe ‘here’ not only fol-

cf. OLat cuius-ce, ipsius-ce but Lat ce-do, Osc ce-bnust. This strongly suggests that
PIE *ḱe was not an enclitic particle but rather a stressed adverb. According to
Wackernagel’s Law, Proto-Indo-European enclitic particles always occupied the
second position after the first stressed word in a clause, being thus bound to fol-
low their hosts in case of eventual univerbation. The same reasoning is applicable
to PIE *re, whose reflex in Latin precedes verb forms as do all originally stressed
adverbs which eventually became verbal prefixes in Latin (such as Latin per- from
per ‘through’ etc., cf. the adverb Skt pári ‘around’ and the preposition Lith per̃
‘through’).
     From this it follows that Goth hi-ri ‘come here!’ probably reflects a recent uni-
verbation of two stressed adverbs, Goth *hi (< PIE *ḱé ‘here, hither’) and Goth *ri
(< PIE *ré ‘back’). The original meaning of Goth hi-ri must have been something
like ‘back here!’, which typologically resembles such adverbially based quasi-im-
peratives as, for instance, MoG her-ein ‘come in!’ (literally ‘here in!’). The lack of
breaking in Goth hi-ri can be explained by the recent date of this univerbation,
which only occurred after breaking had ceased to operate. An even more recent
reanalysis of Goth hi-ri as hir-i, i.e. an ordinary 2sg. imperative form, gave birth to
secondary 2dual hir-jats and 2plural hir-jiþ.
