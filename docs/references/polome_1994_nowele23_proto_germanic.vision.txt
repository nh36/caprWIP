Dealing with Germanic for the purpose of comparison with the other
Indo-European languages implies, as in the case of the other north-
ern and western ancient European languages such as Slavic, Baltic
or Celtic, the prior reconstruction of the Proto-Germanic prototypes
of the historically documented linguistic forms. This entails a num-
ber of problems connected with the type of evidence available for the
oldest period: even if the earliest samples of runic inscriptions can
be dated back to the first centuries of the Christian era, no text of
any sizable length is preserved before Ulfila's translation of the
Bible into Gothic in the IVth century A.D. Thus, the actual docu-
mentation on the earliest stage of Germanic consists almost exclu-
sively of terms quoted by classical authors and of proper names
anthroponyms, ethnic names, toponyms and other elements of the
ancient geographical nomenclature. Such material offers many obvi-
ous drawbacks: apart from the conflicting graphic representations in
the later manuscript tradition, ancient authors often disagree on the
form of definite Germanic names. Confronted with alternate forms
like Inguaeones in Pliny and Ingaevones in Tacitus, Germanists will
determine which form is to be considered as the original one on the
basis of an etymologically oriented analysis linking Ingu- with OE
Ing in the Old English Runic Poem, ON Ingunar-Freyr, Yngvi, etc.,
with an underlying form *Ingw(az). Even more complex is the case
of Germanic names appearing in early sources, prior to the close
contacts of the Mediterranean world with the Germanic territory
proper, e.g., in descriptions of the penetration of the Cimbri and the
Teutons into southern Gaul and northern Italy towards the end of
the second century B.C. Thus, the leader of the Teutons was called
Teutoboduus, obviously a Celto-Latin adaptation of the Germanic

Sound substitution is a common feature in the transmission of
foreign words through non-native intermediaries, and it should be
kept in mind that the Romans especially, in their early contacts
with the Germanic world, worked through Celtic interpreters. Just
as the Arabs substituted [j] for the [g] of the Persian plural suffix in
bardegan, plural of bardeh (older bardah) 'slave' when they took
over the term under the form bardaj, or just as the name of the
American Indo-Europeanist Eric Hamp appears as <Gamp> in Rus-
sian, Germanic names appear in their Celto-Latin interpretation or
transliteration in ancient literature, and one wonders how much one
can rely on such evidence to document or date any specific phono-
logical process within Germanic. Thus, since Caesar writes alces for
Gmc. *alhiz 'elk', it is assumed that Alcis which designates the
Dioscuri in Tacitus represents Gmc. *alhez which is related either to
Go. alhs, OE ealh, OS alah 'temple', or to OE ealgian 'protect', if the
Germanic twin deities are not to be conceived as 'Elkriders'.² Unfor-
tunately, except for the name of a tribune, Balchobaudes, in Ammi-
anus Marcellinus, there is no other evidence of Gmc. -lh- in early
sources, so that it is difficult to establish if the Graecized spelling
with <ch> which prevails for initial Germanic /h/ in Tacitus, e.g., in
Chariovalda, Chasuarii, Chatti, Cherusci, might also apply in this
context. On other hand, it is worth noticing that Caesar uses
Cherusci (= [Strabo] Xnpoñoкot), but Harudes (versus [Ptolemy]
Xapoudɛç). This might well allow us to assume that the contrast
between lenis [h] and fortis [x] articulation already prevailed at that
time as far as initial Germanic /h/ versus postconsonantal internal
/h/ is concerned.

If there is, accordingly, no compelling evidence to ascribe the
development of Gmc. -k- to the presence of a laryngeal in the vicinity
of w, the irrelevance of the laryngeal theory to account for the
Germanic Verschärfung is even more obvious: apart from the many
apparent exceptions to 'Holtzmann's Law' which indicate that it did
not apply universally in Old Norse, it represents a rather common
development of 'lengthened intervocalic /w/ and /j/", which is even
evidenced in Scandinavian at a later date by Faroese. The problem
is whether the Proto-Indo-European laryngeal triggered the length-
ening of contiguous /w/ or /y/ in Proto-Germanic, as Lehmann postu-
lates (1952:63-64) or whether the lengthening of intervocalic /w/ and
/j/ is only a matter of syllable structure in early Germanic, as Frans
van Coetsem suggested as early as 1949.35 In etymologically clear
cases, it would appear that Gmc. *-ww- reflects an earlier non-gemi-
nate *-w- preceded by -u- or a -u-diphthong, and that Gmc. *-jj-
similarly derives from an earlier *-j- after -i- or an -i-diphthong. This
would imply that in a sequence like *-ewX- the laryngeal would be
lost 'without directly causing gemination in Germanic'. 36 Therefore,
recent theories on syllabification³7 will presumably provide a better
explanation for the Verschärfung, more consistent with the histori-
cal data, than the somewhat unwarranted projection of the length-
ening of Germanic -w- and -j- into a distant prehistory.
In conclusion, there is apparently no cogent evidence to assume
that Proto-Indo-European laryngeals are directly responsible for any
specifically Germanic phonological development, and it is therefore
probable that they were no longer part of the phonological system of
the western Indo-European dialect from which Germanic emerged.38
