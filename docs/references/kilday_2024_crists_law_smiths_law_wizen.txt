Crist’s Law, Smith’s Law, and English wizen
Douglas G. Kilday
Independent Researcher

(Draft Version of 20 June 2024)

ABSTRACT: Investigation shows that Crist’s Law *-(e/i)z(d/n)- > *-(ē/ī)(d/n)- operated in
Old Saxon and Old Frisian, but not directly in Old English, so it should not be ascribed to the
Ingvaeonic stage. Apparent failure of Verner’s Law in the OE weak verb wisnian and strong
participles -risen (from two distinct verbs), miþen, wriþen, and -broþen is explained by
Smith’s accent-retraction in Proto-Indo-European and Kortlandt’s metathesis of laryngeal
residues in Proto-Germanic. This further allows the OE homonyms wisnian and weornian
to be analyzed as zero-grade outcomes of PIE *u̯ ei̯- ‘allow to ripen’ vel sim. extended as
*u̯ ei̯-h₁-s- and *u̯ ei̯-s-.


At a linguistic conference in 2002, Sean Crist proposed that *z-deletion in
West Germanic, which had been described as sporadic by earlier authors, was
in fact the result of regular processes.1 In addition to the well-known West
Germanic loss of Proto-Germanic word-final *z in unstressed syllables, Crist
recognized two environments for deletion of *z in the Ingvaeonic languages
(Old English, Old Saxon, and Old Frisian) prior to its rhotacism and merger
with original *r. One is word-final position in stressed monosyllables, namely
the PGmc pronouns *miz ‘me’ and *wīz ‘we’, and the adverb *maiz ‘more’.
Gothic simply devoices final *z in these words (mis, weis, mais), while Old
Norse (mēr, vēr, meir) and Old High German (mir, wī ̆r, mēr) rhotacize it. Old
English (mē, wē, mā) deletes it, as does Old Saxon in the pronouns (mī; wī, we),
which are apparently not recorded in Old Frisian. For the adverb ‘more’, Old
Saxon and Old Frisian attest both mē and mēr, the latter easily explained by
analogical restoration. A parallel restoration underlies the Modern English
adverb more.

   Crist’s other environment for *z-deletion is the position following a short
stressed high vowel (*e, *i) and preceding another consonant (*d, *n), which
results in compensative lengthening. He wrote the rule semi-algebraically:

   *z > ∅ {*i, *e} _ $ with compensatory lengthening of preceding vowel.

Complications immediately arise with the word for ‘reward, payment’. The
usual West Saxon form of this word is mēd, and poetic Modern English meed
suggests that mēd was also the Mercian form. However meord (once written
by error meorð) and meard are more common in Northumbrian, whose
scribes typically confused -eo- and -ea-. Other West Germanic languages agree
with mēd in deleting the *z with compensative lengthening (against Gothic
mizdō, and locally with *-izd- > *-ezd- first): Old Frisian mēde, mīde, meide; Old
Saxon mēda, mieda; Old High German mēta, miata, mieta. Since there are no
comparable examples of secondary *z-deletion in OHG, Crist considered mēta
to be a loanword from Ingvaeonic, arguing that the borrowing of a word for
‘payment’ would be unremarkable in a trade situation. His explanation for OE
meord beside mēd is as follows: “Continental Pre-OE was originally spoken in
Jutland at the northern periphery of the Ingvaeonic continuum; so perhaps the
application of the rule in polysyllabic words did not spread this far north. The
existence of OE mēd beside meord is not surprising if Old English is the
product of some degree of dialect mixture, as is generally assumed.” In other
words, Crist was compelled to invent a new protolanguage, Peripheral
Northern Ingvaeonic, spoken at the very tip of Jutland, which escaped his rule,
and from which OE meord was, in effect, borrowed. The same would apply to
heordan ‘hards of flax’, the other OE word which he recognizes with a high
vowel before *-zd- in the protoform. I have been unable to find any
publication by Sean Crist which updates his conference paper, or any review
of it, so I will attempt a brief review here.
    Firstly, if OHG mēta can be an unremarkable loanword from Ingvaeonic
(presumably Old Saxon mēda, borrowed into northwestern Old German
before the High German consonant-shift reached it, and then regularly shifted
to mēta), then so can Proto-OE *mēda > OE mēd be a loanword from the
Saxon-Frisian area, with meord the regular OE outcome of inherited PGmc

Saxono-Frisia (or part of it), after the opportunistic band of Angles, Saxons,
and Jutes (or most of them) had already settled in Britain. In this view the

    The missionary hypothesis for OFris lirna/lerna from OE is quite plausible,
given that the related causative, OE lǣran ‘to teach’, was borrowed into Old
Norse as lǣra. But as with meord, I find it more economical to assume that the
deletion *-izn- > *-īn-, like *-izd- (> *-ezd-) > *-ēd-, operated only in Old Saxon
and Old Frisian after the separation of Old English, which was effectively
complete by the year 500. If this restriction of Crist’s Law to Saxono-Frisian is
accepted, OE twīn for expected *tweorn cannot be a native word, which is
plausible enough since it means ‘linen’, a traded commodity. Middle English
twīn, twyne ‘twine’ also refers to a traded commodity, as shown by the Dutch
doublet tweern/twijn, the first native, the second borrowed from Frisian.
Evidently part of Saxono-Frisia was the site of flax processing on such a large
scale as to make exports profitable. The aberrant meaning of OE twīn ‘linen’
against ‘twisted thread, twine’ elsewhere (including Middle High German
zwirn, Low German twern, dialectal Swedish tvinne) is explicable if OE actually
borrowed the adjective twīnen ‘made of twisted (flaxen) thread’ from
OS/OFris *twīnan in connection with imported linen, whence the OE gloss
twínen ‘bissina’ i.e. ‘of linen’. This would lead to the back-formation of twín
‘byssus’ i.e. ‘linen’. But in Late OE twīn ‘linen’ was supplanted by līnen, a
nominalization of the adjective ‘flaxen’, from līn ‘flax’. That cleared the way
for the direct borrowing of twīn (in Late OE or Early ME) from Saxono-Frisia
in its original sense of ‘twisted (flaxen) thread’.

learn’, which is a glaring formal anachronism. The original *-é-, later posttonic
*-i-, of causatives must have been syncopated very soon after the shift from
inherited to prototonic accent, and is reflected in no attested language, so the
usual way of citing this one is *laizjan-. OE leornian does not continue *liznōn
directly, but the extended form *liznōjan-, like other Class II weak verbs. An
analogical restoration to *liznōjan- would have had to act on Proto-OE
*līnōjan- from the associated causative *lāzjan-, which seems rather
implausible. One may also note OE (for)weornian ‘marcescere, tabescere,

    There is however another Class II weak verb overlapping in meaning, OE
(for)wisnian, continued as Middle English wisenen ‘to shrivel up, to wizen’, and
cognate with ON visna ‘to wither’. This verb presents a riddle of its own, since
Class II weak stems are referred to the PIE shape *R(z)-néh₂-, and root-final *s
(outside of Gothic) is expected to undergo Verner’s voicing to PGmc *z, as in
*liznōn- < *lis-néh₂- from the root *lei̯s-. I believe the riddle can be solved by
the application of two soundlaws already proposed. The first of these is
Smith’s accent-retraction. In developing a laryngeal-based pretonic theory of
the Germanic Verschärfung, Henry Lee Smith Jr. encountered the apparent
double development of pretonic PIE *ruHw-, which he wrote as Indo-Hittite
*ruAw-, the zero grade of an IH root *réwAw-.7 According to his theory, labial
Verschärfung (Gothic and North Germanic *-ggw-, West Germanic *-ww-)
should be the regular outcome of the pretonic zero grade, IH *ruAw-´ > IE
*ruhw-´ > PGmc *ruγw-´ > Swedish rugg ‘shaggy hair’, dialectal Norwegian
rugga ‘carpet of shaggy hair, rug’. But Old Norse rȳja ‘to pluck wool’ implied
PGmc *rūw-, which Smith found baffling, for his theoretical framework did not
admit a zero grade originating under the accent. One possibility he proposed
was an accent-shift “in certain classes of words” retracting the accent to the
zero-grade root vowel before the absorption of the following laryngeal.8

     Combined with Northwestern pretonic shortening and Cowgill’s fortition,
Smith’s Law helps to explain the discrepancy between East Gmc. *kwiwa-
‘alive’ in Gothic qiwa-, qius and Northwest Gmc. *kwikwa- in OE cwic(u), ON
kvik(k)r, etc. Pretonic shortening involves laryngeal deletion after a PIE high
vowel (*i, *u) and before a resonant (*r, *l, *m, *n, *i̯, *u̯ ) followed by an
accented vowel in Proto-Northwest Indo-European, whose daughters include
Italic, Celtic, and Germanic. The best-known examples are PIE *u̯ iHró- ‘man’ >
PNWIE *u̯ iró- and *suHnú- ‘son’ > *sunú-. This shortening is sometimes called
Dybo’s Law, but it is quite different from what Vladimir A. Dybo proposed to
account for the shortening he observed in Celtic and Italic (but not Germanic)
by backdating Balto-Slavic accentual mobility to PIE. Some authors use it
indiscriminately, as though it were a sporadic process, and if justification for
irregularity is required, they appeal to counter-examples, none of which is
probative. Latin fūmus and pūrus are commonly referred to *dʱuH-mó- and
*puH-ró- to illustrate failure of pretonic shortening. But fūmus need not be
cognate with Greek θῡμός and the like; it can continue Old Latin *fusmos <
*dʱus-mó- from the recognized root *dʱu̯ es-. The ritual term pūrus could easily

simple glides, as in Go. hāwi ‘hay’ and OHG tāju ‘I suck’, Austin had to posit a

with hypothesizing that at least one laryngeal became k in Germanic before w.

‘fallen’ can develop into ‘ziemen’, i.e. ‘zusammenfallen mit’, which is the sense
attested by OE gerīsan, OS girīsan, and OHG garīsan.

meant roughly ‘rinnen’, i.e. ‘flow, run, move as liquid does spontaneously’. The
archetypal liquid, water, can rise from a spring or fall as rain, and the level of a
body of water can rise or fall due to a net influx or efflux. In this view there is
no “Umkehrung der Bewegungsrichtung” involved, and Torp was correct to
connect the Gmc. verbs with Ved. riṇā́ ti, since the underlying idea is flowing,
not rising. The extended root thus belongs with *h₃rei̯H-, itself a double
extension of PIE *h₃er- ‘set oneself in motion’. The force of the *-s- is no more
“desiderative” than it is in *peh₂-s- ‘feed and protect one’s own (livestock)’ and
*kh₂ei̯-s- ‘comb one’s own (hair)’. In *h₃rei̯H-s- it referred originally to water
(or liquid in general) seeking its own level.

enough to produce a cross-form for-weosnian. Presumably PGmc *wisnōn and

‘to come to maturity, to mature’ and *wisnōn- ‘to come to old age, to reach the

verb. The fact that OE weornian and wisnian are practically synonymous, both
meaning ‘to wither’ and the like, probably results from euphemism and its
reaction. One may compare the periodical Modern Maturity, published by an
American retirement association, which would more accurately be labeled
Modern Old Age. Using ‘maturity’ as a euphemism for ‘old age’ eventually
leads to ‘maturity’ being understood as ‘old age’. I suspect that PGmc *wiznōn
was used as a euphemism for *wisnōn, and gradually took over the force of
*wisnōn. But when OE weornian became fully synonymous with wisnian, it
also became superfluous, and only the latter has survived into Modern English
as wizen.
