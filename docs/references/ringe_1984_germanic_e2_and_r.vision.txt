1. Introduction
The origin of Germanic 'e' has been debated for over a century, but no
one view has been generally accepted'; in this paper I shall try to establish
the origin of some examples of this perplexing vowel. The problem can be
stated briefly as follows. Of the late Proto-Indo-European long vowels and
diphthongs, *, *, *ō, *ai, and *au remained in Proto-Germanic; *ā
merged with *ō; *oi and *ou merged with *ai and *au respectively; by the
process called i-umlaut, *ei merged with *, while *eu was split (subphone-
mically) into *eu and *iu; and *ē appeared as *ē, which remained in Gothic
but was lowered and backed to *ā in North and West Germanic. Long
diphthongs (*oi, *ou, etc.) seem to have merged either with the correspond-
ing short diphthongs or with long vowels, except in word-final position;
their Proto-Germanic reflexes in that environment need not concern us
here. However, straightforward reconstruction of Proto-Germanic also
yields another long vowel, usually designated '2', which remained ē in all
the Germanic languages and was not shifted to a². Few reconstructable
lexemes exhibit this vowel; its Proto-Indo-European sources remain a
matter of doubt.

2. Earlier theories
It would be pointless to review here all earlier theories concerning the
origin of e2; I shall summarize the six most important ones briefly, pointing
out the major objections that can be raised against each.
The simplest theory is also the one best supported by evidence outside
Germanic; it attempts to explain only the 2 of her 'here'. Since other
Indo-European locative adverbs in -r usually exhibit short stem vowels
(e.g. Albanian kur, Lithuanian kur 'where'; Lith. kitur 'elsewhere'; within
Germanic, Go. and ON par 'there', and Go. har, ON hvar 'where'), we
expect a Proto-Germanic form *hir 'here'; Old Frisian (OF) hir and OS hir
(/her) could contain short vowels, and thus can represent survivals of such
a form. In some areas *hir can have become *her, which can have been
lengthened under the accent to *her, just as Proto-Germanic (PG) *þar
'there' and *hwar 'where' were lengthened to *pār and *hwar in West
Germanic. Warren Cowgill (personal communication) has suggested such
a line of development; a similar theory was proposed by Johannes Schmidt
(1875: 414-5, 422 ff.) and extended by several scholars of the late nine-
teenth century (cf. Möller 1879: 511 and 1899: 116), though Schmidt and
his followers failed to state the conditions under which lengthening oc-
curred. I find this theory implausible for the following reason. PG *þar and
*hwar were lengthened under the accent only in West Germanic; yet the
theory under discussion maintains that *her was lengthened under the
accent in all branches of Germanic. Unless a good reason for this discrepan-
cy can be found, it seems preferable to suppose that the vowel of her was
originally long.

I have not accounted for examples of 2 not followed by r; however, I

6. Conclusion
The results of this investigation can be summarized as follows. The
pre-Proto-Germanic word for 'here' was *hi, a direct reflex of the Proto-
Indo-European instrumental singular * 'with this'. The final *r of PG
*par 'there' and *hwar 'where' was analogically added to *hi, giving PG
*hir 'here'. The * of this word was lowered subphonemically to *[e] before
the following tautosyllabic *r within Proto-Germanic; this lowered allo-
phone merged with PG */e/ in Gothic but became part of a new */ē/
phoneme in North and West Germanic (PG */e/ having there become */ā/).
In Old High German the Proto-Germanic rule lowering * before tautosyl-
labic *r was generalized to affect all cases of the sequence *ir; apparent
exceptions are later loans, late developments, or words of unknown or
doubtful origin. Of the examples of e₂ not accounted for by this theory, one
is a word of obscure origin found only in Gothic and Old High German, two
are West Germanic words of obscure origin, and the rest developed within
High German by processes not well understood.
It is therefore not necessary to posit a phoneme *2 or a sequence *ei for
Proto-Germanic.
