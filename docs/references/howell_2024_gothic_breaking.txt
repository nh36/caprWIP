                                              4.1 Introduction

Gothic is well known as the only attested early Germanic dialect that shows no
evidence of Umlaut processes; the only conditioned vocalic mutations in Gothic
are effected by following consonants, and the most salient of these is the so-
called Gothic breaking (or Gothic lowering), the lowering (or non-raising) of
PGmc. * /i/, * /e/, * /u/ before Gothic <h>, <ƕ>, and <r>. The use of graphemic
brackets < > with <h>, <ƕ>, and <r> is intentional, since we are on the one hand
poorly informed about what these orthographic symbols represent phonetically,
but on the other hand, the phonetic realization of these graphemes is crucial to our
understanding of Gothic breaking as a process. The purpose of this contribution
is to (re)sift the data in order to determine what we can and perhaps cannot ever
know about Gothic breaking. In her recent brilliant work on the history of Old
English palatalization of PGmc. * /k/ and * /g/ Minkova (2003, 2014, 2015) lays
bare the challenge:

   While the primacy of written sources in historical phonology seems self-evident,
   the interpretation of spelling in relation to the inventory of contrastive entities is
   riddled with problems. The accessible ‘facts’ in the history of Western languages
   are recorded in alphabetic writing systems … yet no written document, at least
   prior to the IPA, can be taken as a precise record of pronunciation.
                                                                  Minkova 2015: 72–3

The fact is, we have only clues regarding the phonetic qualities represented by the
Gothic graphemes <h>, <ƕ>, and <r>, which in turn makes it difficult to isolate
what is going on in the Gothic breaking process. That said, we will argue that

                         4.2 An overview of Gothic breaking
Gothic breaking is a remarkably regular process unaffected by analogical innov-
ations and relatively unburdened by troubling exceptions or irregularities.¹ As is
well known, the Gothic system of short vowels develops from a reconstructed
Proto-Germanic system of four vowels. A salient feature of Gothic is the uncondi-
tioned merger of PGmc. * /e/ and * /i/ as Gothic /i/, though this otherwise regular
merger is disrupted or, alternatively, subsequently lowered before <h>, <ƕ>, and
<r>, where we find the Gothic digraph <ai>, generally accepted as representing
a low mid front vowel [ɛ].² There is no parallel raising in the back vowels since
Proto-Germanic lacks a mid back /o/, but reflexes of PGmc. * /u/ are realized by
the Gothic digraph <au> in the same environments, i.e. before <h>, <ƕ>, and <r>.
Graphemic <au> is generally thought to represent a low mid back vowel [ɔ]:

   Exceptions to Gothic breaking are few. Reflexes of PGmc. * /i/ and * /u/ do
not undergo breaking as seen in the verbal and nominal prefix bi- (Go. biháitja

‘arise’; * uz * rikw iza > Go. ur riqiza ‘out of the darkness’). The adverbial compound
du-ƕe ‘why’ does not show breaking, nor does Gothic fidur- ‘four’, found only
in compounds. Finally, the defective verb attested six times in apparent impera-
tives only, hiri, hirjats, hirjiþ ‘come hither’ does not show breaking (see Cercignani
1984, 1986). The most comprehensive and convincing treatment of these appar-
ent exceptions to breaking is provided by Hill 2017, who notes that all of these
forms are compounds with original morpheme boundaries between a short vowel
and the potential breaking conditioner and that the compounds could well have
originated after the period of breaking was complete.

of these changes must be viewed as independent of the others, they obviously share
characteristics and should be weighed as we attempt to determine the phonetics

                   4.3 Gothic breaking in the literature
Standard handbooks on early Germanic and Gothic generally remain silent on
breaking as a phonetic process and simply state the facts with no commentary (cf.
Streitberg 1910: 66–7; Prokosch 1939: 114; Krause 1953: 77–81; Hempel 1966: 21;
Braune and Heidermanns 2004: 32–6). Only occasionally is an oblique reference
to breaking to be detected in the discussion of the pronunciation of one of the
conditioning consonants, and there seems to be a somewhat widespread view that
breaking could only have been conditioned by ‘strongly’ articulated consonants.
Streitberg (1920: 61), who recognizes that Gothic /h/ must have been a phonetic
[h] by the time of the origin of the extant manuscripts (i.e. sixth century AD),
but claims that the precursor of this [h] must have been a buccal spirant: ‘Zur
Zeit der Brechung kann die Verminderung des Reibegeräusches bei der urgerm.
x noch nicht weit vorgeschritten sein.’ [‘At the time of breaking the reduction of
the fricative Proto-Germanic x cannot have proceeded very far.’ ] In a similar vein,
Janko (1908: 62) states without evidence or justification ‘Ohne Zweifel kann die
sogenannte gotische Brechung des i und u . . . bloß von kräftig artikulierten Lauten
h und r verursacht worden sein. Für das h jener vorwulfianischen Periode folgt
aber daraus notwendig das, daß es ein voller Spirant war.’ [‘Without a doubt the
so-called Gothic breaking of i and u can only have been caused by strongly articu-
lated h and r sounds. For the h of that pre-Wulfian period it necessarily follows
that it was a full spirant.’ ] Braune and Heidermanns (2004: 68) also reference the
phonetics of /h/ in breaking environments: ‘in allen anderen Stellungen ist noch
die Aussprache als Spirant (schwächer als der nhd. ach-Laut) anzusetzen’ [‘in all
other positions the pronunciation as a spirant (weaker than the NHG ach-Laut) is
still to be assumed’]. These are interesting claims given the fact that consonantal
conditioning factors from earliest Germanic to the present tend to be weakened
and lost immediately after the vocalic mutations they condition, as we will see
below.
   Some literature attempts to find ways to characterize the Gothic breaking con-
ditioners /r/, /h/, /ƕ/ as a natural class of some sort. Runge (1973, 1974) argues
that the frequent co-conditioning of vocalic mutations by reflexes of PGmc. * /r/
and * /x/ point to a uvular [R] in early Germanic. This runs afoul of the fact
that rhotacisms of PGmc. * /z/ in Northwest Germanic and in Gothic point to a
phonetic realization of PGmc. * /r/ as an apical trill or flap.
   Vennemann (1972), no doubt constrained by the shortcomings of early binary
feature representations of vowels and consonants, argues that Gothic /r/, /h/, /ƕ/
share only the feature [–high] that might be construed as a possible conditioning of

a vowel lowering. He makes an important point that the feature [–high] ‘is shared
by several consonants not involved in this rule’ (Vennemann 1972: 872). But Ven-
nemann also admits that there is no particular reason to characterize Gothic /r/,
/h/, /ƕ/ as [+low]: ‘I simply write [+low] although the segments /h hw r/ are not
low in an absolute sense’ (Vennemann 1972: 873).
   A recent study by Kostakis (2015) provides a completely different analysis,
characterizing Gothic Lowering (= Gothic breaking) as a dissimilatory process
conditioned by [r], [x], and [xw ] which are assigned the feature [HIGH]. Aside
from the general concern that dissimilatory processes are relatively rare compared
to assimilatory ones, it is not at all clear that any of the conditioning factors of
Gothic breaking can be characterized as [HIGH], and Kostakis has to make some
rather unconventional assumptions, including the claim that the phonetic value
of Gothic <h> and <ƕ> was a velar fricative [x] and [xw ] respectively. In order
to make this work, Gothic <g> is represented in all positions as a stop [g] via
‘gamma fortition’ (Kostakis 2015: 191), a possibility that has been rejected by most
scholars who assume a medial fricative [Ɣ ] or final [x] as the phonetic realization
of the Gothic grapheme <g> (see Roberge 1984 for a lucid discussion of the rather
extensive literature on Gothic final -g).
   To date the ‘natural class’ arguments suffer from two main weaknesses. First,
they fail to account for Gothic-internal evidence that sheds light on the phonet-
ics of the conditioning consonants and prematurely assign phonological features
for which there is little or no evidence. Second, they offer no explanation for the
fact that only Gothic /r/, /h/, /ƕ/ condition breaking even though other conson-
ants possess the alleged crucial phonological feature. Kostakis (2015) does not, for
example, explain why other consonants with the feature [HIGH], such as /k/, /kw /,
/g/ do not condition breaking.



                        4.4 A return to the evidence

Before making any decision about what might unify Gothic /r/, /h/, /ƕ/ as condi-
tioners of breaking, it is worth reviewing the evidence for the phonetic realization
of each of these conditioning factors yielded by the extant Gothic texts. For Gothic
/r/ this is a relatively simple matter, but for /h/, /ƕ/ a number of issues arise
that have generated a fairly substantial scholarly debate, most of which is sum-
marized in Roberge (1984). In particular, the status of Gothic /g/ on the one hand
and /h/, /ƕ/ on the other has generated considerable discussion. In any event,
the graphemes <h>, <ƕ>, <r> all represent phonemes that have a wide range of
phonetic realizations across Germanic, so careful sifting of the limited evidence
yielded by the extant Gothic manuscripts is essential.

   Across Germanic, the influence of rhotics on preceding vowels occurs primar-
ily when the rhotic is in the syllable coda—in very many instances it is the only
environment in which vocalic mutations occur. When a post-vocalic rhotic in
syllable-initial position affects a preceding vowel, as in Gothic (e.g. baíran ‘bear’),
the same effect is found before the rhotic in the coda (e.g. waír ‘man’). In short,
there is an implicational hierarchy which sees the coda rhotics as the primary
conditioning environment for vocalic mutations and the syllable-initial rhotics as
secondary conditioners.

              4.5 Pressing questions and a possible answer

As we review all of the discussion above, a number of questions present them-
selves:

     1) Why does a specific subset of post-vocalic consonants (liquids, glides,
        and weakened reflexes of PGmc. * /x/) repeatedly condition vowel muta-
        tions in early Germanic while other consonants (stops and fricatives)
        rarely do?
     2) What phonetic characteristic(s) do liquids, glides, and [h] share?
     3) While early and modern data give us some clues to the phonetics
        behind these mutations, is there a way to accommodate these data
        phonologically?

One key shared diachronic tendency of post-vocal or syllable coda liquids, glides,
and weakened reflexes of PGmc. * /x/ is that they tend to vocalize over time, though
they tend to yield different vocalized forms. Laterals show a strong tendency to
vocalize as high vowels (e.g. PGmc. * ald ‘old’, Du. oud

and [h] tend to vocalize as [a]-like vowels:

   Already in 1977 Catford began to develop a definition of a new major class
feature approximant: ‘. . . an articulatory channel just a very little wider than
that of a fricative, is called an approximant. . . . a voiced approximant has non-
turbulent air-flow through a channel slightly larger than that of a fricative’ (Catford
1977: 119). Clements 1990 sees the major class feature of [approximant] as neces-
sary, since it captures natural classes not expressed by other features. McCarthy
(1994) argues that given the Catford/Clements definition of approximants, all
Semitic gutturals including [h] are approximants. Hall (2009: 316) characterizes
approximants (including vowels, glides, and liquids) as ‘those segments which
have constriction in the vocal tract which allows frictionless escape of air, while
this is not the case for [–approximant] sounds (i.e. for stops, fricatives, and nasals)’.
   Given this definition of approximants, if we return to the small array of early
Germanic consonantally conditioned sound changes presented in (2) above,
including Gothic breaking, it is immediately apparent that all of these changes
are conditioned by some subset of the class of approximants. It therefore seems
obvious that the key to interaction between vowels and following consonants in
Germanic is the feature [approximant], or, if one prefers a purely phonetic charac-
terization, ‘those segments which have constriction in the vocal tract which allows
frictionless escape of air’.
   It is important to note that [approximant] only explains the blurring of bound-
aries between vowel and approximant segments; the phonetic nature of the
individual approximants will determine the nature of their influence on preceding
vowels. This fact is clear in developments such as Old English breaking, where the
conditioning factors are lC, rC, h, w, but the vocalic segments affected are different.
Only h, for example, causes breaking of long front vowels and l causes breaking

only of non-high short front vowels (i.e. of pre-OE * /æ/ and sometimes * /e/).
So the feature [approximant] only serves as the catalyst for the vowel–consonant
interaction, it does not determine the actual phonetic character of the vocalic
mutation, though approximants with less stricture of the vocal tract (i.e. rhotics
and [h]) would tend to promote lowering of adjacent vowels. The many variants
of laterals and rhotics, often present in different phonetic contexts in the same
dialect, can be expected to exert different types of influence.⁸


                                        4.6 Conclusion

The issue of consonantally conditioned sound changes has vexed Germanic lin-
guists since the nineteenth century, but little consensus emerged on how to
account for developments in the various early Germanic dialects that are on the
one hand strikingly similar but on the other hand different in the particulars of
conditioning and outcome. While it is clear that reflexes of specific early Ger-
manic consonants, especially of PGmc. * /x/, * /xw /, * /r/ were particularly active
in these processes across Germanic, there was no clear indication that these con-
ditioners form any kind of a natural class. A number of red herring assumptions
have clouded the picture. First of all, many researchers assumed that the reflexes
of PGmc. * /x/ causing so many vocalic mutations, among them Gothic breaking,
must have been a phonetic [x]. This assumption, despite the fact that the data point
to a close association between vocalic mutations and reduction and loss of reflexes
of * /x/, led some scholars to seek an answer in consonantal place features unifying
the two conditioners (e.g. that h and r were both uvular).
   We argue here that PGmc. * /x/ developed to [h] in all positions at a very early
period, conditioned vocalic mutations, and then in very many instances was lost
entirely. This [h], along with glides and liquids, belonged to a class of approximants
that tended to interact diachronically with preceding vowels as a result of the non-
abrupt transition from vowel to following approximant. Gothic breaking is but
one manifestation of this broader tendency across Germanic.
