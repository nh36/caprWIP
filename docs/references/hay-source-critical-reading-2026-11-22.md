# Hay article initial source-critical reading, 22 November 2026

This is a bounded reading of the newly ingested priority sources. It does not
adopt or reject a chronology and does not modify the CAPR model.

## Charles Jones (1989)

In the Lindisfarne-Gospels discussion, Jones presents `<gefraign->`,
`<gefreign->`, and `<heig>` alongside `<gefregn>`, `<heg>`, and `<degn>`.
He treats these as evidence that a palatal `[j]` could remain intact and
produce diphthongization in the preceding vowel space, at least as an
orthographic manifestation. [@Jones1989, printed p. 21; verified against
the original page image.]

Later, Jones explicitly sets `[hej]`/`[heij]` for ‘hay’ among the Old English
to Middle English diphthongization material and describes this as palatal
glide vocalization. [@Jones1989, printed pp. 149 and 162; verified against
the original page images.]

**Interpretive distinction:** Jones describes an observed alternation and a
possible diphthong-producing process. He does not by itself establish that
the OE short-vowel and long-vowel phonological reconstruction of *hay* is
unique or that gemination/apocope chronology follows the same route.

## Donka Minkova (2014)

Minkova discusses OE *hēġ* ‘hay’, also spelled `<heig>`, and OE *weġ* ‘way’
as an objection to analyses claiming contrastive long and short diphthongs.
Her argument is that if diphthongal length were contrastive, the expected
contrast /eːj/ in *hay* versus /ej/ in *way* would have reasons to be
preserved; it was not.

**Supersession notice (22 November 2026, 400 dpi image verification):** an
earlier reading reported the printed form as *hæg*. Direct inspection of the
page image (printed p. 178, PDF sheet 198) shows italic *hēġ* with macron
on the vowel and dot on the ġ. The earlier *hæg* report is superseded.
This is a theoretical argument against the *ea/eo/ie* short-diphthong
reconstruction, not a direct transcription of all manuscript occurrences.
[@Minkova2014, printed p. 178; verified against the original page image.]

Minkova also gives a concise West Germanic/Yod/`<g>` correspondence table
for *haga*, *hege*, *sagu*, and *sege*. [@Minkova2014, printed p. 64;
verified against the original page image.]

**Interpretive distinction:** Minkova's discussion anticipates part of the
question: spellings alone do not establish that *heig* encodes a
contrastively long diphthong. Independent evidence must decide whether the
spelling reflects `[ej]` sequence, glide vocalization, or an independent
lengthening process.

## Tomasz Mokrowiecki (2015)

Mokrowiecki argues that acute accent marks in the two studied Late Old
English manuscripts can be useful indicators of quantity, not merely
sporadic or rhetorical marks. The major factor determining accent use is
the marked vowel's quantity, though short vowels can also receive accents
and some accents are misplaced. [@Mokrowiecki2015, pp. 411--412;
representative original page images have been checked.]

**Hay implication:** Manuscript accent evidence can supply independent
support for quantity, but it cannot automatically be transferred to
Lindisfarne forms or to *hay* without a direct attested mark on the relevant
form. The source is useful for methodology and controls.

## Stiles (1995) and 2018 revised extraction

The complete revised Stiles paper is native-text searchable and has 38 PDF
sheets. The 1995 printed chapter is pp. 177--220 in the containing
*Friesische Studien II* volume. A complete and a merged companion scan are
preserved. The 2018 revised text is not treated as a substitute for the
1995 print pagination.

The original 1995 chapter is now OCRed and locally searchable in
`docs/references/local/stiles/stiles_1995_pp177-220.txt`. The OCR is
mapped to printed pp. 177--220 and was visually checked at the opening
p. 177, the morphology discussion at p. 191, and the final bibliography
pages ending at p. 220.

The 2018 revised extraction discusses shared English and Frisian
developments, nominal morphology, and the possibility of independent
selection rather than borrowing. It remains distinct from the 1995
printed chapter.

## Nelson Goering (2025/2026)

Goering's article rejects a universal compensatory-lengthening account of
post-consonantal h-loss and argues that surviving metrical or metricalized
forms do not require real h-loss lengthening. He notes `*iβ-hēg`/ivy as a
potential h-loss example but rejects it as a straightforward simple case
and considers ablaut or other explanation. [@Goering2025, printed p. 301;
verified against the original page image.]

**Hay implication:** The article does not, by itself, settle OE `hay`.
It directly cautions against converting every h-loss environment or
orthographic heavy syllable into a compensatory-lengthening rule.

## Initial assessment

1. Jones is the strongest immediate internal witness for Lindisfarne
   `heig`/`heg` being described as glide or diphthongization evidence.
2. Minkova supplies the main theoretical objection to contrastive
   diphthongal length and a direct discussion of *hæg* ‘hay’.
3. Mokrowiecki supplies a cautious quantity/orthographic methodology.
4. Goering is a control against a post-consonantal h-loss lengthening
   explanation, rather than a positive witness for *hay*.
5. The central Jones, Minkova, Mokrowiecki and Goering passages above have
   received bounded original-page checks; broader article reading is not
   asserted. Stiles pp. 177, 191 and 220 were also image-checked.
