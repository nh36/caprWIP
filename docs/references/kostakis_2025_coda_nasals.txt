Abstract A growing body of research on phonological features claims that they are
not universal elements of language. Rather, it argues that they are emergent, meaning
that they are encoded based on phonetic cues as well as sociohistorical factors. In
emergent feature theory, features are probabilistically encoded. The clearest phonetic
cues result in the clearest featural encoding. But phonetic cues can be blurrier for
certain segments and in certain contexts. In these situations, multiple feature
encodings are possible. Little research has explored the range of emergent specifi-
cations that are possible. This paper argues that coda nasals in early Germanic
languages were marked with the feature [low], while onsets were not. Due to the
lowness specification of coda nasals, the blocking of Northwest Germanic a-umlaut,
Proto-Germanic pre-nasal raising, and high vowel lowering conditioned by coda
nasals and post-velar fricatives in Old Norse can be accounted for in a novel and
straightforward manner.

Keywords Nasals · Rhinoglottophilia · a-umlaut · Pre-nasal raising ·
Vowel lowering · Germanic phonology


1 Introduction

In Germanic language history, it is well understood that the high back tonic vowel
of Northwest Germanic (NWGmc) languages, *[u], lowered to the corresponding
mid vowel, *[o], when a non-high vowel followed in a neighboring syllable. This
height harmonic process is often referred to as a-umlaut (or a-mutation). It has been

described for at least 200 years, with preliminary observations going back to Grimm

(which shares its form and meaning across all NWGmc languages) from Proto-
Germanic (PGmc) *hurnan ‘horn’ (Orel 2003, 195). One curious aspect of a-umlaut,
which has been observed for nearly as long (cf. Grimm 1840, 77, 79), is that the
process was blocked by a coda nasal. For this reason, the *[u] in PGmc *hunđaz
‘dog’ is retained in the NWGmc languages, e.g. Old Norse (ON) hundr, Old Saxon
(OS) hund, Old High German (OHG) hunt, and Old English (OE) hund (Orel 2003,
193).
   While there are formal analyses of NWGmc a-umlaut (e.g., Voyles 1992, 51;
Scheer 1995), there is, to my knowledge, no account that captures the blocking effect
of intervening coda nasals. For the last 180 years, linguists have simply noted the
blocking effect of nasals as an asterisk. That asterisk is perhaps indicative that the
phonetics of nasal sounds is better understood than their phonological properties.
This article aims to fill that gap, not only by providing a phonological account of
a-umlaut blocking, but also by considering how the phonological structure of nasals
can be reconciled with their behavior in other processes, namely, PGmc pre-nasal
raising, and the lowering of high vowels in ON. It is claimed that all these changes
can be captured straightforwardly in segmental terms if coda nasals in Early
Germanic languages were marked with the vocalic feature [low], while onset nasals
were not marked with that feature.
   The concept of a [low] nasal is unconventional in phonology. No mainstream
theory of phonological features explicitly specifies nasal sounds with height features.
However, the notion of a [low] nasal does capture one undeniable fact: nasals often
exhibit interactions with vocalic height in numerous languages of the world. Thus,
there are reasons why such a specification should be expected. Furthermore,
mainstream theories of phonology are embedded within an increasingly questioned
belief that phonological features comprise an innate component of universal
grammar.
   Emergent feature theory (Mielke 2008, 2011) departs from the traditional
hypothesis of innate feature specifications and, instead, focuses on phonetic and
sociohistorical factors that shape learned phonological encoding. Despite this
departure, phonetics maintains an outsized role in the feature encoding process. For
this reason, traditional features, which are based on phonetic information, generally
make accurate natural class predictions. Mielke’s (2008, 118) analysis of a cross-
linguistic database comprising 6,077 natural class patterns reveals that the Chomsky
and Halle (1968) feature system successfully captures 70.97% of these patterns.
However, this also underscores the inadequacy of traditional feature assumptions, as
they fail to account for 29.03% of observed natural class patterns. One important
limitation of traditional feature models is that they struggle to capture contexts that
are phonetically ambiguous. Mielke (2005) illustrates this limitation with the
variability observed in how nasals and laterals are classified in different languages,
sometimes as [ continuant] sounds, and other times as [+continuant] sounds. This

variability occurs because the phonetic cues for continuancy in nasals and liquids are

   The feature [low] that marks coda nasals of Germanic languages should be
understood as an emergent feature. There are many reasons to suppose that this
specification has phonetic groundings. For example, nasal sounds and low sounds
occur articulatorily along the pharynx, specifically the nasopharynx, oropharynx and
laryngopharynx. With respect to acoustics, the nasal formant overlaps with the
perceptual space of F1, the most significant perceptual cue of vocalic height. Nasal
lowness can also be related to Matisoff (1975), who argues for ‘rhinoglottophilia’,
the fact that nasal sounds and post-velar sounds sometimes pattern together as a
natural class. The convergence of these factors has not been subject to much
phonological inquiry. But one implication is that nasal sounds should be found with
emergent vocalic phonologizations in some languages of the world: the early
Germanic language data taken up in this article suggest that nasal lowness is at least
one such phonologization.
   This article is organized as follows. Section 2 presents and analyzes phonological
processes in early Germanic languages in which coda nasals can be shown to pattern
as [low] sounds. These include NWGmc a-Umlaut and nasal blocking (Sect. 2.1),
PGmc pre-nasal raising (Sect. 2.2), and ON lowering (Sect. 2.3). A discussion
follows in Sect. 3, and conclusions are stated in Sect. 4.


2 Coda nasals as [low] sounds in early Germanic languages

In this section, I present and analyze the processes of NWGmc a-umlaut (Sect. 2.1),
PGmc pre-nasal raising (Sect. 2.2), and ON lowering (Sect. 2.3). It is claimed that
these processes support the specification of coda nasals with the feature [low] in early
Germanic languages. A final section (Sect. 2.4) considers cases of vowel nasalization
in languages where coda nasals are marked with the feature [low].

2.1 NWGmc a-Umlaut and lowness blocking

   The features [high] and [low] are interpretable as the phonologization of
articulatory or acoustic properties of high and low vowels. The most widely held
definition of vowel height is articulatory, based on the physical degree of tongue
raising within the oral cavity (Chomsky and Halle 1968, 304–305). However,
phonological height has also been argued to be encoded through acoustic
information, such as the spread of spectral energy (Jakobson et al. 1956) or F1
frequency ranges (Ladefoged 1971).2 Most work in feature theory maintains that
high vowels are marked with the feature [high] and that low vowels are marked with
the feature [low], as in (3).3

difficult to interpret, which is to say phonetically ambiguous. While phonological
analyses generally treat these sounds as non-[high] and non-[low] (Chomsky and

   In Sect. 2, I argued that NWGmc a-umlaut was blocked by coda nasals because
nasals in coda position were marked with the feature [low]. In the remainder of this
section, I will argue that PGmc pre-nasal raising also falls out from the lowness
specification of coda nasals, namely, as a process of lowness dissimilation.
Dissimilations are generally said to result from a violation of the Obligatory

3 Discussion

In this section, I provide some additional context for the patterning of nasals in
Germanic languages. Section 3.1 discusses some assumptions that fall out from
innate models of phonological features, which do not necessarily follow in emergent
feature theory. Section 3.2 considers three cross-linguistic patterns which suggest that
nasals might sometimes emerge as [low] sounds. Since emergent feature theory also
allows for the possibility of [high] coda nasals, Sect. 3.3 offers some specific
arguments against the emergence of [high] nasals in early Germanic languages.

3.1 Revising some traditional assumptions

One issue that is particularly relevant to this article concerns the fact that consonantal
features and vocalic features were traditionally kept apart. While Chomsky and Halle
(1968) still did characterize certain consonants with vocalic features like [±high] and
[±low] (akin to [±compact] and [±diffuse] in Jakobson et al. (1956)), subsequent
revisions to theory found those features were less consequential for consonantal
contrasts. As a result, articulator theory and the first autosegmental representations (e.
g., inter alios Sagey 1986) started to codify a formal separation between consonantal
features and vocalic features. That bifurcation, in turn, made it difficult to predict any

   A separate issue is that traditional feature models do not distinguish between a
phonologically active class of features and a phonetically natural class of features.
Following Mielke (2008; 2011), phonologically active classes make crucial
reference to a group of sounds involved in the targeting, triggering, or blocking of a
phonological process. Phonetically natural classes, by contrast, simply define
groups of sounds that have common phonetic properties. While overlap between
the two is predicted when the sounds involved have clear phonetic correlates, there
are many instances when such correlates are ambiguous, causing differences
between phonetics and phonology. Mielke (2005), for example, finds that the
phonetic correlates of continuancy are ambiguous for nasal and lateral sounds. Due
to this ambiguity, languages differ in their phonological specifications. On the one
hand, there are languages in which these sounds pattern as [+cont] sounds, aligned,
as it were, with their phonetically natural class. On the other hand, continuancy is
not as clear in nasals and laterals as it is in stops and fricatives. As a result, nasals
belong to the phonologically active class of [-cont] sounds in about 36.2% of the
languages of the world and laterals pattern as [-cont] sounds in approximately
45.5% of languages.

3.2 Why nasals might emerge as [low] sounds

The traditional SPE definitions of the features [nasal] and [low] are incompatible
with each other. Specifically, Chomsky and Halle (1968, 305, 316) define nasals as
sounds that “are produced with a lowered velum which allows the air to escape
through the nose...” By contrast, the feature [low] characterizes vowels and
consonants that are “produced by lowering the body of the tongue below the level it
occupies in the neutral position” (the tongue posture in a state of rest that
approximates [ɛ]).31 Thus, the articulatory definition of nasals does not overlap with
that of low sounds: they crucially depend on separate speech organs.
   There are, however, several phonetic properties of nasals – which the original SPE
definitions simply do not capture – that may support the phonologization of the
feature [low]. From an articulatory perspective, velic height is not only important to
nasal sounds, but also to vowels. Phonetic research going back to the mid-nineteenth
century has shown that low vowels, like nasal consonants, have lower velic height
than high vowels (Bell-Berti 1993). This is one way that nasal sounds and low
sounds share phonetic properties. Krakow and Huffmann (1993, 6) also demonstrate
that the lateral pharyngeal walls, apart from the position of the velum, are important
for effectively articulating nasals. Accordingly, nasal sounds (which might some-
times be better understood as nasopharyngeal sounds) also have an articulatory
commonality with the pharyngeal place.
   Perceptual studies have often demonstrated an affinity between low vowels and
nasalization. Beddor (1993, 172–173), citing Hattori et al. (1958) and Takeuchi et al.
(1975), notes that nasalization of low vowels can be achieved by weakening the F1
signal. In high vowels, however, additional manipulations are sometimes necessary
to perceive nasalization. This finding seems consistent with the observation –
sometimes regarded as a linguistic universal – that nasalization processes generally
begin with low vowels, move on to mid vowels, and affect high vowels last (see
Hajak 1997, 116). Counter to this generalization, however, Kingston (2007, 416–
418) finds that ‘gutless’ inventories, those that lack nasalized mid vowels, are more
common than ‘headless’ inventories, those that lack nasalized high vowels.
Otherwise, Kingston reaches a similar conclusion: ‘footless’ inventories of nasal
vowels, those that lack nasalized low vowels but have nasalized high and mid
vowels, are rare. This paucity is expected given that low sounds are the most likely to
nasalize.
   The nasal formant (FN) in nasal consonants and nasalized vowels is broader than
the F1 of oral vowels (Ladefoged and Maddieson 1996, 116–118, 298–300;
Ladefoged 2005, 55–56,180–182). Due to this difference in bandwidth, Beddor
(1993, 181) finds that the result of vowel nasalization is for listeners to perceive
vowel height between FN and F1. For this reason, nasalized high vowels may be
perceived with higher than usual F1 values (yielding perceptual mid vowels), while
nasalized low vowels may be perceived with lower than usual F1 values (also

3.3 Coda nasals in Germanic languages did not emerge as [high] sounds:
    implications of the no-crossing constraint

Just as laryngeal realism has found that the cross-linguistically common process of
final devoicing can obtain from distinct phonological processes (Iverson and
Salmons 2011), so too can changes involving nasals have different phonological
motivations, depending on the features that are phonologically active. Since the
phonological ambiguity of nasal segments, particularly with respect to height
features, has not been systematically investigated, there is no reason why a coda
nasal could not be phonologized with the feature [high] in some languages of the
world.42 This section reviews recent work suggesting that the feature [high] is a
phonologically active specification for certain consonants in early Germanic
languages and argues that nasal sounds do not pattern together with [high]
consonants.

4 Conclusion

In this article, I have argued that coda nasals in early Germanic languages were marked
with the feature [low]. That lowness specification is manifest from several processes. In
NWGmc a-mutation, the feature [low] spreads from a non-high vocalic trigger onto a
high back vowel. The no-crossing constraint makes a strong prediction that any
segment that is marked with the feature [low] should block the application of that
change. Since coda nasals do block a-umlaut, it follows that they are [low] segments.
   The lowness specification of coda nasals can also account for pre-nasal raising.
Mid-vowels – doubly-marked with the features [high] and [low] – violate an OCP
constraint when they are situated before coda nasals, which are also [low]. This
dissimilatory analysis and the representations assumed are corroborated by the fact
that the low vowel [a] is permitted before a coda nasal. Dissimilations are widely
reported to be structure preserving developments. Repairing this sequence would
result in a non-existing segment.
   Cross-linguistically, natural class patterns of lowness are also observable given
that nasals and post-velar consonants trigger the lowering of high vowels. One
implication of this observation is that there should be evidence of [low] nasals
patterning as a natural class with post-velar consonants. Indeed, there is such
evidence in ON, where exactly those sounds trigger the lowering of pre-ON *[i] and
*[u]. Those high vowels become doubly-marked mid vowels when the [low] feature
of the coda nasal and the post-velar fricative spread regressively onto the preceding
high vowel.
   Emergent feature theory comes with an ability to work past the limitations of
traditional models because it distinguishes between phonologically active classes and
phonetically natural classes. Nasals have phonetic and acoustic properties that may
endow these sounds with the potential to phonologize with the feature [low]. This

allophones of liquids and /x/ pattern as [high] sounds, while post-velar sounds and
