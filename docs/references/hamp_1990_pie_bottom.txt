A Personal View

Despite outward appearances, Charles-James N. Bailey is, in fact, a very
private individual. For instance, anybody who has spent even five minutes

with him knows of his strong affection for—and loyalty to—the American
South and the South Pacific! On the other hand, there are a great many
things—very important to him—that only his closest friends know. The
character of his life, like the character of his work, shows this paradoxical

mixture of surface simplicity and deep complexity.

While C.-J. was born? and reared in a border state—Kentucky—in the
least ‘typically Southern’ part of the state, the mountains of Eastern

Kentucky, his roots and relatives are also found in Virginia, Tennessee,
Mississippi, Alabama, and South Carolina. In fact, Middlesboro, his home
town, is in one of the ‘oldest’ parts of Kentucky, right at the mouth of the
Cumberland Gap, where the settlement of Kentucky began. From nearby
mountaintops you can see four states—Kentucky, Virginia, Tennessee, and
West Virginia. His father’s family were Virginians; his mother was from

nearby Rogersville, Tennessee, one of the oldest towns in that state. His
cousins include the writers Alexander Percy (Lanterns on the Levee) and
Walker Percy (The Moviegoer, Love in the Ruins); another cousin was

president of Washington and Lee University. Like most Southerners, C.-J.
Ic], with his passion for accuracy in the least detail, would argue that Hawaii,
being above the Equator, is not part of the South Pacific.

started putting down some roots, buying a condominium in Honolulu, and

C.-J. Bailey’s Contribution to Linguistics

When surveying the scholarly production of Charles-James Bailey over
the last two decades one is struck by three things:
(1) The enormous number of his publications,
(2) The wide scope of the topics addressed in

them and, most

importantly,
(3) The consistency of his writing over time, stretching from his

early programmatic statement in the Hawaii Working Papers in
Linguistics of January 31, 1971, entitled “Trying to Talk in the
New Paradigm”, up to the present.

Later work has added to, refined, and illustrated

the revolutionary

program outlined in “Trying to talk in the new paradigm”. Bailey’s sub-

sequent publications have seldom deviated from or opposed his earlier
ideas. To use Baileyan phraseology, his early work is the basis from which
his later work is derived and by which it is implied.
An understanding of his later work thus, ideally, presupposes a
knowledge of his earlier work, in particular, his landmark 1973 book,

Variation and Linguistic Theory, where his early thoughts are most explicitly
illustrated. I consider myself lucky to have followed Bailey’s writings from
the beginning (I first came into contact with them in 1972), to have been
teaching with him during the especially productive period of 1976-79, and
to have been on his mailing list ever since.

Bailey’s bibliography almost speaks for itself. Almost, because what is
not in evidence are his contributions to theology and other fields of
xxi

xxii

knowledge and his frequent letters to newspapers and magazines, many of
them dealing with linguistic matters. Equally prolific are his handouts

prepared for his students at the Technical University of Berlin. (See p. 667
for an example.)

In regard to their scope, Bailey’s linguistic writings range widely, embracing phonetology, dialectology, historical linguistics, syntactic theory,
markedness theory, creolistics, philosophy of language, methodology, and,

more recently, applied linguistics, particularly language planning and teaching English to speakers of other languages. Having thus stepped outside
the straight and narrow confines of grammatical theory, Bailey, like some
of his similarly-minded colleagues, has been called a sociolinguist, a label

which he himself would reject on the grounds that he has consistently
insisted on the separateness of linguistic and social facts and on the
precedence which linguistic or structural facts should take over social
considerations in variation research. More importantly, Bailey would feel
uneasy about being put into a category which is known for attention to
methodology rather than theory, for his own work quite undisputedly
primarily addresses theoretical questions.
A much more appropriate characterization of Bailey’s linguistics is that
it is decompartmentalizing, ie., that it breaks down the barriers the practitioners of the field have erected between subbranches such as historical
linguistics, dialectology, stylistics, or sociolinguistics, Bailey’s reasons for
abolishing such self-imposed compartments derives from two considerations:
(1) His desire for explanations and predictions

(2) His introduction of temporality into linguistic thinking
The introduction of temporality is necessitated by the very nature of
language as a dynamic phenomenon. Variability and change are not accidental, peripheral, or disruptive properties of human language but the
very reason for its usefulness as a tool of haman communication.

Variability means that a language is capable of satisfying the numerous
communicative needs of its speakers, indexical, stylistic, and informative,
while change allows human speakers to adapt to new situations. Linguistic
creativity consists of more than the rule-governed creativity which lies at
the core of most contemporary linguistic theories.

The changing, variable nature of real language structures is what Bailey
wants to account for and, more importantly, explain. Past researchers have

been aware of the considerable variability of human language but have felt
it necessary to design models that force it into simple invariant systems.

C.-J. Bailey’s Contribution to Linguistics

xxiii

This is done, for instance, by removing the main factors that introduce
variability, namely, change over time and individual behavior. These two
factors have

traditionally been either neglected, or pushed to the

periphery or even completely out of the area considered by linguistics,
labels such as PAROLE Of PERFORMANCE being regarded as justification for

the waste basket containing unexplainable or unexplained linguistic
phenomena. Bailey challenges such practice on several grounds, noting:
(1) That variation and systematicity do not have to go together; that

they are independent parameters.

Instead, he regards

the

linguist’s job as reconciling static universals with the changing
variety of real language structure.

(2) That the static systems brought into being by static theory have
no counterpart in the real world and are thus not empirically
testable claims about human language.
(3) That criteria for distinguishing between competence and perfor-

mance, synchronic and diachronic, and other dichotomies are

non-empirical. At best, such dichotomies are initial heuristic
devices. It certainly makes no sense to regard them as essential
characteristics of human language to be explained by linguistic
theory.

Of the two forces shaping variable systems, time and individual changes,
Bailey is concerned mainly with the former. Variable patterns are regarded
as having been created largely by temporal differences. As a number of
papers in this volume address this question, | will limit myself to a basic
discussion of this principle. I shall come back to individual deviations
below.

An important aspect of implicational scales is that they are constant over
a number of dimensions: spatial, social, and stylistic. Thus, in Table 4, the

presence of feature D will always imply that of features C, B, and A, but
not vice versa. Again, B implies A but not vice versa. What differs is the
interpretation of this arrangement by different groups of users or contexts
of use. The forms are thus multifunctional: feature D could be associated,
for instance, with spatial distribution shown in Table 5.
Table 5 represents the wave-like travel of an innovation over space. The
center of innovation is location number 1. Innovation B is added after
innovation A thus implies innovation B. Innovations travel from 1 to 5
with the result that the largest number of innovations is found right at the
center and that all innovations reach outlying locations in a strict order.

What goes for space also goes for other dimensions, for instance, speech
styles. Location 1 could be a most advanced relaxed style with 2, 3, and 4
being increasingly more formal styles. If we combine these stylistic and
spatial dimensions, we might find that the most advanced style at one
location corresponds to the middle-of-the-road style at the center of the
innovative location.

As they were conceived originally, implicational scales display rather
than explain data and, thus, taken by themselves, do not meet Bailey's

requirement that linguistics should explain existing variation and predict

future patterns. To regard the development of devices for the display of
data as the essence of Baileyan linguistics would miss the main important

theoretical concerns that appear throughout his writings on variation. For
the transition from display to explanation, additional apparatus is needed.
This is provided by Bailey in the form of markedness-theoretical conventions, and consideration of linguistic naturalness.

coincide and overlap. Nevertheless, such a separation allows one to make
important distinctions such as that between targeted language acquisition
(ordinary child acquisition) and untargeted language acquisition (such as
pidgin/creole development).

Markedness is manifested in linguistic development in the following
ways:

(1) In the case of complexity-acquiring systems (child language acquisition, development of pidgins) more marked categories
develop after less marked ones such that the presence of more

marked categories implies that of corresponding less marked
ones.

(2)In the history of fully-developed adult languages the opposite
tendency is encountered. All things being equal, linguistic change

goes in the direction from more to less marked,
In both instances the prediction of future states is possible, an important
consideration for people who work in the applied areas of linguistics such
as second language acquisition and language planning.

Bailey’s contribution to markedness theory is considerable and can only
be briefly touched upon here. An important insight is that markedness can
relate both to linguistic entities or structures and to the environment in

which they appear. Such a view allows linguists to explain seeming
anomalies by appealing to the concept of markedness reversal.

The most convenient illustration of the principle of markedness reversal
comes from an area unrelated to linguistics such as the social conventions
of dress. The unmarked state of affairs in Western societies is to be
dressed in public, the marked one to be undressed. However, markedness

reversal makes it possible for a dressed person to be marked or stand out
at a nudist beach and an undressed person to be unmarked in the same
environment. A parallel case in language would be the unmarked imperative without pronoun, don’t cry, and the marked presence of the pronoun
in tbe imperative environment, don’t you cry, which conveys the special
meaning of sympathy. Note that here we are dealing with constructional

iconicity, the marked construction standing for marked meaning. (See
Bailey 1984.)
A summary of some of the refinements that Bailey has made to marked-

ness theory (particularly with regard to phonetology) is found in Bailey
1985a.
Of great interest are his ideas that an unmarking in one part of grammar
may lead to increased marking in another part and his observations on

differential susceptibility to marking in the input and environment of
phonological rules.
It should be noted that such refinements are not necessitated by requirements of internal consistency but rather by Bailey’s concern for an ex-

planation of data concerning linguistic change. That such explanations can
be provided is illustrated by Bailey in numerous articles and, most explicit-

ly, in his course on phonetology at the Technical University of Berlin.
Language being a highly complex phenomenon, it should not surprise us
that complex explanatory mechanisms are needed to deal with it.
One of Bailey’s constant worries has been the proclivity of practitioners
in fields such as historical and general linguistics to be perfectly happy with
inadequate outdated and unsuitable models. His own prediction regarding
any shift from the static to the dynamic paradigm was that, as time went
by, “the hostilities which the new paradigm will evoke will be somewhat
more muted than before, while the inertia against it will be proportionally
greater” (1971:122). Such inertia is deplored in many of Bailey's articles,
for instance, when he castigates historical linguists for neglecting their

proper job (1985b:58):
Let me state my thesis at the very outset. The job of the historical-comparative linguist is to deal with change and to compare its
results. 1 can almost hear each reader saying to himself or herself:
‘Who doubts that? Why did he go out of his way to tell us that?’
The answer to this question lies in the fact that numerous writings
on historical and comparative linguistics make use of models explicitly designed for the idiolect and explicitly designed to exclude

change and comparison—phonemes, morphemes, states, isoglossic
dialects, etc.—models that can with much less grace or ease be
turned to historical-comparative ends than a diver’s suit could be
turned into an evening gown.

Whereas Bailey has refined and tested his model over the years and
while many of his followers, such as some of those represented in this
volume, have taken his ideas aboard, inertia still persists in compartmen-

talized mainstream linguistics. One might wish to argue that this situation
is to the detriment of the field, since a time-incorporating model lends
itself much more readily to applications than a static one. That his linguistics is indeed a highly practical one with close affinity to applied linguistics
was realized by Bailey very early on. However, in recent years applied

questions, in particular, that of teaching English to speakers of other
languages, have become more prominent in his writings.
I can highlight only two areas where his new emphasis is particularly
noticeable, namely language planning and second Janguage teaching.
Language planners have for a Jong time been hampered by a view that
regarded closed systems where everything was determined by everything

else as the ideal end point of the planning process. Thus, they were
concerned with accidental gaps, asymmetries, irregularities, and a notion of
simplicity directly related to economy and communicative efficiency. It has
turned out, however, that such a view of language is misguided for two
reasons: it ignores the importance of language as a means of adapting to
new circumstances, ie., the importance of having at one’s disposal rulechanging creativity, and it ignores the difference between mechanical
simplicity and regularity on the one hand, and naturalness and marked-

ness, which characterizes human communication systems, on the other.
A particular contribution of Bailey’s to language planning is in the area
of developing writing systems where his phonetology would seem to be a

much more suitable basis than shallow phonemic systems or unjustifiably

deep transformational phonologies. In particular, his phonetology is ideal
for languages that exhibit widespread lectal variation.
In regard to second language teaching, Bailey’s insights are of importance in a number of areas. First of all, implicational scaling provides a

tool for describing interlanguages such as occur with second language
learners. Secondly, criteria of markedness and naturalness rather than rule

simplicity or contrastive considerations serve to explain learners’ errors.
Thirdly, by pointing out to foreign language learners the importance of
using unmarked and leveled forms, they can be helped to get closer to
near-native performance in their target language.

Over the years students who have gone through the phonetology classes
in Berlin have acquired a superior understanding of the functioning of the

language they are going to teach to German school children. As their
numbers grow, one might hope that the incidence of “footling English”
(see the Glossary) in Berlin schools would decrease.
It would seem appropriate to return to a number of issues which were

briefly mentioned earlier, namely:
(1) language contact
(2) individual deviation
(3) the independence of linguistic structure
These issues raise questions as to the limitations of the Baileyan approach to language, or better, questions the limits of explanatory power of

linguistic theories as well as those about the origin of markedness.

In his programmatic paper of 1971 (pp. 124-25) Bailey stated that creole
situations are much more common than language development in isolation.
In his terminology creolization means the mixing of linguistic systems.
Mixing is one of the reasons why, on the one hand, changes over time of
full languages do not always proceed from more marked to less marked
and, on the other hand, why complexity-changing development does not
always go from less to more marked. Bailey’s original assumption that

mixing increases markedness has been refined in his own later writings and
in the writings of some of his followers including my own (Mihlhauster
1985). By emphasizing the centrality of mixing, Bailey altered a situation
where language contact and mixing was increasingly being pushed to the
periphery of linguistics. His insistence at the Technical University of Berlin
that creolistics should occupy a central position in the linguistics syllabus
bears witness to this thinking.

Mixing of separate systems is not the only area of language contact
Bailey has addressed. His work on dialectology and historical linguistics

makes frequent reference to intralinguistic mixing such as dialect contact
or style shifts.
His position on the independence of grammatical structures from pragmatic and social parameters, at least in my interpretation, is one where the
unmarked case appears to be independence and the marked one pragmatic influence on structure. In his introductory lectures on linguistics at

the Technical University of Berlin, Bailey used to distinguish a grammatical core consisting of phonetology and semantax from a pragmatic outer
area. Just as dependence between phonetology and semantax is relatively

rare, so is dependence between grammar and pragmatics. However, we
have already noted that marked pragmatic contexts or marked meanings

tend to promote marked syntactic structures and to this needs to be added
that speakers of many languages also engage in activities such as hypercorrection, standardization, and the creation of esoteric styles for taboo or

other reasons. In as much as such changes take place in time, they can be
arranged on implicational scales. However, it should alsa be borne in mind
that deliberate changes can ignore or replace existing implicational scales

and that they typically do not take account of markedness hierarchies.
Thus, prestige borrowing from the superstrate in pidgin development can

upset established developmental tendencies as can an unsuitable teaching
syllabus in the case of second language acquisition.

In 1971 Bailey suggested that performance is in the individual, which, to
me, implies that the processes least likely to lend themselves to prediction
are individual activities, particularly deliberate individual changes. (For an
example of his own contributions to filling in lexical gaps in English and

German, see the Glossary.) The extent to which such processes shape
language, particularly small languages with a closed network of speakers,
remains to be established. Bailey’s own concern has been with processes

where naturalness plays a major role and he has suggested on a number
of occasions that the other type of process should be explored by people
like myself. I still remember his remarks on the occasion of a lecture at

Oxford where he referred to me as the “King of the Abnatural.” Recent
developments in the areas of chaos and catastrophe theory in other
branches of knowledge suggest that there is a vast hidden empire for such
a king to rule over.

Closely related to the question of individual preferences is that of the
pragmatic influence on grammar. Bailey’s implicational scales are neutral
with regard to whether such implications are local (e.g., different environments of a phonetological process) or involve interlevel dependencies (e.g.,
semantactic preconditions to phonetological rules). I am not sure to what
extent they could incorporate pragmatic prerequisites to grammatical

xxxii

analysis and whether his relative lack of concern for this question can be
justified in the long run. In as much as variation is functional and sensitive
to pragmatic considerations, an answer to this problem needs to be found.

Such an answer could change the status of variation theory from one
which explains grammatical changes to one that can explain the overall
process of human communication.
This brief survey of some of C.-J. Bailey’s contribntions to linguistics has

been structured in such a way as to help more linguists talk in the new
paradigm. The basis for my wanting to bring this about is both a personal
preference for a Baileyan approach to language as well as a number of

more substantive reasons. Many linguists, at one point or other in their
career, will have shared these concerns. They include:
(1) Linguists have for a long time regarded the cultural construct of
invariable grammar as the essential natural core of language. We

have reasons to reject such a view as mistaken and unlikely to
lead to significant insights.
(2) Scientific prediction, it is widely acknowledged, is contingent on
investigators coming to grips with change, fluctuations, instability,
and ‘messy’ data. It is unlikely that linguistics will be able to
escape from this obligation.
(3) It is not likely that linguistics can get away indefinitely with the
claim that it is totally value-free and can be carried out as a

self-contained intellectual exercise. Rather there is an urgent
need

for the practitioners in this field to address social and

moral issues and pay attention to the needs of the communities

they work in. One aspect that is particularly stated in Bailey's
work on new modifications is that linguists shonld be making
qualitative judgments.
(4) Related to this is the growing demand for applicable linguistics
from applied areas such as speech therapy, language planning,
language teaching, and speech recognition research. All these
disciplines require

an understanding of variable data

and a

Baileyan approach may help bring solutions to their problems.

(5) The concepts put forward by Bailey would seem to provide a
viable alternative to more mechanistic approaches to language,
ie., approaches that have operated with machine or conduit
metaphors in explaining human communication.
