     On the Interpretation of Older Runic swestar on the
                         Opedal Stone
Summary

The form swestar is one of the most problematic in the Older Runic language. Though it is
obviously an instance of the word for “sister”, the ending -ar has defied a satisfactory analysis
ever since the inscription was unearthed in 1890. A systematic consideration of the various
sounds the rune transliterated as a could represent, and their further development to Old Norse,
reveals that none of the previous interpretations of the form can be correct. Instead, a new
solution is suggested, reading the ending as -ar (not -ār), and identifying the form as a vocative
singular. An appendix reviews the survival of the Indo-European vocative case in Germanic and
examines the other alleged vocative forms of Older Runic.

3. Earlier Proposals and Clarification of the Problem

3.0. Opedal swestar has prompted a number of interpretations.

3.1.1. In the view of Wilhelm Streitberg (1892: 108; also 1894: 363; 1896: §§160,
179), the desinence -ar causes no difficulties concerning the development of PGmc.
long *ē in North Germanic, and indeed has no bearing on the matter. For Streitberg
interprets the -ar as -ar, a shortening of the original *-ōr ending of PIE *suésōr,
and not a development of PGmc. *-ēr at all. This analysis was accepted by Hirt
1894: 275; Bethge 1900: §370; Brugmann 1906: §245; also Prokosch 1939: 255;

3.1.2. Unfortunately, the proposal must be rejected. With the sole exception of
absolute final *-ō#, which is shortened to -u at the North West Germanic stage, PIE
(PGmc.) long vowels retain their length in Older Runic. Even if, for the sake of
argument, we were to accept a shortening of *-ōr to -ar, the latter would not yield
Viking Age Runic -iR (sustiR = [systiR] Tryggevælde, Jacobsen and Moltke 1942:
No. 230), literary Old Norse /-ir/ (cf. §3.4.2.2). We would have to explain these
forms as the result of analogy from the other r-stems. However, the evidence of the
other Germanic languages clearly indicates that the word for “sister” had already
gone over to the -ter-stem pattern by the time of Proto-Germanic. Streitberg’s
proposal was rejected on this ground by Bugge as early as 1898 (in Bugge-Olsen
1891–1924: I 304). Most scholars have recognized this fact and framed their
hypotheses accordingly.

3.2.0. The problem as originally stated remains. This seems an appropriate point,
therefore, at which to specify its nature in more detail and to bring in the additional
data mentioned above (§2).

PGmc. short *a (in all positions); hence its transliteration as a. Already in the
earliest inscriptions, this rune is also used to represent the sound developed from

tradition8. This graphemic evidence is supported by that of Sami (Lapp) loanwords,
which indicates the early acquisition in North Germanic of an approximate value
[a:] by PGmc. *ē in accented syllables, cf. Lid 1952: 238. Moreover, the rune e (e),
which is used for the reflex of PGmc. short *e (accented syllables) and of PGmc.

(though erroneously, cf. above §3.2.1) attributed to Bugge, but actually came from
Alois Walde (1900: 62–64).

      For a long time this was the standard view, cf., for example, Michels 1902:
119; Janko 1903–04: 265, 266; 1905: 64; Kock 1906–29: IV 15 (cf. II 154 fn1, III
9); Noreen 1913: §§34, 198.1; Boer 1920: §142.A; Noreen 1923: §138; Brøndum-
Nielsen 1928–73: §§119.1, 471.1, 480.1; Heusler 1932: §§102.5. 106, 237; Harding

Krause, cf. Krause 1948: §§53.9; 102, Krause-Jankuhn 1966: I 176; Krause 1968a:
120; 1971: §58.12.

3.3.2. The sound-value [æ(:)] posited by this and the preceding theory is patently a
compromise: a phonetic interpolation into the sequence (PIE) PGmc. *-ē > ... YRu.
i > ON ‹e, i›, motivated solely as a concession to the orthographic fact that —
although the rune e with the value [e(:)] was available — the symbol employed, ‹a›,
was also used to represent a low, back/central vowel, the reflex of PGmc. short *a
(cf. §3.2.0, above). This ‘concession’ amounts to no more than the fact that the
vowel [æ(:)] is traditionally regarded as a (high) type of front ‘a-vowel’, rather than
a (low) type of ‘e-vowel’.

        The assumption of such multiple values for a single graph as those proposed
for the rune ‹a› cannot easily be reconciled with modern graphological and
phonological theory. It is a common feature of European alphabets (including the
Older Futhark) that a single graph is used for two phonemes that differ as regards
length, but otherwise share relevant distinctive features. This would allow use of ‹a›
to denote both short [a] and long [a:]; any other values assigned to the graph would
be allophones of either the phoneme short /a/ or the phoneme long /a:/ (cf. also
Steblin-Kamenskij 1962: 3). This makes nonsense of the development in the terms
proposed: (PIE) PGmc. long *ē in final syllables joins the Older Runic phoneme
/a:/ (as a special allophone [æ:]) and then unconditionally splits off, duly appearing
as ON /i/ (while ORu. /a:/ presumably continues on its way to ON /a/?). For
criticism of the assumption of the value [æ:] for the graph ‹a›, cf. also Antonsen
1970: 313–15 and Barnes 1973: 360–61. (Mutatis mutandis for a supposed [æ]
allophone of /a/.)

the [æ(:)] theories, therefore, that it regards the rune ‹a› as having the same ‘a-

        This taking the a-rune at its face-value was the ‘original’ interpretation, cf.
Kock 1892: 19–20 (with literature), but was subsequently given up — no doubt on
grounds of phonetic implausibility (see below) — in favour of the [æ(:)] theories
(though it seems to have been still adhered to by Jóhannesson 1923: §49). When the

long [a:] vowel in final syllables as it did in root syllables. That is to say, PGmc.
*/e:/ in final syllables shows up in the unstressed vowel phoneme ORu. /a:/, and the
further change to YRu. ‹i›, literary Norse /i/ is the regular development of this
phoneme.

     This is essentially a restatement of the preceding theory (§3.3.) in terms of
phoneme theory.

3.4.1.2. The value of such a restatement is that it clearly reveals the inconsistency
and inadequacy of the theory it replaced. Should the second and third person
singular endings of the dental preterite have contained a PGmc. long *ē, as is
usually thought, then it is inexplicable why this vowel should not be reflected by
‹a›-spellings in Older Runic, instead of the ‹e›-spellings that actually occur (cf.
Krause 1971: §104 for a list of attested forms). Nonetheless, proponents of both the
[æ(:)]-reading and the [a:]-reading seem either not to have noticed this fact or not to

3.4.2.1. It is possible to cite a counter-case to the proposed development of ORu.
/a:/ to ON /i/ in final syllables. Unfortunately, this counter-case is provided by the
development of an ending whose Older Runic shape has to be established by
theoretical arguments, as an absolutely certain attestation of it is (so far) lacking. As
might be expected in a situation of this kind, the reconstruction of the Proto-Norse
form of this ending has been the subject of some controversy.

      The desinence in question is the genitive singular of the i-stems. This
category constitutes the only possible exponent of long /a:/ in final syllables in the
Older Runic language that would not be a reflex of PGmc. *ē by the hypothesis
under discussion.

     That the parent-form is PGmc. *-aiz (cf. Go. -ais, e.g. qēnais Mk 6/17) is
beyond dispute13; what is not agreed, however, is whether ON -ar, as in vinar,
nauðar is the regular reflex of this pre-form or not.

      The problem arises because the correspondences between the three categories
for which Proto-Norse *-aiR can be posited are not one-to-one. We are presented
with two apparent reflexes: -er/-ir and -ar:

a) adjectival nom. pl. masc. ending: blinder/-ir (cf. þeir)
b) verbal (pres.) optv. 2sg. ending: hjalper/-ir
c) nominal i-stem gen. sg. ending: vinar, nauðar

Purely numerically, the development to -er/-ir would seem to be the ‘regular’ one.

      It appears, therefore, that we must fall back on theoretical arguments.

      The adjectival ending -er/-ir is certainly secondary, and the second singular
(present) optative can be convincingly explained as a remodelling, but, though the
analogical explanation of the i-stem genitive singular seems to work in purely
formal, mechanical terms, it is very difficult to motivate.

       First, we would have to assume that the Old Norse state of affairs, with both
masculine and feminine i-stems having the same ending -ar, is not in fact a
straightforward reflection of the Proto-Germanic state of affairs, where both had the
                                                                    Opedal swestar April 12, 2017/11


ending *-aiz. On the contrary, this situation arose in the following way: for some
reason, the regular reflex, uniform †*-ēR (or †*-iR/-ir) underwent an analogical
replacement, independently in both genders, mysteriously resulting in a uniform
ending *-āR (or -aR/ -ar). An analogical change is presumably the result of some
functional motivation (for example, simplification, or the maintenance of an
important opposition); one that creates an output that is functionally identical to its
input is unusual. In the present instance, it would seem more sensible to assume that
the identical correspondents to identical parent forms are the result of regular
phonological developments (and a morphological archaism, cf. Grønvik 1981: 63–
65, 205–06).

       Further, as has been powerfully argued by Harald Bjorvand (1972: esp. 213–
14), the suggested analogies are completely at odds with the ‘analogical drift’ of the
language, which was towards the formal differentiation of masculine and feminine.
This can clearly be seen in the (relatively late) tendency for masculine i-stems to
adopt the endings of the a-stems, just as happened independently in Gothic and
West Germanic. (A series of developments whereby the ‘regular’ ending †*-ēR, or
†*-iR/-ir, is replaced first by that of the masculine u-stems and then by that of the a-
stems seems a bit much to have to swallow.) On the other hand, maintenance of the
inherited genitive singular ending by the feminines was supported by its
coincidence with that of the ō-stems (which had far-reaching repercussions, cf.
Heusler 1932: §§217a, 218).

      In my view, these theoretical considerations are overwhelming, and the i-
stem genitive singular can accordingly be reconstructed as ORu. */-a:R/.14 This in
turn must constitute a valid parallel to the supposed ORu. †/-a:r/, as the later
development of */-a:R/ to ON /-ar/ clearly shows that the regular outcome of ORu.
long /a:/ plus non-nasal consonant is ON short /a/ plus reflex of the consonant. A
development ORu. †/a:r/ > ON /-ir/ is thus seen to be impossible.

did the vowel in *-āR (cf. §3.3.2). — Indeed, it appears that, in unaccented syllables, the phoneme
/a:/ occurred only in the desinence *-āR.

3.4.2.2. But, aside from this straight parallel between two individual sequences
(and some may regard the Older Runic i-stem gen. sg. */-a:R/ argued for above as
too uncertain to be used for this purpose), a development ORu. /a:/ > /I/ is out of
place among the changes which affected the system of vowels in final syllables
from the end of the Older Runic period until the earliest literary period.

       If, for the time being, we leave out of account the question of the fate
undergone by a putative long /a:/ phoneme, we can make the following
observations on the basis of the remaining uncontroversial developments. Vowels
in final syllables are subject to loss, if short (unless nasalized or followed by m, n, l,
or r), shortening, if long. Short vowels were lost at the end of the Older Runic
period, long vowels shortened at the end of the Transitional Period (cf. further
footnote 4). In the course of these quantitative changes, there is a general movement
towards the three extremes of the vowel-triangle. As Haugen puts it for the high
vowels (1949: 386); ‘all the long and protected short vowels fell together into i if
they were palatal, u if they were velar’. It is to be noted that in the process of
shortening, TP /o:/ was unrounded, yielding ON /a/, whereas nasalized TP /õ:/ (<
ORu. -ōn) was raised, yielding ON /u/. (On the distinction between /o:/ and /õ:/ and
the development of /õ:/ to ON /u/, cf. Grønvik 1976:144 [but delete *augōn and
augo, -u gen. dat. sg. as examples], 146; also fn 16 [on the ō-verbs] and fn 26 [on
derivative -ōni-nouns of the type skipun.)

4. The Rune a Represents a Short Vowel in the Categories in Question

4.0. If it be accepted that a development of PGmc. */e:/ > ORu. /a:/ > ON /i/ is not
possible, how then are we to explain the data cited in §3.2.0, and in a manner that is
consistent with both the graphological and phonological facts?

       I would propose that all the alleged reflexes of PGmc. */e:/ as ORu. /a:/ rest
on false analyses, and that in each case the rune a represents short a.

determine what the pre-forms of adverbs were. (In addition, it is by no means

Hollifield gives as an example of PGmc. *-ē > ORu. -ā (reading it as anā, 1980:

oblique cases, the suffix-form -an- was generalized throughout the singular at a
very early date (cf. ORu. gen. sg. wiwilan Veblungsnes [Marstrander 1952a: 19;

doubtless have acted as a model for this levelling, as will the parallelism with the
opposition between a- and ō-stems referred to under (2) below.

North Germanic. This merger provided the motivation for a renewal of one of the
two endings so as to re-establish the gender distinction in this category. In the
event, it was the masculine that innovated. There was in all the oblique case-forms
(except dative plural) a distinction between masculine and feminine n-stems marked
by the opposition -an- versus -ōn-, similar to that between the masculine a-stems
and the feminine ō-stems; the nominative singular was brought into line by a further
extension of the vowel a, producing the ending -a, corresponding to the -ō of the
feminine n-stems. The creation of the masculine nominative singular desinence -a is
thus to be seen against the background of the functional trend to create and maintain
gender distinction in the n-stems. The rebuilding of the masculines antedates the
earliest runic inscriptions (cf. the data in §3.2.0, and under (1) above).

7. Appendix: The Vocative in Germanic Nouns and Adjectives

7.1.1. One of the valuable archaisms of the Gothic language is its preservation of
formal traces of the Indo-European vocative case in the singular of substantives.

       The vocative does not have its own distinctive ending in Gothic, but is
identifiable on distributional grounds. In inflectional classes with a sigmatic
nominative, the vocative form is that of the nominative, minus the -s (and thus
identical in form with the accusative), otherwise it has the same form as the
nominative. The only exception is provided by the u-stems (on which, see Watkins
1966: 113–14, and Szemerényi 1980: 161, with note (1)).

       This rule produces forms different from the nominative in the a-stems, ja-
stems, i-stems, u-stems, and nd-stems (no vocatives are attested to ‘root nouns’,
which should belong here, as they have sigmatic nominatives). The synchronic
relevance of the rule is demonstrated by the nd-stems: the voc. talzjand, to nom.
talzjands, is analogical; the phonologically regular form would be †talzjan from
                                                           Opedal swestar April 12, 2017/23


ePGmc. *talzjand. (Incidentally, we may note that the nominative talzjands is itself
analogical, as pre-Gmc. *-onts should yield †-ans.) For attestations of Gothic
vocative forms, see the relevant sections of Streitberg 1920 and Jellinek 1926.

7.1.2. In addition to this morphological marking in the noun, it is generally held
that the vocative is also marked syntactically in Gothic, in so far as vocative
adjectives take the weak inflection, see Behaghel 1923: 176, who lists a single
exception: Lk 1/28 faginō, anstai audahafta (fem. sg.) “rejoice, O one lucky in
grace”, Gk. χαῖρε, κεχαριτωμένη (here and elsewhere following Streitberg’s
text, 1919 etc.). This state of affairs is regarded as another archaism of Gothic: ‘Das
im Vokativ stehende Adjektiv zeigte zweifellos ursprünglich die schwache Flexion,
ein Zustand, der im Gotischen noch fast rein bewahrt ist.’ (Behaghel 1923: 175). As
for the origin of this use of the weak inflection, in Behaghel’s opinion, ‘Die
einfachste Erklärung für die schwache Flexion des Vokativs ist die, daß diese von
den [n-stämmigen — PVS] Koseformen der Personennamen übernommen ist’
(178).

unflektierte Form’ (176). This is also true of Old Norse, cf. Heusler 1932: §386,
Wessén 1970: §31. The modern Scandinavian standard languages have come to
generalize the use of the weak form in vocative function, though the strong form is
also found in Modern Icelandic (Stefán Einarsson 1945: 118), Faroese (Lockwood
1955: 112), and in Norwegian dialects. German, on the other hand, has eliminated
the weak form at the expense of the strong, cf. Behaghel 1923: 176-178. In fact,
there is more than one example of strong adjective inflection in Gothic, cf. also

       According to Behaghel, ‘Wenn im weiteren Verlauf der Entwicklung auch
die starke und die unflektierte Form im Vokativ auftritt, so ist das eine Einwirkung
des Nominativs, dem der Vokativ ja sehr nahe steht’ (1923: 178). I take this as
meaning no more than that the nominative form came to usurp the function of the
vocative also in the adjectives, with the result that the alleged rule that the weak
form was used in the vocative was lost — more of a description than an
explanation. In the light of the evidence given above, it would appear that
Behaghel’s theory does not fit the facts very well anyway. (Compare the much
better textual support in all the dialects for the rule that adjectives take the weak
form in the comparative.)

       Curme (1911:368-370) and Sturtevant (1928), on the other hand, present an
explanation of the Gothic material which has the virtue of being tailored to the data.
They see a semantic distinction between the strong and weak forms of the adjective
in a vocative function: ‘The weak form is more commonly used in direct address, as
the usual object here is merely to make clear and definite the individual to whom
reference is made. The strong form of the adjective is, however, sometimes used
here to call attention to the [sic, ‘some’ intended? — PVS] quality in the individual
rather than merely to point out an individual, and hence the strong form adds the
idea of a predication to the statement.’ (Curme 1911: 369). (This latter nuance
would fit swestar mīnu liubu on the Opedal Stone tolerably well, cf. §4.4)

       Whatever interpretation we put on the synchronic Gothic situation, I do not
think we have enough evidence to maintain that adjectives ‘originally’ took the
weak form when in a vocative function in Germanic. (Indeed, in so far as the weak
adjective inflection is a Germanic innovation, the ‘original’ situation must have
been that there was only one form, the ‘strong’ one.) The evidence of, on the one
hand, the Scandinavian languages, which we can see extending the use of the weak
form in the course of their recorded history, and, on the other, German, which has
done the reverse, shows that the Gothic situation (whatever it may be) need not
reflect ‘Proto-Germanic’ conditions anyway. (Compare further the remarks of
Gonda 1956: 95-96, also 101-102, on the the usage with vocatives, and Szemerényi
1960a: 16-18, on the sphere of the weak form in Germanic.)

      From the point of view of the Opedal inscription, this evidence suggests that,
should we wish to take the words swestar mīnu liubu as one syntagm (cf. §5), it

would not be a serious objection that liubu was in the strong form. And, even if it
were to be upheld that use of the weak form of the adjective in a vocative function
was the ‘original’ Germanic rule, it could be countered that we should not be
surprised if Older Runic shows some signs of development towards the situation
found in Old Norse. Besides, the usage of weak and strong forms in Older Runic is
particularly erratic (cf. Krause 1971: §112). (It is to be remembered also, that we do
not HAVE to take swestar minu liubu as one syntagm, cf. §1.1 and note 19.)

7.2. It would appear to be the standard view that Gothic is the only Germanic
language to preserve formal traces of the Indo- European vocative in substantives.
Compare Streitberg 1896: §172, Bethge 1900: §316 (cf. §329), Boer 1924: §174,
Hirt 1932: §26, Prokosch 1939: 236. Krahe-Meid 1969: 7 (etc.) are unclear on this
point (they do not regularly discuss Older Runic in their grammar).

REMARK. The valiant attempt by Bülbring (1896) to identify vocative forms in Old English was
promptly refuted by Kluge (1896). Bülbring’s view was accepted by Streitberg 1896: §182
(despite §172, referred to above!), but this was probably before Kluge’s refutation appeared
(especially as the foreword to Streitberg’s grammar is dated ‘10. Okt. 1895’). Presumably,
Streitberg was the source of Wackernagel’s statement (1926: 306) that ‘unter den andern

time of the loss of word-final *-z# (i- and u-stems), and *-a# (a-stems). Strong feminines could
have kept a distinction longer, but the above sound changes will have presumably completed the
process of syncretism, were it not already complete earlier.

7.3.0. Among the authors of recent handbooks, only Krause dissents from the view
that the vocative is confined to Gothic. In his Handbuch des Gotischen, we read:
‘Davon [viz., of the Proto-Germanic cases — PVS] ... ist der Vokativ nur im
Gotischen (und im Urnordischen) bewahrt’ (1968b: §113). In his Die Sprache der
urnordischen Runeninschriften, he cites three supposed vocative forms: Alawīd (a-
stem) and Alawin (i-stem) on the Skodborg Bracteate, and, probably, Alugōd (a-
                                                              Opedal swestar April 12, 2017/26


stem) on the Værløse Fibula (1971: §27).20 (We may note that Hollifield 1980: 33
accepts these vocative forms for Older Runic.)

       Before examining these supposed exponents of the category, I would like to
point out that, as far as I can see, there is no valid reason, a priori, why the Older
Runic language should not have preserved vocative case-forms. It is the earliest
attestation of any Germanic language, the oldest texts pre-dating Wulfila, and in
several respects presents a more archaic form of language than does Bible Gothic.
The retention of short stem vowels after both heavy and light syllables in particular,
means that Older Runic will have had a clearer formal basis than Gothic for
preserving distinct vocative forms. Thus, if Gothic has vocative forms, Older Runic
may well have them too, though this is not, of course, to say that it follows that it
must have had them. (Further, even granted that Older Runic did have a formal
vocative, it does not necessarily follow that Krause, and I, have succeeded in
correctly identifying instances of it.) The choice seems plain, however: in these
instances, we either have corrupt inscriptions or we have vocative forms.

       I cannot forbear remarking, that it seems to me that some scholars are so
averse to the idea of having vocative forms in Older Runic (though they never give
substance to their objections), that they would far rather pronounce the relevant
inscriptions corrupt than accept a vocative reading.

7.3.1. The Værløse Fibula (Krause-Jankuhn 1966: I No. 11, cf. also Marstrander
1952b: 79–83) comes from the grave of a woman and is dated to approximately 200
A.D. The inscription on it consists solely of the runes alugod, and interpretation is
made difficult by the absence of any linguistic or material context. Thus, for
example, we do not know whether the clasp had belonged to the deceased in life
and had already borne the inscription then, or was only inscribed for the burial;
alternatively, it might only have been ‘donated’ to the deceased at the burial and
may, or may not, have borne the inscription prior to that.

7.3.2.1. As it was found on a bracteate, the Skodborg inscription (Krause-Jankuhn
1966: I No. 105) is difficult to date with certainty. Linguistically, we can classify it
as an Older Runic inscription, however, on the basis of the retained stem-vowel in
the word auja nt. nom. sg. (< PGmc. *-an). In this connection, it is significant to
note the earlier dating of the bracteates, to c. 400–550/575, that has been adopted by
Danish archeologists, see Nielsen 1969, especially 28, 36. Thus, we can date the
Skodborg inscription to some time before c. 500 (cf. footnote 4). Fully in keeping
with this dating is the absence of final devoicing of spirants in alawīd (cf. Moulton
1954: 10; Ralph 1980: 4, arbitrarily denies that the form has any relevance).

follows:

     auja alawin, auja alawin, auja alawin jaÇalawīd

7.3.2.2. In the view of Marstrander 1929a: 119–21, the Skodborg Bracteate is
Gothic, because: 1) the co-ordinating particle ja(h) is found only in Gothic and

Brakteaten eller guden — hvad der kommer ut på det samme — tenkes talende.’ On

expressed; such an omission is irregular (in the Sjælland Bracteate 2 inscription,
gibu auja is the second sentence, in the first the ‘speaker’ is named). This furnishes
an argument against Marstrander’s interpretation that is not connected with the
question of dialect.

      I reject Marstrander’s Gothic classification of Skodborg.

       Even if, for the sake of argument, we were to accept Marstrander’s view that
there were Goths in the North at the relevant time, it by no means follows that
Skodborg is Gothic. As the bracteates are basically a phenomenon of Southern
Scandinavia and present-day Denmark, and as Marstrander himself regards the bulk
of them as linguistically North Germanic, I would suggest that, if a North Germanic
classification is possible, it should be adopted. And, of course, if some of the data
which a theory was set up to account for can be explained without it, then the
theory itself is undermined. (I might also add, that I find it somewhat surprising that
these putative ‘Goths’ should be using a form of language that agrees so closely
with Wulfila’s Bible Gothic.)

7.3.3. Though I would not set too much store by Værløse alugod, there seems to be
a reasonable probability that the names on the Skodborg Bracteate are instances of
the vocative in Older Runic. It must be conceded, however, that the possibility
cannot be excluded in the case of either of these inscriptions that the relevant words
have been left incomplete due to the incompetence of the metal-smith.

       In the case of swestar on the Opedal Stone, on the other hand, there is no
question of the omission of runes. The form cannot be explained away without the
(more or less gratuitous) assumption of the crassest incompetence on the part of the
inscriber — although he wrote the sequence birgŋguboro, which moreover
contains three unparalleled rune-forms, cf. footnote 3. To my mind, it would be
perverse to make this assumption when the alternative reading as a vocative is
available. However, if I have achieved nothing else in this article, I hope to have
established that these are the only two reasonable interpretations of the form.25
