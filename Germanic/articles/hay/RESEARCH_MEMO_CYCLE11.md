# Research memorandum: the inherited direct singular and its written reflexes

## Identity and question

Cycle 11, research branch `research/hay-article`, base `d8b59c62`.
No SC or executable definition is being adjudicated. The falsifiable
claim is that ninth-century Mercian ⟨heg⟩ continues the inherited
early-apocope direct singular without borrowing any part of another stem.
A same-environment control for final /e.i/ > /ej/, or an independent
Mercian example of ⟨eg⟩ spelling disyllabic /e.i/, would support this
claim. Evidence of prior final contraction together with unambiguous
glide notation would exclude both routes through retained hiatus.

## Existing state and diagnosis

The frozen SC047 research comparison selects the direct singular; its

observations. The research-only derivations and controls in the Cycle 10

## Phonological evidence

The local source holdings were checked first. Hogg §§3.19, 5.131–154
and Campbell §§234, 237.1b, 266–267, 406, 512, 553–557 cover
desyllabification, vocalization, contraction and metrical synizesis in
different environments. Hogg's *kla.wu > kla.u > klau* accounts for
*clēa*, with *cnēo/trēo* as related structural comparisons. Unlike
the proposed /e.i/ > /ej/, the second vowel is back and the resulting
sequence belongs to a native diphthong class. Campbell's loanword
gliding is prevocalic, while metrical synizesis is not a general
lexical sound change. None of these controls motivates a new formal
rule. Brunner §129 n. 5 provides the closest apparent ⟨ig⟩ case:
Lindisfarne *heigsta* occurs beside *heista/hesta*, is nonfinal,
reflects a different lost consonant, and is explicitly doubted.
Campbell's *streide/gecēide* group shows why ⟨ei⟩ does not diagnose
the source or fate of the second segment; *hefie* beside ⟨-ige⟩
controls the opposite direction. No convention for retained /e.i/
written ⟨eg⟩ was found in the early Mercian, Northumbrian or Kentish
evidence examined.

Ringe & Taylor §6.7.1 and §6.9.3 motivate *w*-loss and contraction.
Vespasian *gecēd* constrains nonfinal unlike-vowel contraction in
the very tradition that writes *heg*. Vespasian *sīem* (Hogg §5.146)
offers a positive retention comparison, but with *i.e* in the
opposite order. *Strēidæ/strēide* and metrically disyllabic *strēd*
show nonfinal hiatus can persist, without dating a particular
glossator's pronunciation. Final *glī* tests identical vowels;
*sǣ/ǣ* cannot distinguish contraction from loss of the second vowel.
There is no new bound for word-final unlike /e.i/ before the earliest
English attestations. No formal case was added: modeling /e.i/ >
/ej/ now would test an assumed transition, not independent evidence
for it. The existing controls retain their original scope.
Brunner §130 n. 3 also provides a specific alternative to
Campbell's re-formation analysis of *glīg*: the former compares
*nīġ-* from a sequence with an inherited *j*. Neither example
independently establishes a new English glide from syllabic *i*.

## Compound history and adjudication

Wright–Wülcker I:186.13 and I:237.36 print *highus* and *heghus*.
The latter is from Harley 3376 (collection VI); the former is in
the Supplement to Ælfric's vocabulary, whose manuscript attribution
in the edition is qualified. The glosses independently secure the
lexeme and permit a synchronic direct-shaped first element.
Campbell §349 n. 3 shows that *bedtīd, hrycgmearh, cynlīc* can use
a nominative-shaped base, which need not preserve an ungeminated
stem; §359 supplies independently inherited composition vowels.
A historical connector would change the environment for apocope
and gemination. No specific connector or inherited singular shape
is demonstrated for *heiweg*. Its provisional hay-road
identification is unchanged.

**Reformulate the article; defer the attribution of the Mercian
direct singular.** The premise-by-premise argument and adversarial
assessment are in `ARGUMENT_CYCLE11.md`. A narrow Anglian
restoration account is viable, but neither a matched final
resyllabification control nor direct observation of restoration was
found. Protected vocalism is the better-supported early-apocope
explanation in West Saxon. The data do not determine the English
donor paradigm, the origin of OHG *hewi*, the parse of *wj*, or the
chronology in the disputed final position. No production, registry,
benchmark, corpus, or frozen-model change follows.
