## D1. Dahl's competing derivation

### Source control

Dahl (1938: 82–86, 95–97) corresponds to PDF sheets 104–108 and
117–119 in the held scan (one-based). The printed chronology and
the notation of the displayed derivations were checked in the
page images, with a magnified view of p. 97. The earlier
[reassessment](../../DAHL_REASSESSMENT.md) is not a substitute
for this collation: the final sign in the reconstructed input is
superscript **n**, the parenthesized vowel is **æ**, and the
postdiphthongal **i** in the intermediate carries the
nonsyllabic mark. Do not silently convert it into the second
syllable of A's *he.i*.

### Chronology and the actual chains

Dahl's conclusion on pp. 83–86 is **loss of oral *-az* > gemination
> loss of short nasal *-aⁿ***. Neuter *cynn* and comparative
neuter geminates motivate the second edge. His appeal to Weser
*kunni* is explicitly qualified even in his own discussion
(authenticity and interpretation); it is not the sole evidence.
Masculine *hysi* and the mixed short and doubled stems motivate
the first edge, but the morphological classification and
direction of levelling are themselves argued rather than
observed. Resistance of long nasal vowels provides an analogy
for differential timing, not direct evidence for short nasal
vowels. See pp. 85–86 for the separate, later compound-medial
loss of *a* in his analysis.

For ‘hay’ he adds a distinct premise: specifically English
differentiation of nuclear *au* was already underway when
nasal loss exposed the final palatal; by a stage such as *ǣo*,
breaking up that nucleus to recover onset *w* was implausible
to him. This is a relative order with an independently supported
general *au* development but no independent dating witness for
its relation to nasal loss **in this family**. The first
displayed differentiated intermediate already contains the
palatal; Dahl does not place its final vocalization to
syllabic *i* in this ‘hay’ derivation. His rule for final
palatals instead deletes the nonsyllabic segment after long
nuclei. The protected palatal remains intervocalic following
a front vowel or front element. The note on that page also
distinguishes the potentially divergent instrumental:
if *j* fell before instrumental *i* early enough, a labial
could have been resyllabified; Dahl argues for counteracting
paradigm influence, not phonological impossibility in every cell.

**Original notation, normalized only for typography.** Here
`i̯` represents Dahl's mark under the nonsyllabic palatal;
the macron on *ǣo* is shown on the first letter. A single
initial reconstruction marker is used per chain:

- Direct: \*haui̯aⁿ > hǣoi̯(æ) > hǣoi̯ > hīe, hē > *hī(e)g, hēg*.
- Genitive: \*haui̯as > hǣoi̯æs > *hī(e)ges, hēges*.

**CAPR-facing normalization.** Dotting the OE palatal and
using `j` for Dahl's marked consonantal element does not
decide its exact phonetics:

- Direct: \*hau̯.ją > hǣo̯.j(æ) > hǣo̯j > hīe (WS) or hē
  (Anglian) > *hīeġ* or *hēġ*.
- Genitive: \*hau̯.jas > hǣo̯.jæs > *hīeġes* or *hēġes*.

The intermediate includes diphthongal differentiation and final
ending loss. Mutation and final palatal loss are bundled in
Dahl's next printed arrow; the interpretation requires that
the palatal's triggering effect not be lost before mutation.
The differentiated long nucleus supplies the quantity; neither
the original chain nor its normalization inserts a contraction
of short *e.i*. In the direct cell the displayed form loses
the palatal and then gets it back; the accompanying prose
also allows its analogically supported retention. This is
consonant-only influence from obliques already sharing the
nucleus, not Ringe and Taylor's whole-stem replacement
before umlaut. The oral/gemination/nasal order is shared with
B2, not the labial premise: B2 starts with consonantal *wj*.

### D1 assessment

Dahl gives a coherent explanation of why long Anglian and high
West Saxon nuclei could share an ancestry without English labial
gemination. His rejection of an inherited English *hawi/hewi*
is conditional on a diphthongal segmentation and early enough
differentiation. The absence of attested English *w*-forms in
his ‘hay’ material is real negative evidence for their survival,
not proof that no precursor contained *w*. Independently
motivated loss before unstressed *i*, or transfer of a stem
without *w*, can remove it. Conversely the later existence
of those mechanisms does not refute his diphthongal model.
The diagnostic tests are whether final loss is independently
quantitative, and whether comparable material fixes the
segmentation or the order without presupposing the outcome
of ‘hay’. No production verdict or chronology edge changes.

## D2. Does final palatal loss follow long nuclei?

### Independent evidence and controls

Dahl (97, PDF 119) analogizes final palatal loss to final labial
loss. These are not a single attested process: Hogg §§2.76–77,
7.69 distinguishes the two approximants and warns that ⟨i⟩
beside ⟨g⟩ can be another spelling for the same consonant.
An independent test must distinguish inherited Germanic *j*,
secondary palatal *g*, and a genuinely syllabic vowel. It
must also establish the input's quantity without assuming
the proposed loss or restoration.

| Environment | Evidence and limitation |
|---|---|
| Short vowel + final palatal | ‘Way’ and ‘day’ are secure quantity contrasts from the existing inventory, but their palatal is secondary. Their retention shows that short-V-plus-palatal is a viable OE structure, not that inherited *j* necessarily had the same history at the relevant prehistoric date. No secure independent short, inherited final *j* input has been identified in this bounded set; the direct ‘hay’ input under H2 is the question, not another witness. |
| Long vowel + inherited final *j* | ‘Key’ *cǣġ* is a positive retention comparison. Hogg (1979: 105, PDF 17) explicitly identifies the final palatal with the inherited formative and derives dat. *cǣġe* from a long nucleus before umlaut. Ringe and Taylor (235, 286, PDF 250, 301) independently show a long *kā-* in their northern WGmc input and inherited *j* in the result. Oblique support is possible but not demonstrated to have restored the final palatal; the word rules out unqualified inference from heavy quantity to observed loss. |
| Short diphthong + inherited final *j* | The theory predicts loss if “diphthongs” includes short diphthongs, as its literal wording does. The controlled short-diphthong input tests the prediction, but no independently reconstructed, securely quantified lexical example establishes that environment in this panel. |
| Long diphthong + inherited final *j* | *Īeġ* ‘island’ compares retention in another labial family and is represented in the existing inventory. It is not decisive because formation, chronology and analogy in the family are disputed. Its protected comparators and Dahl's own proposed alternatives are taken up in D6. |
| Apparent loss | The w-less *glī* of ‘glee’ has rivals involving identical hiatus or an analogical palatal-bearing form; its derivation cannot independently establish prior final consonantal *j*. In ‘free’, Campbell §§238.1, 410, 648 contrasts *frēo* from vocalic *i* before a back vowel with the *frīg-* stem from the consonantal glide of the front-vowel forms (§§410, 648: pp. 168, 269, PDF 186, 287). Neither the contracted ‘cow’ *cȳ* nor the preconsonantal losses in Campbell §§243–245 and Hogg §7.71 establish absolute-final consonant loss. |

If a final quantitative rule operated **after** *w*-loss and
short desyllabification, it would not remove short *hej* and
could remove *hēj*. If it preceded either event, the latter
sequence would escape simply because it had not yet formed.
To preserve triggering of mutation in Dahl's own direct
history, deletion must follow the relevant mutation or its
conditioning. That dependency is internal to his analysis;
there is no independently dated absolute-final process here
to place on either side of mutation, high-vowel loss or the
resolution of the later *w*-loss input.

### Executable distinction

The frozen `OEJLossAfterHeavy` matches **V + C + j** or
**short V + C + C + j**, with the stated restriction on *r*.
It has no right-edge requirement, does not remove *j*
immediately after a single nucleus, and has no target
`*ʝ` or `*i`. The frozen front-diphthong strengthening targets
*j* only with a following vowel. Neither is Dahl's proposal.
The [overlay](palatal.py) tests Dahl's literal absolute edge
with the frozen long-vowel and diphthong classes, before and
after mutation, high-vowel loss and the merger; it makes no
claim that the source independently fixes any of those six
placements. The [manifest](palatal.tsv) separates full
submissions, reused by ID when already fixed, from
source-based or synthetic suffix probes. It does not conflate
inherited *j* and secondary *ʝ* before the merger. The
separately sourced key alternatives use their stated
grammatical cells and developmental inputs, not substitutions
made to repair an output.

## D3. What the later evidence establishes about *w*-loss

### Dahl’s objection in context

The testable claim is not that every Old English *e* before
written ⟨g⟩ comes from *ewi*, but that independent words can
establish regular loss in that phonological environment.
Dahl (100–102, PDF 122–124; p. 101 checked against the image)
argues from the competing compound spellings of ‘island’.
Girvan, starting from the same ungeminated reconstruction as
Chadwick and Luick, accounts for Northumbrian *-eu* by adding
*u* back to *ē*, itself derived from *ewi*. Dahl admits
another way to derive *-eu* from this input, through assimilation
of *i* to *u* rather than loss of *w*. The analogy he cites there
is *eowu* ‘sheep’. What he specifically calls insecure is the
evidence for regular loss between *e* and *i*, invoked to obtain
*ē* and then *ēg*. His preference for a single base for both
sets of nominatives helps drive the objection; it is not an
independently observed phonological constraint.

He knows the *g*-bearing infinitives of ‘call’ and ‘strew’ and
the labial in ‘show’ (102 n. 3), which he treats as obscure but
possibly influenced by *awi*-forms. Those are not the
same cells as the w-less endings cited by the later grammars.
In ‘glee’ and ‘new’ he considers the high-vowel comparison,
and explicitly concedes that assimilation in *iwi* is plausible
in itself (102–104, PDF 124–126); he doubts its use in the
proposed history given his account of gemination and the
apparent West Saxon confinement of the relevant forms.
The phrase about insecure traces on p. 101 does not enumerate
individual examples. It would be unwarranted either to assign
him the later full list or to infer from silence that he was
unaware of a particular gloss.

### What the enlarged comparisons decide

Campbell (1959: 100–101, 166–167, §§237.1b, 406;
PDF 118–119, 184–185) and Ringe and Taylor (2014: 258–261,
318; PDF 273–276, 333) separate the loss from the subsequent
fate of the high vowel. The central comparisons are:

| Comparison | Evidential role |
|---|---|
| ‘Prepare’ *ġierwan/ġerwan* vs. *ġiereþ*, *ġierede/ġerede*; Campbell also adduces ‘roll’ *wyleþ* vs. *wylwan*. | The alternation within a root is phonological: earlier *-CijV-* syncope created protected *wj* in the infinitive, whereas the finite forms still had *wi*. After *w*-loss the remaining consonant can onset the next syllable; **no hiatus** is created. This independent control argues against an ad hoc rule between two particular vowels. |
| ‘Pillow’ *pyle* from Latin *pulvīnus* via pre-OE *pulwīn*. | The conditioning high vowel can also be long if fully unstressed. The loan is independent of the labial *j*-stems. Its date does not fix a date for resolution of postvocalic *e.i*. |
| ‘Flock’ *ēde* and Vespasian ‘call’ *ceð, cede, geced*. | Short *e* after mutation, with *w* followed by unstressed *i*, offers the closest independent parallels. Comparative forms and the represented cells motivate *ewi* without assuming anything about direct ‘hay’. In these reconstructions mere deletion of *i* cannot produce the reported long *ē*. West Saxon *eowede* is analysed as remodelled on ‘ewe’, not a refutation from an identical unrestructured input. |
| ‘Strew’ *strēdun*, contested early glosses *streidæ/streide*, Beowulf *strēd*. | Possible evidence for persistence of the product of loss before contraction, not three unqualified short-input proofs. The lexical identification of the early glosses and the metrical interpretation of the verse retain the qualifications in the [first-cycle dossier](ADJUDICATION.md). |
| ‘Sea’, ‘law’; ‘know’, ‘betray’ and ‘show’. | *w*-less comparisons with a long preceding nucleus. Deletion of the weak vowel and contraction can converge, so their outputs do not uniquely establish contraction. ‘Show’ also has uncertain prehistory; ‘corpse’ has competing stem histories. ‘Crow’ is another long-nucleus verbal comparison, not another short *e.i* witness. |
| *nī-* ‘new’, *glī* ‘merrymaking’; *strēn* ‘couch’. | The first two are identical-vowel comparisons, not evidence for identical timing in short unlike hiatus. ‘Couch’ would extend the unlike sequence to a nasal under the proposed reconstruction; its stem formation remains conditional here. |

The supported generalization is loss of noninitial *w* before
fully unstressed *i/ī*, after either a vowel or a consonant.
The data do not limit it to short or long preceding nuclei,
to a particular vowel quality, or to one word class. The
surviving *cleweþa* ‘itch’ is a real complication: Ringe and
Taylor (260–261) suggest analogy or competition with a different
suffixal input; neither explanation is independently established.
Continental *nī* and *streidun* support the
plausibility of parallel loss, not an identical ancestral date.
For English, ‘ewe’ motivates **umlaut > transfer out of the
i-stems > w-loss** on Ringe and Taylor’s stem-transfer analysis.
‘Prepare’ motivates **w-loss > general syncope** because loss
creates a light syllable and bleeds syncope. These are historical
dependencies with separate witnesses, not dates inferred from
component positions.

Postvocalic loss can leave two syllabic vowels, e.g.
*ewidi > e.idi*; that is the intermediate predicted by removal
alone, not a pronunciation read directly off the contracted
form. The attested relics allow more than one resolution
history. For short-input *ēde* and
*cēð*, a length-producing development is needed if the
reconstruction and quantity are correct. Contraction accounts
for it without positing a palatal consonant; the endpoint does
not independently exclude gliding followed by palatal loss and
lengthening. Final ‘sea’ and ‘law’ can lose a weak vowel
without changing the earlier quantity; final ‘glee’ involves
identical vowels. None establishes that final **short unlike**
*e.i* behaved as in *e.i.d* or *e.i.þ*. The evidence is uneven
by position, but that is not proof of a position-dependent
resolution law or of a specifically dental trigger. Ringe and
Taylor (318) leave the timing open and allow unlike hiatus to
persist longer than identical hiatus.

### Frozen checks and verdict

The existing [H0 baseline](baseline.json) independently
distinguishes the live executable contexts: in A, ‘prepare’
loses *w* in the present but not in the infinitive, ‘sea’
passes through long *ǣ.i*, and direct ‘hay’ passes through
short *e.i* and renders `hei`. The ‘call’ participle and
‘strew’ participle reach short *e.i* before *d*, but the
frozen outputs `ġecid` and `streid` do not reproduce the
putative length-producing history. ‘Flock’, ‘pillow’ and
‘prepared’ are unadmitted as submitted; their historical
evidential standing is independent of that gate. The
[D2 control](palatal.json) preserves *cǣġ* as a comparison
for final inherited *j* after a long nucleus, **not** as a
w-loss positive. No added run, changed input, or new census
is needed for this literature increment.

**Research verdict:** the subsequent comparative evidence
removes the basis for dismissing regular *w*-loss in *ewi*
as an expedient. If an English direct *hewi* existed, *he.i*
has an independently supported route. The evidence does not
establish that this direct stem existed, require it for the
Northumbrian compound forms, or diagnose its final outcome.
Dahl’s differentiated-diphthong history remains a competitor;
its starting point and the actual treatment of compound
‘island’ require separate tests. No production verdict or
chronology edge changes.

## D4. ‘District’: a close reconstruction, not a matched attestation

### Collation of Dahl’s English material

Dahl (98–99, PDF 120–121, image checked) lists only final members
of names and derivatives. The table transcribes his readings;
multiple loci in a row share the accompanying description unless
specified otherwise. `HE` loci are Plummer volume I by page/line;
`Ct` loci are Sweet, *Oldest English Texts*, by item/line; `Chr`
is the Parker Chronicle in Earle–Plummer. Verification is against
Dahl’s printed page, not the manuscript, except that the indicated
Sweet comparisons independently check his transcription against
an edition. Dahl’s dialect labels are witness assignments, not
proof that each name originated in that dialect.

All the putative district elements are **second members**;
weaker stress than on the first member is a reasonable working
hypothesis, but neither a measured stress level nor an inference
that these elements were historically unstressed. In particular,
Latin embedding and ethnonym derivation prevent the endings
from being read as a single Old English noun paradigm.

### What historical contrast is established

Gothic *hawi/gawi* and OHG *hewi/gewi* alongside *houwi/gouwi*
make the two inherited labial-plus-palatal families close
comparators, not cognates of one another. Ringe and Taylor
(2014: 173) distinguish inherited *hewi* from the geminate
history in *houwi*; Orel (2003: 128) lists the Gothic and
OHG district pair, and Kroonen (2013: 171) explicitly lists
**OE *-gē*** with a hyphen. The coexistence of continental
labial reflexes motivates testing analogous reconstructed
cells. It does not force the same levelling, timing or stress
in English. Campbell (1959: 245 §610 n. 4) adduces
*Eosterge* as a locatival form and accounts for some variants
by re-formation with ‘island’. This is positive evidence for
a territorial second member, not an independent attestation
of a free *gē* with directly known quantity.

Dahl’s regular direct branch is long and palatal-less
(*-gīe/-gē*); he thinks the corresponding protected stem lost
its palatal through dissimilation under weak compound stress,
leaving no donor for direct-cell restoration. He also proposes
a separate contracted history within *Sudergeona*. The
hypothesis is coherent, but a second-member position from which
weak stress is inferred is not independent evidence that this
dissimilation operated. The most useful contrasts are between
mixed graphic forms and different grammatical contexts, not
between a securely attested short/long simplex ‘district’ and
a simplex ‘hay’. Thus *hewi > he.i* and *gewi > ge.i* are
worth testing under equal conditions, but sameness of their
English inputs is itself a counterfactual, especially given
initial-palatal effects and the compound evidence.

## D5. What *hei weg* can tell us about compound position

Dahl (86 n., 97, 104; PDF 108, 119, 126) separates loss
of the old vowel at the end of a first compound member
from simplex apocope. He treats the compound loss as a
development in the individual West Germanic dialects and
places English differentiation of inherited *au* before both
the relevant compound-medial loss and loss of the residual
simplex ending. That does not order the two losses more
precisely against one another. In the ‘glee’ comparison
he explicitly puts simplex loss of the exposed palatal
**after mutation**; in a primary compound the palatal of
the first member instead remains within the word and can
survive without borrowing from an inflected simplex. He
mentions *hēg-* and *īg-* by comparison under an argument
stated explicitly for West Saxon. He treats the unusual
*glii-* as secondary relative to *glīg-*, not as an example
of the simplex word-final loss (104, PDF 126, image checked).
The compound argument does not require the creation or
contraction of short *e.i*; that belongs to a different
starting representation.

The testable consequence is conditional but important:
on Dahl’s reconstruction, a first member with a long vowel
and surviving palatal is possible even if an unremodelled
simplex would have yielded *hē*. This follows from the
different edge, not from quantifying the accent of *hei*.
An ordinary internal /VjC/ sequence is not in itself a
compound, and a weak final *second* member is still at the
absolute word edge. Dahl’s specific weak-stress dissimilation
account for ‘district’ is an additional claim, not a
consequence of distinguishing these edges. His West Saxon
formulation does not independently establish the Kentish
generalization; using the charter to establish it and then
as its prediction would be circular.

### Date and interpretation of the witness

Dahl’s printed p. 96 (PDF 118, rechecked against the image)
has **“heiweg (‘hay-road’), 43/3 (A.D. 859)”**, not 839 as
in the OCR. The preceding *Hegforde* entry has A.D. 858.
Sweet, p. 450 (PDF 464, image checked), prints **cyniges hei
weg** at the same locus and heads the charter **859**.
There is therefore no demonstrated 839 misprint in Dahl;
the discrepancy is between the image and the OCR. The paused
manuscript’s note attributing 839 to Dahl needs correction
at a later authorized article revision; it has not been
changed in this assignment. Separately, Lowe (2001: 85–86)
gives **? 859** for S 1196 (Cotton Augustus II 16) because
Æthelwulf appears in the witness list despite dying in 858.
The date of the sheet, the transaction and the witness list
need not be identical. This is a diplomatic qualification,
not support for an alternative 839.

‘Hay-road’ is Dahl’s explicit reading, also compatible with
the meadows in the bounds and the later road-name comparison.
It is not a contemporary gloss of the phrase. The ‘highway’
alternative and the earlier discussion of *hēah-* remain
relevant; lexical interpretation does not follow from an
endpoint match. If ‘hay’ is right, ⟨hei⟩ is an exact
**graphemic** counterpart of the frozen A serialization.
It is compatible with inherited short material plus gliding,
and with inherited diphthongal vocalism plus retention of
the compound palatal under Dahl’s account. Kentish *dei*
shows how ⟨ei⟩ can write a vowel plus palatal, not that
⟨ei⟩ measures quantity or identifies hiatus. Neither candidate
is corroborated specifically unless one can discriminate
the earlier structure independently. With no matched
Kentish simplex known to have lost its palatal, the charter
does not demonstrate differential treatment.

## D6. The four lexical families

The relevant comparison is not merely whether a word ends
in written ⟨g⟩. The rival accounts predict different
distribution across cells, inherited labial and palatal
representations, and compound positions. The following
checks use Dahl’s original pp. 99–107 (PDF 121–129), Sweet’s
editions and the later reconstruction in Ringe and Taylor
(2014: 53, 246, 250, 259). The held text corpora and
glossary loci give a bounded distribution check, not an
exhaustive independently dialect-tagged DOE census.

### ‘Glee’

Luick and Girvan, as presented by Dahl (96, 103), obtain
the direct cell from \*gliwi > gli.i > glī. Written *glīg*
then starts as an inverse spelling for the long vowel and
supplies the graphic or reinterpreted palatal to inflected
forms; it is not the first product of hiatus contraction.
Dahl explicitly accepts that the assimilatory development
is phonetically credible. His objections are the apparent
restriction to West Saxon, and his own reconstruction of
an undissolved diphthong at ending loss, which leaves no
syllabic *gliwi* input at that date. On his account the
mutated direct branch loses final *j* and can restore it
from the protected forms; the unmutated branch yields
*glīo(w)*. The labial branch of the obliques involves
assimilation to the unmutated diphthong’s second element;
the mutated branch preserves the intervocalic palatal. He
treats *glīw* as a compromise between the high-vowel palatal
and *glēow*, not straightforward evidence that the shortest
spelling is oldest. A first-member *glīg-* instead
potentially preserves its consonant directly (D5).

The attested split is real in the checked material: Sweet’s
*Pastoral Care* p. 231 (PDF 243, Hatton; image checked)
has *gligmonna*; the Cotton facing-page text also preserves
it. Dahl’s protected *glige* at 183/25 is not a bare
simplex. The early Mercian *gliu*, *glio*, and protected
*gliuuæ/gliowe* (Épinal 398, 550; Corpus 825, 1112)
remain the labial comparison in Ringe and Taylor 250
(PDF 265, image checked). The latter do not assign an
unequivocal dialect to poetic *glīw*. There is no secure
counterexample to Dahl’s West Saxon concentration in this
comparison, but overlapping copies, poetic forms, and the
uncertainty of bare ⟨i⟩ prevent turning it into an exclusive
historical sound-law distribution. Campbell §§237.1b,
411 treats *glīman* and the possible analogical creation
of *glīg-* cautiously. The hypotheses would be better
discriminated by an early protected palatal predating
levelling, or secure unremodelled regional direct cells.

### ‘Hue’

Dahl’s p. 105 (PDF 127, image checked) contrasts Mercian
*hio(w)/heow* with West Saxon *hiew/hiw* and inflected
*hiwes/hiewes*, *hiwe/hiewe*. He expects but cannot find
a West Saxon *hī(e)g* in his sources. His proposed avoidance
of homophony with ‘hay’ presupposes the lost form and the
sound identity he needs to explain; no attested confusion,
independent replacement trajectory, or dated transition
establishes avoidance. Ringe and Taylor (250) instead
propose a fronted labial glide in the protected history,
yielding *hīew > hīw*, and a distinct w-less direct
development in West Saxon, which they reconstruct rather
than cite as the ordinary attested simplex. The attested
labial stem is consistent with the separate development and
does not require a detour through an erstwhile homophone.
‘Hue’ is therefore a negative check on extrapolating the
‘glee’ palatal to every *iwj* stem, not independent evidence
for either ancestry of ‘hay’.

### ‘New’

Dahl (105–107, PDF 127–129, image checked) assigns the
masculine nominative the oral ending *-az*, already lost
before English differentiation, with an inherited *niwi*
input. His neuter *-ą* survives long enough for the
English diphthong to resist dissolution. He then requires
masculine labial material to oust the expected neuter
*nī(e)g/nīow* and lets oblique labial vocalism remodel
*niwe* to *niowe*. This is not a direct observation of
an oral/nasal contrast: Vespasian *niowe* is reported in
both masculine nominative (80/10) and neuter accusative
(68/32). Dahl’s other analysis distinguishes a simplex
*ija*-stem from a compound *ja*-stem, prompted by the
lack of the inflected palatal forms the single-stem
analysis predicts. The *nīg-/nī-* competition, with
*nicealtan* in Dahl and *nīcenned*, *nīcumen* in Ringe
and Taylor (259), discriminates the **distribution**
of composition material from the simplex labial stem.
It does not alone choose retention plus contraction over
direct *iwi > i.i > ī*, nor fix the relative chronology
of oral and nasal loss. The analogue with ‘glee’ is partly
morphological and the quantity diagnostics are not matched.

### ‘Island’

Dahl’s HE series includes frequent *-eu* (e.g.
*Laestingaeu*, *Heruteu*, *Peartaneu*, *Seleseu*), *-ei*
(e.g. *Cerotaesei*, *Herutei*), possible *-ea*, a small
number of *-eig/-ig* readings including alterations,
and uncertain oblique *-e/-ei/-æi/-awi* (99–102,
PDF 121–124). Sweet’s text of Bede compares *Heruteu*
with *Heruteig* in the passage glossing the name *insula
cervi*, and elsewhere *Heruteu* with *Herutei*. This
fixes the lexical family there; the forms are neither three
independently attested quantities nor three simplex cells.
The early West Saxon forms *Sceapige*, *Turecesiege*,
*iegbuendum*, and *igland*, and the later charter
*Ceortesege* (Ct 45/22, /30), preserve a palatal-bearing
spelling in different positions and cells. *Sel(es)egi*
before Latin *ecclesiae* admits Latin genitival spelling,
and Latin case frames or inferred vernacular locatives
must not be treated as secure direct-cell evidence.

Dahl’s regular diphthongal branch yields a long nucleus
and eventual independent or analogically supported
palatal; he explains the Northumbrian compound *-eu*
instead from loss of the palatal element before retained
final *u* under weak stress. His alternatives for *-ei*
include a spelling of a palatal-bearing form and a
locative development; neither is licensed automatically
by the letters. Ringe and Taylor’s separately reconstructed
geminate-type branch (246) also yields *īeġ/ēġ* after
mutation and high-vowel loss. This is a useful **conditional
long-nucleus control** alongside ‘key’, not evidence for
an independently measured short or long direct simplex
in the HE. Where an edition shows a correction, that
correction is evidence for a written alternant; without
independently dated reading layers it cannot date a sound
change.

## D7. Diphthongal assimilation on the continent

Dahl’s excursus (107–109, PDF 129–131) is a positive
alternative to ordinary gemination. The trigger in the
labial forms is the surviving labial **part of a nucleus**;
the target is the **following palatal**. Compare ordinary
gemination, which doubles an already consonantal labial
before *j* without first changing *j*. In normalized
notation the competing protected inputs are
\*au̯.jes > au̯.wes versus \*a.wjes > a.wwjes.
Both can ultimately write a diphthongal or geminate-looking
labial sequence; those later spellings alone cannot recover
the first operation or its timing.

| Comparison | Evidence and discrimination |
|---|---|
| OHG *hewi/houwi*, *gewi/gouwi*, *gistrewi/gistrouwi* | Dahl groups the first members as old direct forms and attributes the diphthongal labial stem to copying of labial articulation from the nucleus. Ringe and Taylor (53, PDF 68, image checked) explicitly recognize inherited *hewi* beside geminated *houwi* and compare the verb *gistrouwen*; their verb is not the same grammatical form as Dahl’s nominal pair. Orel 128 independently lists *gewi/gouwi*. Rare ungeminated forms pose a general problem for the claim that every consonantal *wj* geminated before any relevant ending loss, not for every theory allowing established cell differences. Dahl admits gemination is the simplest account of doubling spellings taken by themselves. |
| OS | Dahl cites direct *gō(i)* and dat. *gauwa*: distinct cell material is real on his evidence, but an alternation by cell is compatible with either a conditioned development or transfer from protected material. Kroonen (215) lists *hōi* with an asterisk; its citation is not by itself a verified token. |
| OFris, ODu and MDu | Dahl cites OFris *hā* and *gē* as outcomes with distinct intermediate vowel histories; Kroonen (171, 215) lists OFris *gā/gē*, *hā*, and MDu *gou(w)/gooy* for ‘district’, while Orel lists OS compounded *-gō/-gā*. Dahl’s specific route through a restored labial in OFris ‘hay’ and his appeal to inherited singleton forms in ODu rely on his reconstruction and cited van Helten; the individual original texts and their dates have not all been collated. Do not equate modern Dutch *hooi* with an independently observed Old Dutch stage. |
| ‘New’ | Dahl cites OS *nigemo*, *nigean*, and compound *nī-*; OFris *nige* beside *nia/nie*; Dutch place-names in *nij-*; and labial OHG/OS *niuwi* and ODu *nūwi* (108, PDF 130, image checked). The continental palatal spellings matter precisely because Dahl cannot appeal to specifically West Saxon mutation there. He allows a distinct stem class as an alternative source of w-forms. Ringe and Taylor cite OS/OHG *niuwi* and independently compare ‘sew’ (*siuw-*), interpreting the high-vowel evidence in favour of earlier gemination. A toponymic series in *nij-* does not establish a matched simplex cell or one dated origin. |

Dahl’s chronology requires the English differentiation of
the nucleus to precede the loss exposing the palatal.
On the continental direct route the corresponding
differentiation is later, so the newly exposed sequence
can resolve instead to a short nucleus, consonantal *w*
and syllabic *i*. In protected inputs the retained labial
element of the diphthong motivates palatal-to-labial
assimilation; an oblique can in turn supply its stem to
the direct cell. Evidence for the **completed** labial
reflex is independent of English (especially in OHG).
The interpretation as copying, the absolute sequence of
events, and the proposed analogical direction are not
independently fixed by those reflexes. The chronological
problem Dahl raises is worth testing even where one
prefers the alternative of early ungeminated direct
cells and ordinary gemination in protected forms.

| 3. Is absolute-final palatal loss defensible? | As a conditional reconstruction, yes: the loss and later restoration distinguish exposed and protected cells and could preserve written variation. As an independently established sound law with Dahl’s broad quantity conditioning, no: *cǣġ* retains an inherited palatal after a long nucleus. Supposing restoration there too is possible, but is another historical claim requiring evidence. CAPR’s postconsonantal heavy-context deletion is not the same operation. |
| 4. Does the quantitative condition support regular short /hej/? | It makes survival after a short vowel **compatible** with the proposed restriction, once glide formation is independently assumed. It does not positively attest such survival: D2 found no secure independent short monophthong plus inherited final palatal in its comparison panel, and *hei/heg* cannot establish vowel duration or the syllabicity of the earlier sequence. |
| 5. Does ‘district’ discriminate? | It exposes a hidden premise: Dahl needs weak-stress dissimilation to remove the palatal from protected forms and deprive the exposed stem of a donor. The attestation is largely compound, often embedded in Latin, and partly ambiguous with ‘island’. Neither that dissimilation nor unremodelled direct-simplex quantity is established. Initial palatal conditioning also differentiates the frozen ‘district’ and ‘hay’ paths before their comparable post-labial hiatus. The family challenges indiscriminate analogy but does not choose the ‘hay’ history. |
| 6. Can simplex versus compound explain *hei weg*? | Yes, conditionally: first-member *j* can be retained if deletion is truly restricted to absolute word edge. The Kentish compound has no matched unremodelled simplex in the same witness, and ⟨ei⟩ does not identify a short nucleus, long nucleus, or retained consonant. This explains how the form could coexist with Dahl’s loss; it does not demonstrate either the loss or its domain in Kentish. |
| 7. What weaknesses does the lexical comparison expose? | The frozen cascades distinguish some direct and protected inputs, but they do not generate the full attested cell and compound distributions: ‘new’ needs a separate account of the labial simplex and palatal or contracted composition forms, ‘hue’ a reason for the predominance of labial forms, and ‘glee’ independently justified stem histories. ‘Island’ illustrates how quantitative and morphological inference can outrun mixed written evidence. An output that fits a preferred reflex does not supply these missing histories. |
| 8. Is the continental theory a separate question? | Yes. A/B2 compare paths inside the frozen English investigation; neither pits a surviving nuclear labial trigger against ordinary consonantal gemination across continental stem cells. OHG supplies genuinely independent reflexes, but their chronology and morphology do not yet choose the mechanism. D7 spells out the contrasting intermediate predictions rather than scoring identical endpoints as different historical evidence. |

Dahl’s strongest contribution is to make the distinction
between **retention in a protected position** and
**restoration in an exposed cell** historically consequential.
His account can link a long nucleus, divergent direct
and protected reflexes, and a retaining compound edge
without contracting short *e.i* or substituting an entire
protected stem. The required intermediate nucleus, the
loss and its restoration, and the chronology that protects
the obliques are nevertheless distinct claims. The
English evidence does not require the entire package.

The most useful new evidence would be (i) a securely
localized direct form distinguishing short mid vocalism,
long mid vocalism, and diphthongal vocalism independently
of ⟨g⟩; (ii) an independently inherited short-nucleus
palatal control, together with a long-nucleus comparison
not dependent on analogy for its classification; (iii)
matched direct, protected, and first-member forms in the
same dialect and period; or (iv) continental evidence
ordering exposure of the direct palatal against the
emergence of labial or geminate reflexes. Until then,
carry *cǣġ* forward as an independent countercontrol,
and keep all three Anglian mechanisms open: regular
desyllabification, contraction with later palatal
restoration, and protected-stem transfer. Dahl’s
diphthongal route remains a distinct competitor, not
an adopted change or a synonym for any of the frozen
variants.

## J1. What underwrites Jones’s short ‘hay’?

Jones (1989: 149, PDF 163, image checked) prints
**[hej]/[heij]** as late Old English alternatives,
referring back to §2.3.6. A single *e* contrasts with his
doubled long vowels; the second form has a complex
nucleus rather than a doubled monophthong. His [j] is
explicitly a **voiced palatal fricative** (55, 149),
approximately [ʝ] in modern IPA, not the approximant
/j/. Thus his contrast is schematically [eʝ] versus
[ei̯ʝ], not [e.i] versus [ej]. The second vowel symbol in
the second form represents an offglide within the
stressed space, not an independently syllabic vowel.

The source of the proposal is traceable. At 19–21 Jones
compares short-vowel-plus-fricative reconstructions with
long-vowel or diphthongal outcomes. At 21 he specifically
quotes Lindisfarne ⟨heg/heig⟩ alongside the variants of
‘ask’ and ‘thane’ as possible cases where the fricative
stayed consonantal while the preceding vowel broke. At
55–56 he does so again: Table 2.29 groups the two spellings
under [ɛ]/[ɛi], and the explanation explicitly takes
⟨heg/heig⟩ as an example of diphthongization before a
palatal fricative. The difference between the table’s
[ɛ] and p. 149’s [e] does not turn the nucleus into a
long monophthong. The printed gloss in Table 2.29 is
**‘meadow’**, whereas pp. 21 and 149 say **‘hay’**.
That inconsistency calls for checking the edition and
Latin loci, not for silently correcting the table or
discounting the explicit transcriptions.

Neither of the two graphic forms shows independently
measured vowel length. The plain/digraph alternation is
the empirical starting point for Jones’s interpretation
of **nuclear shape**. The comparison with ‘ask’ and
‘thane’ motivates an analogous treatment, but their
position before a nasal does not independently calibrate
the quantity of this absolute-final example. Campbell
(1959: §§50, 266–269) and Wright (1925: §321) support
the background on palatals and late breaking; Jones
does not identify a source that independently assigns
shortness to the ‘hay’ tokens. At 30–31 he discusses
Aldred’s variable marking of long vowels by doubling
or an acute, including independently long lexical
controls and cases of homorganic lengthening. At 56 he
actually appeals to the acute in ⟨wég⟩ ‘way’, but does
not make the corresponding argument for ‘hay’ or show
that a single unaccented graph proves shortness. Nor
does he derive the short vowel from a prehistoric
etymology. It is the input he assigns within his
interpretation of the written alternation.

Table 3.17 (150, PDF 164, image checked) instead presents
West Saxon ⟨hēg⟩ and ⟨hīg⟩ as **[heej]** and **[hiij]**
in its comparison with Middle English. This distinct
reference column does not retroactively lengthen the
late Old English pair on the previous page. Conversely,
the Middle English spellings through 154 cannot verify
the short quantity of particular Northumbrian tokens.

**Research verdict:** Jones is a genuine published
precedent for short Northumbrian vocalism and a retained
fricative, not independent proof of quantity and not
precedent for a prehistoric \*he.i derivation. A direct
edition and manuscript audit is needed for the attribution
and reading of the individual ⟨heg/heig⟩ tokens. The
published quantity claim is on record even if that audit
ultimately weakens its empirical basis.

## J2. What the palatal changes can actually explain

Jones (19–21, 55–58, 149–154) distinguishes four
outcomes of a postvocalic voiced palatal fricative. His
analysis concerns the location of the second element in
the rhyme and whether it preserves its own quality and
consonantal character. Table 3.19 (153) distinguishes a
peak capable of holding two vowel elements from a
consonantal coda. Tables 3.20–21 use a velar example
(*boga*) to illustrate the transfer from coda to peak;
the model is then applied to both fricatives, not cited
as direct evidence for ‘hay’.

| Outcome | Distinguishing prediction |
|---|---|
| Retain [ʝ]: [eʝ] | A single vowel and a consonantal coda remain. Neither a vocalic offglide nor increased duration is implied. |
| Vocalize [ʝ]: [eʝ] > [ei̯] | The second portion of the peak bears the palatal element’s own quality, and the original fricative no longer occupies the coda. Jones compares ‘maiden’ at 21, 56. This predicts a diphthong, not yet a long monophthong. |
| Vocalize and assimilate: [eʝ] > [eː] | At 57 the second element loses its consonantal character **and** takes its quality from the first vowel. At 58 the resulting peak is internally durationally complex but uniform in quality. Jones’s statement allows simultaneous reinterpretation; it does not assert an attested intermediate [ei̯]. |
| Break before retained [ʝ]: [eʝ] > [ei̯ʝ] | Palatal quality enters the peak but the fricative stays in the coda. Jones treats ‘ask’, ‘thane’, and the reported Lindisfarne ‘hay’ pair in this way. Retention of the fricative is the difference from simple vocalization; the letters alone do not prove it. |

These are not interchangeable with direct contraction
of two syllabic vowels, \*e.i > ē: that account has no
consonant to vocalize. They are also not the same as
literal loss followed by compensation, [eʝ] > [e] >
[eː]; Jones (20–21) explicitly offers **substitution**
instead of loss, making the old fricative the source of
the second portion. A serial account through [ei̯]
followed by monophthongization makes an additional
intermediate prediction that the p. 57 analysis by
itself does not require.

For \*e.iD > ejD > ēD, the first arrow gains no direct
support here. If its result is an approximant [j],
Jones’s comparison supports the second arrow only by
analogy: either establish an intervening strengthening
to [ʝ], or show separately that an approximant can
undergo the same reanalysis. If desyllabification
directly yields [ei̯D], that instead tests the serial
vocalic route and bypasses a consonantal stage. A
research trace should therefore keep `*e*i` (syllabic
hiatus), atomic `*ei` (complex peak), `*e*j`
(approximant), and `*e*ʝ` (fricative) distinct at the
input to the tested operation, even when spellings or
final outputs converge.

**Research verdict:** postvocalic reanalysis predicts
that a consonantal trigger may become a nuclear
offglide, supply a second interval of like quality, or
survive after breaking. Compare fricative versus
approximant, short versus long nuclei, and the same
sequence before a consonant, at word edge, and before
an independent vowel. Jones (154) proposes that
vocalization is favoured by voicing but explicitly
acknowledges voiceless examples and insufficient
evidence. He does not demonstrate a categorical split
between dental context and absolute edge. ‘Maiden’
and ‘thane’ make that split unsafe to assume: nasal
context can coexist with diphthongal spellings, and
the actual dialect, stage, consonant and spellings
must be checked before either becomes a counterexample
to a more narrowly conditioned history.

**Research verdict:** both long monophthongs and

both retained consonants and diphthongal developments
are possible in the final series. That provides a positive
reason to test all those outcomes, but not a positive
reason to assign a categorical final/preconsonantal
**hiatus** rule: most examples start with a consonant
of independent ancestry, some retain it, and dialect
and chronology matter. The orthographic ‘maiden’
contrast prevents treating a following dental as a
sufficient condition for length without checking the
actual input and domain.

## J4. What the two-stage theory predicts

De Chene and Anderson’s [1979 article abstract](https://doi.org/10.2307/413316)
formulates a strong historical analysis. Exclude cases
where disappearance and lengthening are independent;
in the remainder their source consonant is immediately
adjacent to the affected vowel. A consonant weakens
in a coda to a semivocalic or laryngeal glide, which
joins the nucleus; monophthongization of that complex
nucleus yields a phonologically long vowel **if and
only if** there is independent length contrast in
the system. A synchronic alternation between a consonant
and length does not entail a single historical act of
compensation.

The published evidence is not simply a set of matched
lengthened endpoints. The searchable article extracts
compare, among others, Latin voiced *s* before a dental
in the ancestors of *nīdus* and *sīdō*, and exclude
cases of consonant disappearance without a causally
related length change. Komi Ižma *l* loss and lengthening,
compared with a labial reflex in other Komi varieties,
illustrate a possible intermediate, not a diphthong
directly attested in Ižma. The comparison with earlier
and later French monophthongizations (article 527–528,
as recollated by Kavitskaya) motivates the strong
claim about an existing length contrast. A dialect in
which coda weakening operated but monophthongization
did not can retain the complex nucleus. Neither the
weakening contexts nor the second-stage outcome can
be inferred from a long vowel alone.

Kavitskaya (2017: 41–58, [especially her discussion of
Komi and Old French](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.495438))
separates coalescence after full consonantal vocalization
from lengthening based on perceptual reinterpretation
of an extended vocalic transition before loss. The
latter needs no phonologized glide intermediate. Her
discussion of the chronology of French length and the
typological exceptions makes antecedent contrast a
testable tendency, not a universal prerequisite.
Old English already had length contrasts independently
of the ‘hay’ problem, so the strong universal, even
if accepted, could only license length here; it cannot
choose among these histories.

**Source boundary.** Jones (20–21, 57) attributes the
segment-substitution account to de Chene (1979), and
uses ‘rain’ and his other Table 2.7 comparisons for its
Old English application. His citation of dissertation
p. 66 is attached to the Latin comparison, not to an
independently proven Old English intermediate. The
2014 Routledge preview reproduces the 1985 Garland
edition. Its preface (xiii) confirms that chapters 1–3
preserve the June 1979 UCLA dissertation circulated by
the Indiana University Linguistics Club in December,
apart from a stated alteration on p. 207; chapters 4–5
are Garland additions. The available preview does not
reach p. 66. The Cambridge abstract and indexed JSTOR
extracts are accessible, but the complete journal scan
and the relevant dissertation pages were not obtained.
Consequently the English application is reported through
Jones rather than attributed to an unverified passage
of either original.

**Research verdict:** compare direct syllabic contraction
with (i) fricative or approximant vocalization yielding
a separately recorded complex nucleus and (ii) an
optional second-stage monophthongization. Keep direct
source-sensitive assimilation and literal consonant loss
as separate counterfactuals. A complex nucleus formed
directly from hiatus may feed the same second-stage
operation if its structure and conditioning match; it
does not attest the consonant-weakening stage of the
1979 analysis. A retained coda after diphthongization
is evidence for a different pathway, not an intermediate
of weakening to an empty coda.

## J5. Can Aldred’s marks discriminate vowel quantity?

Jones (30–31; p. 30 image checked, PDF 44) distinguishes
an acute from doubling of the **same vowel graph**.
His ⟨gast/gaast⟩ series includes both marked and
unmarked long-vowel forms; ⟨stan/stán⟩ occurs 31:5,
although comparative evidence establishes historical
length. His ‘sheep’ versus ‘ship’ comparison has the
advantage of matching the written consonantal frame
and vowel graph, while ‘ship’ has no accent in his
sample. It is not a matched *e* plus final palatal
contrast; his acute/doubling before homorganic clusters
is also a separate environment. On p. 56 he treats
⟨wég⟩ as positive evidence of lengthening in ‘way’.
It therefore cannot be assumed to be a measured short
control in Aldred’s pronunciation merely because ‘way’
begins with a historically short vowel.

| Comparison | Evidential weight |
|---|---|
| ⟨heg/heig⟩ | No acute is printed on the three established Aldred ‘hay’ loci. ⟨ei⟩ is not a doubled ⟨ee⟩, and is exactly the form whose vocalic structure is disputed. Three sparse, cross-verse tokens, all in double glosses, cannot establish a marking rate. |
| ⟨weg/wég/we⟩ | Same scribe and close phonological environment if ⟨g⟩ represents a final palatal. The positive ⟨wég⟩ motivates Jones’s lengthening interpretation locally. His Table 2.29 gives forms, not complete counts or loci, and ⟨we⟩ lacks the written coda. |
| Comparatively long monosyllables | Jones’s ⟨tid⟩, ⟨lar⟩, ⟨ric⟩, ⟨stan⟩ and ‘sheep’ show that marking is possible but variable in closed forms. They do not measure the missing denominator for independent long *e* in the same palatal and gloss context. |

Jones reports the Ross–Stanley index with discrepant
years (1962 at 30, 1956 at 55 and in the bibliography);
the catalogue identifies Book II of the commentary to
the 1956–60 facsimile. The actual index was not acquired,
so no complete accent/doubling counts for a tightly
matched final-palatal series can be claimed. Hulme’s
original 1896 discussion (cols. 19–22, searchable
[volume scan](https://archive.org/details/modernlanguageno11johnuoft))
argues that no manuscript marks all its long vowels
and that marking varies across copies. Clemoes’s 1952
study and Ker (1957: xxxv ff.) supply the manuscript
background as cited by Jones; their relevant pages
were not directly collated here. Ker’s descriptive
account of the acute as a stroke is independently
reported by Mokrowiecki (2015); it identifies the mark,
not the phonetic quantity of an unmarked form.

Ritt (2000: 498–501, 505–510) changes the question:
if an acute is most useful where length is not
otherwise predictable, the **distribution** of marks
may reflect writers’ expectations about quantity,
not a blanket marking rule. His Matthew census has
426 accented occurrences and explains 328 of 376
non-ambiguous ones under his model. These are counts
of **accented** forms across environments, not a
denominator of independent long and short final
palatal forms. They do not disprove length in unmarked
‘hay’. The full figures, limitations, and Farman’s
separate practice are already recorded in the
[Lindisfarne dossier](../../LINDISFARNE_EVIDENCE.md).

**Research verdict:** positive marking matters locally;
its absence is not evidence of equivalent force.
Jones’s short reconstruction for ⟨heg⟩ is compatible
with this comparison, but he does not derive it from
matched accent or vowel-doubling rates. A genuine
positive test requires a complete, comparable set of
historically long and short final-palatal monosyllables
with the relevant loci, marked and unmarked, and
allowance for double-gloss context. No new Farman
calibration was needed or performed.

tradition; it is not another Aldred example.

Ross and Stanley’s Book II is identified by the
[catalogued commentary](https://catalogue.nli.ie/Record/vtls000444010/TOC),
but a readable copy of the index was not obtained.
The independent cross-check is Cook (1894), s.v.
*heg*, plus Kemble–Hardwick, Skeat and now the primary
folio images. **Research verdict:** the primary record
improves the textual basis of Jones’s comparison and
confirms the dotted cancellation, but neither lack of
an acute nor ⟨ei⟩ determines vowel length or shows
how the palatal originated.

## J7. Short and long in Jones’s two comparisons

The contrast is neither an OCR accident nor adequately
explained by assigning every example to a different
period. Jones 21 and 55–56 compares late Northumbrian
spellings; at 149 he recalls them with [hej]/[heij].
The West Saxon base column of Table 3.17 (150) instead
gives conventional long [heej]/[hiij], against which
he presents twelfth- to fifteenth-century spellings.
These are different source series used for different
arguments, so the two entries do not literally assign
contradictory durations to the same written token.
But the difference is not a fully worked-out dialect
history either: at 152 Jones uses [hej] > [heij] >
[hei] as an illustration of trigger effacement in
the **Middle English** discussion, without reconciling
the short illustrative input with his long West Saxon
base. It is an alternative mechanism in that passage,
not new independent short-vowel evidence or an explicit
chronological ordering of the regional histories.

Jones cites Jordan (1974: 120–123) for the comparison
in Table 3.17. Jordan’s work is the translation and
revision of the German grammar by Eugene Joseph Crook,
published by Mouton; the [edition record](https://archive.org/details/handbookofmiddle0000jord)
is verified, but a readable copy of those pages was
not obtained from the archive or publisher. It is safe
to attribute the **Middle English comparison** to
Jordan **as cited by Jones**. It is not safe to attribute
Jones’s exact phonetic transcriptions, regional
provenance for each spelling, or his short and long
contrast to Jordan without the pages.

The conventional long form is independently documented
as a **scholarly reconstruction**: Campbell §§120.2,
580.4 derives *hēg/hīg* from a long diphthongal history,
assigns the attested direct stem to oblique influence,
and reconstructs an undisturbed direct *hewi*. Dahl
(95–97) also obtains length, but from a different
prehistoric nucleus and history of the exposed palatal;
the shared quantity is not independent manuscript
evidence. Minkova (2014: 178–179) uses the conventional
length of *hēġ* and historically short *weġ* to
illustrate the later merger, which removes their
agreement as a diagnostic of earlier quantity.

**Research verdict:** Jones’s short form is a
historically relevant published hypothesis about the
Northumbrian material, supported by his interpretation
of a now-verified graphic alternation. His juxtaposition
of two analyses leaves open real regional differences,
but supplies no date or independent measurement for
one, and no prehistoric \*he.i derivation. His long
reference forms document a received reconstruction,
while the later spellings cannot distinguish it from
the short history once the contrast is neutralized.

## J8. What syllabicity shifting does not establish

Jones 68–73, §2.4.5, starts with a **single complex
nucleus**. In the proposed Middle Kentish interpretation
of *hyald/hyeald/hieald* (Table 2.36), peak prominence
shifts to its second component, and the original first
component becomes a prevocalic glide. His Old Icelandic
comparison (70–71, Table 2.37, ‘sea’ among the examples)
likewise concerns an original first element losing
syllabicity and forming or joining an onset. The historical
notation [j] used for a glide in this discussion should
not be translated as the fricative in §§2.3.6 and 3.4.2.
At 71–73 Jones explicitly doubts a blanket interpretation
of Kentish ⟨ie/ye⟩ in the *eo* series: the letter following
the supposed glide is often not ⟨o⟩, some examples lack
an ancestral diphthong, and both monophthongal spelling
and onset insertion are alternatives. Onset insertion
adds a segment; shifting makes an existing vocalic
element less syllabic.

The proposed ‘hay’ input \*he.i has **two syllabic
peaks**, the second unstressed. An offglide resolution
makes the second element part of the preceding nucleus,
*hei̯*, leaving no separate coda consonant. A consonantal
resolution instead gives *hej* with a newly formed coda;
if a vowel follows at another stage, that consonant
could onset the next syllable. Neither mechanism is
the same as an onset produced by shifting the peak
rightward in an already diphthongal nucleus. Both preserve
the palatal quality of *i* without creating a stop or
fricative directly. A fricative realization requires separate
evidence or a separately modelled development.

[Mosionek and Zembrzuski (2019: 5–23, §§2–6)](https://doi.org/10.7311/0860-5734.28.2.01)
distinguish diphthongization, gliding, glide insertion,
and contraction, and explicitly consider the allowed
diphthong templates and weight. Their *nerian/lōcian*
comparison has a following vowel and supplies an
onset diagnostic, not a match for a new coda. The
case of *feohes* attributed to Opalińska involves
*h*-loss and a separate length/absorption history;
it does not establish contraction for short post-*w*-loss
*e.i*. The Opalińska dissertation (2002: 170–187,
207), 2004 article (235–252), and 2006 chapter
(111–132) remain indirect here. The located 2004
record and Kobayashi’s discussion of its *h*-loss
examples do not substitute for checking the author's
full text; see the previous [source assessment](ADJUDICATION.md).

**Research verdict:** the sources motivate a
testable change of syllabicity, not its occurrence
at a specific historical edge. At the same mid/high
hiatus input, compare retention, a complex nucleus
and coda formation at absolute edge, before a
consonant and before an independently syllabic
vowel; distinguish length and stress without
pooling phonotactically different contexts. A result
requiring a consonant must fail on an otherwise
identical vocalic offglide, and an onset-driven
analysis must not be credited with explaining a
coda by the same evidence. Neither Jones’s comparisons
nor the modern analyses demonstrate a division
between final gliding and pre-dental contraction.

**Historical assessment and verdict.** The historical
phenomena concern potentially distinct West Germanic,
pre-OE, recorded OE and Middle English developments.
Only adjacent unstressed *i/ī* as a trigger for *w*-loss
has the stronger independent controls described in D3.
Hiatus must be created before it can resolve in that
derivation (stage-entailed); no independent ordering of
final versus preconsonantal resolution, subsequent

the joint experiments. Positive evidence for transfer
is regionally asymmetric: West Saxon high vocalism
motivates protected material if apocope was early, but
there is no new reason to infer whole-stem levelling
for the earliest Anglian mid vowel. **DEFER adoption**:
retain the isolated candidates and negative controls,
no registry verdict or propagation to production.

## Comparative adjudication

The earlier literature and the two principal authors
do not all address the same arrow. Sievers’s accent
suggestion, Kögel’s labial-to-palatal conversion, van
Helten’s regional gemination, Chadwick’s directionally
different stem transfers, and Bülbring’s and Luick’s
w-less alternatives concern the origin of the input
and distribution across cells (D1). Dahl supplies a
specific **prehistoric** competitor: maintain the
nuclear diphthong, expose a nonsyllabic palatal at
ending loss, treat exposed and protected palatals
differently, and allow retention or restoration.
Ringe and Taylor (2014: 53, 173, 246, 250, 259–261)
instead separate ungeminated survivals from the
gemination/resolution history and document comparisons
for regular *w*-loss. Their preferred vocalism and the
direction of levelling are additional historical
judgements, not necessary consequences of a Foma
endpoint. Hogg (1992: §5.133) and Hogg and Fulk
(2011: 252) constrain the loss comparisons and the
distinction between the inherited glide and a palatal
fricative. Jones and de Chene address **what can
happen after a palatal or glide is in the rhyme**.
They cannot supply the prehistoric input assumed by
a candidate CAPR route.

The composite history \*hewi > he.i > hej is
historically defensible as a **conditional regular
account**. The first arrow has independent evidence
for its phonological mechanism; the second has
independently motivated types of hiatus resolution,
but no securely matched early word-final short *e.i*
control that positively establishes a consonantal
rather than nuclear result. No uniform regional
sound law is inferred.

| Witness | What the composite history explains, and what it still needs |
|---|---|
| Ninth-century Kentish *hei weg* | A short vowel plus palatal or a complex nucleus can give the spelling; an inherited long/diphthongal first member is also compatible. First-member position blocks Dahl’s absolute-edge deletion in the assay, and the witness does not by itself establish the free simplex. Dahl prints 859; Lowe cautions about the date. |
| Ninth-century Vespasian ⟨heg⟩ | A retained short mid vowel with a palatal accounts for the written mid vowel and final consonant without levelling. The spelling leaves length open. Direct short survival is therefore viable, not demonstrated; neither the long contraction-and-restoration history nor protected transfer is excluded. |
| Tenth-century Aldred ⟨heg/heig⟩ | Now verified in manuscript images as alternatives in the same lexical gloss tradition. Short [eʝ]/[ei̯ʝ], an approximant history, and long or nuclear variants are not measured by the written alternatives. A history through /j/ needs separate evidence if Jones’s [ʝ] is to be taken literally. |
| Farman ⟨hóeg⟩ | Independent local evidence favours length. Unchanged short survival is not the best account of this witness by itself. A subsequent lengthening with retained/restored palatal or protected transfer is possible, but neither can be projected backwards or assumed just to save the model. |
| Apparent contraction before consonants | Short-root length in ‘call’ provides a positive nonfinal comparison. Direct contraction versus glide/offglide then assimilation is unresolved; ‘strew’ is mixed, ‘flock’ has an admission/ending problem and ‘couch’ is etymologically conditional. The locally successful overlays do not constitute a distinct stop- or coronal-conditioned law. |
| Independently long plus palatal | *Cǣġ* shows that inherited *j* can survive after a long nucleus. It is not a reflex of short *e.i* and cannot decide its resolution. Likewise, the West Saxon high vowel under early apocope provides positive evidence for a different source of vocalism, not for generalized Anglian analogy. |

### Sixteen answers

1. **Dahl’s new information:** he supplies a fully
   specified alternative to syllabic *hewi*, separating
   differential exposure, quantitative deletion and
   morphological restoration. His source inventory also
   gives useful tests of weak compound members and
   distinct continental predictions.
2. **Jones’s new information:** he explicitly permits
   short mid vocalism for Aldred and distinguishes
   retention, vocalization, assimilation, and breaking
   before a retained fricative. That is not a derivation
   of the palatal from prehistoric syllabic *i*.
3. **Rejection of *hewi*:** not independently sustainable.
   The proposed diphthongal chronology is coherent but
   not demonstrated for the English direct form. Regular
   *w*-loss makes the alternative more credible if *hewi*
   was present; OHG *hewi* does not prove that premise.
4. **Final loss after long vowels:** not independently
   established as a general law. *Cǣġ* is a positive
   long-vowel countercontrol. Restoration there would
   need independent confirmation, not an exemption.
5. **Short-vowel survival under Dahl’s condition:** the
   condition permits survival if a short palatal-bearing
   form already exists. This is a relative prediction, not
   a positive witness for the origin of short /hej/.
6. **‘District’:** no decisive discrimination. Weak
   stress, lack of a secure simplex and uncertainty over
   palatal-bearing donors make it a test of the premises,
   not a proof of a parallel history.
7. **Compound *hei weg*:** materially different. It
   is protected against absolute-edge loss in D5’s
   diagnostic and may retain material absent in a
   simplex. Its written vowel does not decide the
   earlier quantity or establish deletion elsewhere.
8. **Jones’s short argument:** identifiable and
   phonologically explicit: a single first vowel in
   the interpretation of a plain/digraph alternation
   before a surviving fricative. It is not derived
   from matched accent/doubling counts or an
   independent measurement of those tokens.
9. **Preconsonantal assimilation:** a useful precedent.
   ‘Rain’ and ‘thane’ motivate comparison with lengthened
   forms, but ‘maiden’ and breaking variants exclude
   treating a following dental as sufficient without
   independent controls on dialect, stage and segment.
10. **De Chene’s contribution:** it makes a two-stage
    weakening-plus-monophthongization analysis a serious
    comparator and predicts a possible retained offglide.
    It does not demonstrate the intermediate in ‘call’
    or ‘hay’, and its strong prior-contrast claim is not
    taken as a universal.
11. **Direct stop/coronal contraction:** there is positive
    evidence for resolution yielding length in some
    nonfinal cases, not independent evidence for a direct
    mechanism confined to stops or generalized to all
    coronals. The present ‘call’ fricative already defeats
    the stop-only class as a unified account.
12. **Absolute versus compound-internal position:**
    Dahl loss and the D5 explicit domain arms distinguish
    them; the H6 edge arm distinguishes positions only
    as supplied to it. An isolated first-member suffix
    treated as a whole word cannot demonstrate compound
    protection. H1/H2 do not impose that split.
13. **Indistinguishable endpoints:** syllabic and atomic
    hiatus representations can both render `hei`;
    written ⟨heg/heig⟩ does not uniquely separate
    palatal quality and vowel quantity. Direct contraction
    and early glide loss can both yield `ġecēþ`;
    a protected long vowel and a contracted then
    restored long vowel can yield the same long
    palatal-bearing form. Continental copying and
    gemination may converge on the same labial spelling.
14. **Useful discriminators:** a matched early final
    short hiatus witness, independent vowel quantity,
    a direct/protected/compound set in the same
    tradition, secure ancestral consonant and aperture
    in a retained/lost preconsonantal pair, and evidence
    ordering continental labial outcomes relative to
    ending loss. Keep *cǣġ* independent of the target.
15. **Cascade deficiencies exposed:** unsupported
    proto admissions for some comparisons; a prefixed
    root susceptible to a weak-vowel deletion proxy;
    no short *e.i* treatment in the frozen contraction;
    and surface convergence of distinct nuclear
    representations. Regional spelling, morphological
    levelling, and unmodelled compound structure are
    not automatically defects in the West Saxon
    production target.
16. **Positive case for regular survival:** improved but
    not settled. An independent regular *w*-loss
    mechanism, an explicit published short Northumbrian
    comparison verified in the primary manuscript, and
    an independently plausible form of desyllabification
    together make regular survival more than an attempt
    to fit ⟨heg⟩. The phonetic outcome and scope remain
    unproved, particularly for Farman and for the
    West Saxon high-vowel witness.

For review, the priority is an independently localized
final/continuing-stem comparison, not another suite of
endpoints optimized on ‘hay’. A matched Aldred quantity
comparison requires the actual Ross–Stanley index or a
diplomatic census with both marked and unmarked
denominators; the three primary-image readings are
