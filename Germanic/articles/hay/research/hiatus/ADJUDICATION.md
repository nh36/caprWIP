# First experimental cycle: hiatus after labial loss

## Identity and question

Research-only overlay of the canonical, A and B2 Old English cascades,
not a new SC registration. Branch `research/hay-article`; intake
`4fc313d19a52e5b6471afcb664b41bb797d70e98`; exact adoption source and
runner `113101ac5f169b589e1a9647cdf24d32cdd4d919`. This asks whether
short stressed *e* followed by syllabic *i* regularly resolves
differently at the word edge and before consonants. An independently
derived non-dental contraction would refute a dental-only formulation;
a reliably identified final short input with contraction would
refute categorical final preservation. A retained intermediate *j*
in the same Anglian development would discriminate glide-plus-loss
from direct contraction. Model endpoints cannot supply any of these
historical premises.

## Current state and diagnosis

[SC054](../../../../docs/sound_changes/audits/sc054-adjudication.md)
already recognizes noninitial *w*-loss before unstressed *i/ī*, after
umlaut and before general syncope on the source argument. SC063 is
quantitative high-vowel loss; SC079 is heavy-context *j*-loss, and
SC086 contains several explicit contractions but no short *e.i*
operation. The exact executable conditions and placements, distinct
from those historical claims, are in [§3](README.md#3-stage-local-diagnosis).
Neither the baseline witnesses nor the archived catalogue convert
CAPR's selected inputs or outputs into independent reconstructions.
In particular, A's direct ‘hay’ produces hiatus, its protected
obliques are predictions, and the canonical/B2 outcome is a high-vowel
comparison for West Saxon, not a reconstruction of Vespasian quantity.

## Rechecked literature and historical scope

**Post-labial controls.** Ringe and Taylor (2014: 258–260, 317–318),
Campbell (1959: 100–101, 166–167, §§237.1b, 406) and Luick
(1914–1940: 219–220, §243) support the post-umlaut loss and the
subsequent unlike-vowel resolution in ‘call’ and ‘flock’. Ringe
and Taylor allow a delay in unlike-vowel contraction, with identical
hiatus possibly resolving earlier. Campbell prints mostly palatal-
retaining equivalents outside his cited Vespasian set; Luick considers
paradigm remodelling from a *j*-stem in some verbs. Neither the
attested length nor the tooth consonant by itself proves an intervening
glide. Sievers–Brunner (1965: 108–115, §§127–130) separates older
labial histories, later *h*-loss, and renewed attachment of endings;
its wider diphthongal controls are not instances of the same *e.i*.

**The nasal comparator.** Ringe and Taylor (260) derive *strēn*
from the ‘spread’ stem plus *-in-*, with umlaut and *w*-loss
giving *streinu*. Campbell §237.1b also places Vespasian *strēn*
with the post-labial contractions. Sweet (1885: 608 and text of
Pss 6:7, 40:4, 62:7, 131:3) indexes **feminine** *stren*; ⟨strene⟩
at 6:7 and 40:4 glosses accusative *stratum*, while bare ⟨stren⟩ at
131:3 glosses genitive *stratus*. The frozen `couch-dir` is a
reconstructed direct-cell test, not a token attestation for a bare
nominative or accusative. These forms establish the Anglian stem with
*n*, not measured vowel length or a directly attested intermediate.
The short precontraction nucleus is motivated by the weak-verb
etymology, not by the overlay; if that etymology stands, a stops-only
or dental-obstruent-only rule needs another account for the noun.
The Old Saxon, Old High German and North Germanic ‘strew’ forms
support the verb history, not independently the noun's precise
formation. The early gloss identifications remain qualified by
Pheifer §59.2 n. 1; Beowulf 2436 requires separate metrical treatment.

**Inherited palatal loss.** Campbell (§§240, 243–245, 467, 489;
pp. 104–106, 187–188, 197–198) treats loss with lengthening of
palatal *g* before *þ, d, n* as characteristic of West Saxon,
established by early West Saxon, but not exclusive to it.
Corpus Glossary *ongēn* and later regional variants, plus
sporadic *l*-cases such as Epinal *snēl* and Epinal/Corpus
*strēl*, rule out dismissing all regional evidence as late West
Saxon. They do not establish an exceptionless all-coronal law
for one early Anglian variety. The retained forms in the same
lexical families, and the coexistence of different stems, require
regional, chronological and phonetic controls. Hogg §7.15
(2011: 252) distinguishes inherited /j/ from the palatal voiced
fricative historically available from velars, while qualifying
their possible distinctness and merger. The spellings and the
lengthening tradition do not locate the loss on one side of that
merger. Thus the loss process is possible early enough in some
varieties, but its application **after an independently evidenced
gliding of syllabic *i* in these Anglian forms** is not demonstrated.

**River and prior length.** Ringe and Taylor (317–318) explicitly
allow Anglian \*eahi > ehi and corresponding West Saxon \*iehi with
inherited root-noun inflection, or production of the obliques
directly from already contracted nominative *ēa*. The model's
short *e* in the local probe is therefore conditional; the spellings
do not establish its quantity, and the source of hiatus would be
different from ‘hay’ even on the inherited analysis. Hogg §§3.19,
5.131–133, 5.146–149 (2011: 58, 172–175, 180–183) distinguishes
the early short-plus-*u* diphthongal template, front-vowel absorption
and residual Anglian hiatus. His assumption of automatic prior
lengthening of the syllable-final stressed vowel in the later
hiatus discussion has broad scope, but the bulk of his examples
involve *h*-loss; it is not direct quantity evidence for *w*-loss.
[Goering (2026: 298–318)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1360674325000164)
reassesses **postconsonantal** *h*-loss: archaic scansions can
preserve earlier syllable weight without phonological lengthening.
That undermines automatic deduction of length from those scansions,
not lengthening after intervocalic *w*-loss. Do not equate the
two environments or infer the opposite rule from his result.

**Modern resolution models.** [Mosionek and Zembrzuski (2019:
5–23)](https://doi.org/10.7311/0860-5734.28.2.01), §§2–6,
distinguish incorporation into an admissible diphthong template,
high-vowel gliding, glide insertion under cluster/weight constraints,
and contraction when alternatives are blocked. Their different
inputs make the ranking a source of tests, not a verdict on this
hiatus: test whether *ej* is retained before a consonant, whether
the consonantal representation survives before a vowel, and
whether a long nucleus feeds loss rather than retaining both
components. Their *nerian/lōcian* contrast shows why a following
vowel and the weight and segmental context should not be pooled
with absolute final *e.i*. The account of *feohes* attributed to
Opalińska involves *h*-loss, length and vowel absorption, not the
new short post-labial sequence. The original dissertation (2002:
especially 170–187, 207), [2004 article, pp. 235–252](https://doi.org/10.1515/flih.2004.25.1-2.235),
and 2006 chapter (111–132, especially 117) were identified; their
full text was not accessible in the holdings or publisher material
consulted. The comparison with her proposals here is explicitly
through the 2019 discussion, not firsthand verification.

The portion historically at stake is early Old English after
umlaut, especially Mercian and Northumbrian attestations, with
West Saxon and Kentish controls kept separate. The independently
argued ordering is umlaut > *w*-loss > general syncope
(Ringe and Taylor 260; transfer of ‘ewe’ and the light syllable
of ‘prepare’). The creation of hiatus must precede its resolution
in that derivation. No source establishes the narrower H3/H4
placement, the H5 order, or a new chronology edge across dialects.
The executable foot proxy and the continuation of an early candidate
past HVD are not historical stress or date evidence. No new positive
evidence for analogical transfer in Anglian ‘hay’ was found; protected
vocalism remains the independently motivated West Saxon comparison
conditional on early apocope, as in the earlier dossier.

## Twelve adjudication answers

1. **What does the cascade do?** A ‘hay’ ends with two vowel tokens
   and spells *hei*; canonical/B2 give protected-type *hīeġ*. A/B2
   produce *ġeciþ, ġecid, streid* from the short-root comparison.
   The ‘call’ and ‘strew’ environments canonical creates instead
   contain long *ēa*. See the trace and admissions ledger in §2.
2. **What is missing?** The baseline lacks the independently
   motivated nonfinal short-root contraction in ‘call’, does not
   show modelled *w*-loss plus incorporation in back-vowel
   controls, and does not derive the cited short-root ‘flock’ and
   ‘couch’ histories. The latter two are blocked at admission, not
   falsified. Retained *g* outcomes do not cover the historical
   loss variants. *Strēdun* deserves testing apart from the verse.
3. **Which stages matter?** Canonical long diphthong formation
   prevents the short input; A/B2 create it at *w*-loss, then
   `OEUnstressedEIContraction` deletes the prefixed root vowel in
   ‘call’. `OEContraction` has no short *e.i* clause. Lowering
   and HVD interact with the ending and with candidate prior
   length, and rendering conceals internal distinctions.
4. **Do nonfinal cases form a class?** *d, þ, n* form a dental
   subset, not a uniformly stop or uniformly obstruent set if
   the couch etymology stands. These witnesses do not by
   themselves establish that all coronals, or all consonants,
   condition contraction.
5. **Is final vs. preconsonantal productive?** It is a useful,
   falsifiable contrast: mixed outcomes account for ‘call’ better
   than universal gliding and retain a final component for ‘hay’
   unlike universal contraction. The panels provide no second
   selected post-labial short *e.i* lexeme, and no secure
   independent final counterpart establishes productivity.
6. **Are dental stops enough?** No, as a unified account of
   ‘call’ before *þ* plus ‘flock’ and ‘strew’ before *d*. They
   also exclude the conditional nasal case. Non-dental stops
   added by broad H3 have no positive witness here.
7. **Do broader coronals help?** They admit ‘couch’ in the
   local trial and go beyond dental obstruents. Neither the
   full comparison nor the captured corpus shows additional
   support for *s, l, r*; testing exactly *d/þ/n* against those
   exclusions remains necessary.
8. **Can contraction go through a glide?** In the experiment,
   early H4 reaches the same ‘call’ and ‘strew’ endpoints as H3
   with *ej* visible in between. Historical compatibility is
   conditional on the independent loss environment and timing;
   endpoint agreement is not a trace of historical *j*.
9. **Is the loss chronology defensible?** Early loss in some
   varieties is defensible, not the specifically needed
   post-gliding ordering in the same early Mercian history.
   Late H4 hits inherited clusters that H3 excludes and misses
   the prefixed root when its guard is lost. That last result
   is an encoding limitation, not chronology evidence.
10. **Can a long vowel plus inherited glide be regular?** H5
    glide-first demonstrates executable compatibility. Prior
    length followed by gliding fails at the edge under this HVD
    ordering; reversing the order exposes unlicensed lengthening
    of pre-existing *ej*. Neither arm supplies independent
    evidence for regular long mid *ēġ*; protected inheritance
    and contraction plus secondary restoration remain distinct
    from these candidate paths.
11. **Which predictions differ?** Final *ē* vs. *ej* vs.
    *ēj* vs. syllabic *e.i*; stop vs. fricative vs. nasal
    coverage; isolated vowel vs. post-vocalic *j* after the
    first nonfinal operation; catching inherited *g* after
    merger; and loss vs. retention of the second vowel across
    HVD. H3 and early H4 at *d/þ/n* cannot be separated by their
    final endpoints; independent retention or a chronological
    interaction is needed.
12. **What evidence could separate them?** A firm nasal
    etymology and ending for *strēn*, matched *d* and *þ* cells
    of early ‘call’, and an independently supported early
    *e.i* before a non-dental or at the edge. For the latter,
    river and Northumbrian ‘tree’ must pass the inflection and
    vowel-quantity checks below before counting. For H4,
    independently dated retained/lost palatals from the same
    variety are more useful than new fitted endpoints.

## Verdict and propagation

**DEFER adopting any overlay.** Retain the adopted source and
published manuscript unchanged. Prioritize direct, conditioned
contraction as the source-motivated nonfinal comparator, not a
universal sound law; retain early glide-plus-loss as a discriminable
competitor and short final gliding as viable, not established.
Unrestricted early H1 removes the final palatal; H2 cannot explain
the nonfinal contraction controls alone. The broader corpus did not
rank the promising early arms, but it revealed why late inherited
palatal loss must not be used as automatic corroboration. West Saxon
high vocalism and the earlier limits on Vespasian quantity stand.

No registry row, chronology edge, production test, corpus target,
benchmark, fingerprint, certificate, article or PDF changes. There
is no `Registry-verdict` line for an unregistered experiment.
Positive/negative comparisons and reproduction commands belong to
the isolated research runner and README. The unresolved issues are
the historical onset of nonfinal resolution, independent inherited
final controls, and a defensible stress representation for prefixed
roots, not an article-revision authorization.

Success would be independent evidence discriminating at least one of
the current class, timing or input alternatives. A confirmed narrow
regular contrast would strengthen the direct-cell account of Anglian
‘hay’ without fixing its vowel quantity automatically. A securely
counterexample-bearing panel would instead narrow the reconstruction's
scope or raise the importance of independent evidence for protected
stem transfer. For CAPR the highest-value near-term result is a clean
separation of root stress, weak-vowel reduction and genuine hiatus
resolution. Do not implement this next cycle during closeout.
