# The positive case for an inherited short ‘hay’

## What the early form can tell us

The strongest argument begins with the **cell**, not the letters. In Vespasian Ps 103:14, Sweet (1885: 335) prints ⟨heg⟩ glossing *faenum* in the accusative singular; elsewhere in this tradition the uninflected form recurs in direct uses. Independently of ‘hay’, the same edition gives Vespasian ⟨weg⟩ in both nominative and accusative (Ps 17:31, 33; Sweet 207). Its short historical *e* and secondary palatal show that a short mid vowel with a final palatal fits the orthography and grammar of the actual witness. Vespasian ⟨deg⟩ (Ps 55:2; Sweet 263) offers a second short historical vowel, but its vowel began as *a*. Neither control derives its palatal from final syllabic *i*. In particular, they furnish positive comparability, not positive evidence that ⟨eg⟩ excludes a long vowel. The [existing control audit](../REGULAR_SURVIVAL.md) did not find a sufficiently matched, independently long opposite control. No redetermination of manuscript quantity follows from the count of spellings.

Early nasal apocope, independently motivated by continental singleton reflexes insofar as those are inherited, leaves the English direct form ungeminated. Fronting, umlaut and independently attested loss of *w* before syllabic *i* then give a coherent regular chain to short hiatus: *hawją > hawi > hæwi > hewi > he.i*. Compare Ringe & Taylor (2014: 44–47, 173, 258–261), Campbell (1959: §§237.1b, 579.4) and the [priority register](SCHOLARLY_PRIORITY.md). The independently supported operations and the early form’s proper paradigm cell are what make this more than a spelling conjecture. The proposed last step *he.i > hej* loses syllabicity rather than deleting the second segment's quality. It retains ordinary short *e* and requires no stem transfer. This is the most economical **direct consonantal** account **if** word-final hiatus resolved by that operation in this environment. It is not automatically preferable to every other kind of resolution.

## Why the last arrow remains conditional

The inherited labial comparisons, including ‘claw’, ‘knee’ and ‘tree’, support the survival of a second vowel’s quality on loss of syllabicity, but their back-vowel outcomes do not show preservation of a short mid vowel **plus an independent consonant**. The quantity disagreement for ‘claw’ also removes any right to treat it as an unqualified short-input parallel. ‘Call’ and the qualified ‘strew’ evidence supply closer *w*-loss comparisons, but not a free word-final *e.i*. The [corrected ‘flock’ entry](FLOCK_QUANTITY.md) has final long *ī*. Its old short-final sensitivity is an admissible counterfactual, not an actual comparison compelling early resolution. The new stress handoff shows that distinct executable windows are not needed just to recognize the prefixed root, not that the lexemes shared a historical window.

The [‘river’ check](RIVER_OBLIQUES.md) strengthens the adverse side of the argument: the Chronicle’s mid-vowel genitive ⟨e⟩ is syntactically secure, and Orosius has clear mutated obliques as well as demonstrably remodelled cells. If that genitive descends from inherited *ehi*, uniform final consonantal gliding makes the wrong prediction. The documented remodelling in *eas* does not settle the ancestry of *e*. Earlier resolution of *w*-generated hiatus might make the environments historically different, but the record has not established that ordering. Jones (1989: 149–150) independently takes a short mid vowel seriously in the late evidence, but his short transcriptions begin with a voiced **palatal fricative**. His added offglide is not the hypothesized birth of an approximant from *i*. His separate long transcriptions do not measure Vespasian. Conversely, inherited long *cǣġ* beside *cǣġe* (Hogg 1979: 105; Ringe & Taylor 235, 286) shows that long vocalism and inherited palatal retention are compatible; an analysis cannot simply stipulate that all such palatals disappeared.

| Historical explanation | What it explains without analogy, and what it still owes |
|---|---|
| Direct short vowel and consonantal desyllabification | Retains direct-cell ancestry and predicts a Vespasian-compatible short /ej/. Needs independently defensible final conditioning; inherited ‘river’ is adverse to uniform application. |
| Direct nuclear incorporation | Also preserves the source of the second vowel. Short and heavy nuclei are both executable without mistaking two vowels for one segment. A short complex nucleus does not itself predict a consonantal spelling or pronunciation, and a later reanalysis would need independent evidence. |
| Lengthening with palatal retention | Avoids the logical need for secondary restoration, given *cǣġ*; neither that comparator nor the long *hay* hypotheses establishes how a long nucleus arose in this exact direct input. |
| Contraction followed by secondary palatal supply | Nonfinal contractions make loss of hiatus plausible. They do not prove final contraction here or the actual transfer of a consonant without its donor vowel. A bare *hē* stage and a historically accessible donor remain independent obligations. |
| Protected stem substitution under early apocope | Has a plausible donor and a positive motivation in West Saxon high vocalism, which the unchanged mid-vowel routes do not explain. This does not show transfer of the Anglian vowel or independently settle the direction of levelling. |
| Late apocope or Dahl’s distinct reconstruction | Late apocope directly preserves a protected-type stem and is motivated independently by forms such as *bedd*. Dahl begins with a diphthong and an existing palatal, not short hiatus, and requires his own loss/restoration account. Neither is defeated merely because another history can print ⟨heg⟩. |

## Quantity and regional interpretation

The inference that early shortness costs fewer operations **conditional on the early order and consonantal outcome** is stronger than equating a written vowel with a measured one. The early documents do not independently choose short over long. In the Paris Psalter (101:4, 101:9), the dative verses allow either a resolved short lift, if the first syllable is light under the appropriate intervocalic parse, or a long stressed syllable with the ending in the dip. In a closed direct form a short vowel can still be heavy. That specific ambiguity is distinct from the Psalter’s broader limits as a quantitative witness (Fulk 1992: 410–414). Later shortening or merger before /j/ removes the contrast rather than proving earlier equality with ‘way’ (Minkova 2014: 178). See the [quantity dossier](../../QUANTITY_EVIDENCE.md) for the actual verse lines and conditions.

The early Mercian, Kentish, and later Northumbrian observations must not be pooled. Brooks & Kelly’s preferred ‘hay-way’ supports a possible **first-member** comparison, not direct ancestry or a free-word final structure. The probable same-hand ⟨heg⟩ before *f* and ⟨hei⟩ before *w* are not a minimal pair, and the ‘highway’ alternative stays open. Dahl prints 859; that does not resolve the charter’s dating layers. Aldred’s ⟨heg/heig⟩ and Jones’s Northumbrian interpretation establish neither Vespasian quantity nor Farman’s pronunciation. Farman’s acute favours length **in his own gloss** without choosing its source; the historically short controls are not measurements of his synchronic quantities. West Saxon high vocalism is positively different, not inferred from macron placement or Eadwine’s witness alone. See the [Canterbury proposal](../CANTERBURY_CHARTER_PROPOSAL.md) and [quantity controls](../ORTHOGRAPHIC_CONTROLS.tsv).

**Publication judgment.** A regular inherited direct route is available and positively motivated. Short /hej/ is a defensible Vespasian reconstruction under early apocope and a specific final development; the witness is **compatible**, not identified uniquely with that outcome. Conditional economy does not yield independent preference for vowel quantity, and the present evidence does not select consonantal desyllabification over nuclear incorporation. Removing the *necessity* of whole-stem transfer in Anglian ‘hay’ would remove one analogical cost from early apocope; it would not explain the English *bedd/nett/cynn* geminates or settle the inherited status of the continental singleton reflexes. The two evidential advances that would justify stronger wording are (1) a matched, historically independent quantity contrast within the Vespasian tradition and (2) a dated, independently reconstructible final short *e.i* control or comparably direct evidence for its resolution. Recovering the history of the actual mutated ‘river’ genitive would sharpen the second test. Neither another surface-fitting FST nor more attestations with the same ambiguous spelling can substitute.
