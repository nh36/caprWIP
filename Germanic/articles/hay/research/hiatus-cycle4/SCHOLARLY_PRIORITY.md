# P1a — Scholarly priority and the short ‘hay’ proposal

## Question and source standard

Did an earlier author derive the inherited, ungeminated direct singular of English ‘hay’ through labial loss and final desyllabification, yielding a short mid vowel plus a consonantal palatal without levelling? The relevant test is the complete historical conjunction, not an earlier *hewi*, a short transcription, or a mechanism proposed for a different nucleus or position.

The register names the earliest **verified in this search** or an explicit secondary attribution where the original is not checked. It does not claim exhaustive priority. The [Dahl–Jones source dossier](../hiatus/DAHL_JONES.md), [literature matrix](../../LITERATURE_MATRIX.md), and [Q2 entry audit](SOURCE_ENTRY_AUDIT.md) supply the preceding original-edition checks and distinguish exact locators from secondary reports. ## What is and is not new for ‘hay’

Chadwick (1899:138–139) accounts for *heg* through protected forms; his ungeminated direct stem and his analogy are not the proposed conjunction. Campbell (§579.4) explicitly supplies undisturbed *hewi*, but attributes the attested direct form to the influence of inflected forms. In Dahl’s account of Girvan (1931:§§25f., 267 n. 2; Dahl 96, 101), *ewi* can yield long *ē* in the discussion of Northumbrian forms; restoring a labial or assimilating the ending does not yield short /hej/. Luick §§98, 243, 252 separates protected cluster behavior, contraction and palatal vocalization; the latter is not evidence for the reverse direction from final syllabic *i*. Dahl himself begins with a diphthong and an existing consonantal palatal and derives long vocalism. Jones (149) supplies a short late Old English [heǰ], framed through Northumbrian variation, but already assumes a palatal **fricative**. His [heǰ] > [heiǰ] analysis runs from consonant to nuclear complexity, rather than from final hiatus to a new consonant. Ringe & Taylor (173, 258–261) supply the ungeminated comparison and the independent phonology of labial loss without proposing the last arrow for Vespasian.

The exact-string and equivalent-term search used `hewi`, `heg`, `heig`, `hawi`, `*hawja`, ‘short’, ‘glide’, ‘desyllabification’, and the older scholars’ names. The indexed results included [etymological summaries](https://www.etymonline.com/word/hay) listing forms and [Versloot’s historical reconstruction study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/english-language-and-linguistics/article/reconstructing-the-historical-phonology-of-old-english/27089BC71DA789290187BAA8ACE9C526) on the wider phonology; neither supplied the complete claim. This negative search is not proof of originality. The exact chain already appears **within CAPR** in the [first-cycle composite assessment](../hiatus/DAHL_JONES.md#sixteen-answers); the current cycle should not claim to have originated it.

**RETAIN** independent support for the initial loss step and the published precedents for short vowel interpretation and possible desyllabification. **DEFER** credit for a new demonstrated historical reconstruction or sound law: the specific final consonantal outcome and its identification with Vespasian remain the claims requiring evidence. The bounded contribution is the combination of premises into a testable direct-cell hypothesis, matched comparisons with competing histories, and a source-audited distinction between what those tests can and cannot establish. No production verdict, article model or corpus source changes.
