# Ball & Stiles (1983): argument, evidence and scholarly prose

**Third comparative model of historical-linguistic argumentation for CAPR**

## 1. Bibliographical identification and scope

Ball, C. J. E. & Patrick Stiles. 1983. The derivation of Old English *geolu* ‘yellow’, and the relative chronology of smoothing and back-mutation. *Anglia* 101, 5–28.

Source: the stored 24-page PDF, `Ball and Stiles (1983) - The Derivation of Old English geolu and the Relative Chronology of Smoothing and Back-Mutation.pdf`. PDF page 1 corresponds to printed p. 5. All printed pages 5–28 were read; the main argument occupies pp. 5–21, the etymological appendix pp. 22–24, and the references pp. 25–28. Images of pp. 18, 20 and 24 were inspected to verify the important mass-noun passage, summary table and comparative argument. Other readings derive from the supplied text extraction, which occasionally corrupts special characters. The analysis normalizes unambiguous forms and uses conventional `>` for historical development. It does not independently collate the medieval manuscripts or the authors' cited editions.

This is a particularly useful third paper after Laker (2007) and Versloot (2026). Laker dismantles arguments against a shared chronology; Versloot compares two historical systems over a corpus. Ball and Stiles start with a single apparently anomalous lexical form, establish a general distribution from a corpus, solve the anomaly through a phonological and morphological argument, and return to the general chronological question. The scale and method are unusually close to the article on ‘hay’.

An additional reason for choosing it is substantive rather than stylistic: §4.3, p. 18, explicitly considers the effects of mass-noun semantics and relative stem frequency on the **direction of paradigmatic analogy**. It offers a published precedent for a question central to the hay investigation. This is a precedent for the reasoning, not proof that ‘hay’ followed the same history.

## 2. Central thesis and hierarchy of the argument

### 2.1 The historical problem

Anglian Old English ordinarily lacks back-mutated diphthongs before velars. Two explanations are possible:

- Back-mutation operated in this environment and was subsequently undone by smoothing. This is Campbell's chronological account.
- Smoothing preceded back-mutation; ordinary Anglian back-mutation was restricted so that it did not operate in the relevant velar environment. Luick had entertained restriction of back-mutation but did not settle the chronology.

The competing accounts predict much the same late Anglian forms. The issue is whether the early textual evidence establishes which process was already operative when the other began.

A conspicuous difficulty is *geolu* ‘yellow’. The Leiden Riddle's manuscript form *goelu*, conventionally emended to *geolu*, looks like unusually early evidence of back-mutation. The same lexeme occurs among the early glosses. If all of these diphthongs are indeed back-mutation products, the chronological distribution is less clear. The authors argue instead that *geolu* may have acquired its diphthong by analogy with regularly broken oblique stems in *-lw-*.

### 2.2 The whole argument as a dependency structure

```text
Problem: ordinary Anglian back-mutation products are absent before velars
|
+-- H1: back-mutation precedes smoothing; smoothing removes the evidence
|   +-- Campbell's sporadic surviving <ea> spellings
|   +-- possibility of spelling retention after phonological change
|
+-- H2: smoothing precedes back-mutation; restricted back-mutation
    +-- Early textual survey: §§3.1–3.3
    |   +-- smoothing alone, or smoothing and back-mutation
    |   +-- no securely diagnostic contrary sequence
    |   +-- audit apparent exceptions
    |       +-- corrupt or ambiguous glosses
    |       +-- bearug: comparative etymology (appendix)
    |       +-- scea-: palatal diphthongization alternative (§5)
    |       +-- geolu: residual lexical problem (§4)
    |
    +-- Historical explanation of geolu
    |   +-- West Saxon gefu and gelu predict a parallel development
    |   +-- observed outcomes diverge
    |   +-- inherited wa-stem paradigm supplies -lw- in protected forms
    |   +-- breaking of e before -lw- supplies geolw-
    |   +-- generalization of broken stem supplies geolu
    |   +-- independent comparanda: gearu, meal, fat, related derivatives
    |   +-- different frequency of stem forms can explain different levelling
    |
    +-- Consequences
        +-- geolu does not establish early back-mutation
        +-- scea- no longer needs to be grouped with geolu
        +-- smoothing-before-back-mutation preferred
        +-- local Mercian exceptions to velar restriction recognized
        +-- linguistic stages of early texts can be arranged by the changes
```

This is my reconstruction of the argumentative dependencies, not a diagram printed by Ball and Stiles. The dependency is important: the authors do not derive the general chronology solely from the etymology of *geolu*. They first establish the distribution in the early witnesses, then remove the principal obstacle to interpreting it. If their account of *geolu* fails, the other evidence survives but the chronology becomes less secure. If their account of *bearug* fails, a separate early-back-mutation candidate re-enters the argument. The structure is therefore partly cumulative and partly dependent on exclusion of counterexamples.

### 2.3 The levels of conclusion

The paper makes several claims of different scope:

1. A relative chronology: smoothing was earlier than ordinary back-mutation in the Anglian evidence.
2. A statement of the conditioning of back-mutation: it generally did not operate before velars in Anglian.
3. A reanalysis of selected early spellings in *scea-* as palatal diphthongization of restored *a*.
4. A proposed sound change: breaking of short *e* before *lw*.
5. A morphological explanation of *geolu*, together with a possible explanation of stem distributions in ‘meal’ and ‘fat’.
6. A relative ordering of the linguistic stages represented in early texts.

The first, second and sixth claims are not identical. The chronological priority of smoothing alone does not logically entail restriction of back-mutation before velars: the observed later lack of diphthongs and the absence of a further general smoothing process are additional premises. The authors recognize regional exceptions in §6.1. Likewise, ordering texts by the changes cannot then be used as independent evidence for that order.

## 3. The competing historical models

### 3.1 Campbell's analysis in its strongest form

The authors give Campbell's argument in §2.2, pp. 6–7, and concede its initial theoretical plausibility. An illustrative derivation for ‘days’ is (their example, simplified notation):

| Stage | Campbell's chronology |
|---|---|
| Restoration of *a* | *\*dagas* |
| Second fronting | *\*dægas* |
| Back-mutation | *\*deagas* |
| Smoothing | *dægas* |

Sporadic surviving *ea* spellings would then be remnants of a previously widespread phonological stage. They could even enter texts from varieties in which the spelling did not reflect the local phonology. This gives a reason to expect occasional diphthong spellings alongside the usual smoothed outcome.

The authors' response is not that the series cannot produce the result. It plainly can. They ask whether the predicted intermediate stage has the right historical distribution. That is a crucial methodological distinction for CAPR: a derivation's internal success and its historical adequacy are separate questions.

### 3.2 The alternative chronology

The alternative places smoothing before back-mutation. The early stages of the textual record should accordingly include smoothing with little or no evidence of back-mutation. Later texts should show both, but ordinary Anglian back-mutation should not produce diphthongs before velars.

| Diagnostic environment | Earlier smoothing | Earlier back-mutation |
|---|---|---|
| Early smoothing environment | Smoothed or partly smoothed spellings possible | Smoothed spellings imply later point in the proposed sequence |
| Independent early back-mutation environment | Unmutated forms expected before back-mutation | Mutated forms expected if spellings reflect the change |
| Later ordinary back-mutation environment | Mutated spellings possible | Mutated spellings possible |
| Later velar environment | Unmutated because the environment is excluded | Monophthong from back-mutation followed by smoothing |

The last row is observationally equivalent on ordinary late spellings. Consequently, it is the dated early forms in the first two rows that are potentially discriminating. The table is an analytical reconstruction, not the authors' own display.

The residual problem is not resolved merely by counting the later monophthongs. The early examples alleged to attest back-mutation have to be examined individually to establish whether they really belong to the relevant historical class.

| 17–18, §4.3 | Test and extend proposed change | *geolwe*, *geolwian*, ‘meal’, ‘fat’, ‘tar’; direction of levelling |

The organizing principle is a progression from the particular to the general, back to the particular, and finally to the general. The opening form is not a decorative illustration. It is the obstacle that the remainder of the paper is designed to resolve.

The section on *bearug* is moved out of the main narrative because its detailed comparative etymology is necessary for one premise but would interrupt the central investigation. This is a good example of a scholarly appendix that does genuine inferential work.

The identifiers below are assigned for the purposes of this analysis. Status refers to the strength of the proposition on the supplied article's evidence, not to its truth in the light of all subsequent scholarship.

### Critical dependency paths

- **Chronological argument:** P04 + P05 + adjudication of exceptional cases (P06–P08, P13, P17) support P18. This is an abductive historical argument, not a deductive theorem from missing spellings.
- **Lexical argument:** P10 + P11 + P12 support P13 as a motivated explanation. Neither morphology nor the attested broken stem by itself establishes the direction of levelling.
- **Mass-noun argument:** P15 + semantics support P16. P16 does not follow from the mere absence of plural tokens.
- **Dating argument:** P18 licenses P20, conditional on adequate environments. P20 cannot be recycled as independent evidence for P18.

The argument model is deliberately more explicit than the published prose. It records assumptions whose acceptance is largely tacit in the original. That is exactly the distinction we need between a scholarly write-up and the internal control on its reasoning.

## 6. The central philological operation: removing apparent exceptions

### 6.1 Establishing the distribution before adjudicating the exception

Table one (§3.1, pp. 7–9) collects examples of smoothing and back-mutation in early Anglian manuscript texts, inscriptions and charters, with references and selected dating information. It is a diagnostic table rather than a catalogue of everything known about each document. The survey claims fairly extensive coverage before c. 850, excluding coins. The authors explicitly acknowledge that some texts have no relevant environment for either process.

This is a good example of the difference between absence of an observation and an observed absence. A text containing no suitable back-mutation environment cannot demonstrate that back-mutation had not occurred. A text with suitable environments and systematically unmutated forms can contribute to the chronological argument, subject to its spelling practices.

The paper recognizes an additional complication: Ruthwell has back-mutation evidence but lacks smoothing environments. In footnote 4 (pp. 9–10) the authors examine independent linguistic dating criteria, concentrating on unstressed vowels rather than using back-mutation itself as an independent chronological diagnostic. This is an exemplary precaution against circular argument.

### 6.2 The glossaries and the problem of textual dependence

Épinal, Erfurt and Corpus are treated as related documents, not independent samples of the same language at three points in time. The archetype is linguistically heterogeneous and later copies can preserve or modernize earlier forms (§3.2.1). The authors nevertheless identify a trend: Épinal has numerous unsmoothed spellings and little secure back-mutation; Erfurt has more smoothing; Corpus shows general smoothing and widespread back-mutation (§§3.2.2–4).

The inference is historical, not simply palaeographical. A ninth-century manuscript copied from an earlier exemplar can preserve an earlier linguistic stage. The Leiden Glossary is accordingly considered in a separate remark: its copyists and spelling conventions make some of the diagnostic vowel sequences difficult to interpret.

The authors take each of the apparently mutated Épinal forms in turn. *hreacca* is lexically and textually doubtful. Several *scea-* spellings have an alternative phonological explanation. *bearug* has an alternative comparative reconstruction. A corrupted gloss involving *olor* cannot straightforwardly establish the change. This leaves *geolu* as the salient residual problem.

The transferable method is not to eliminate inconvenient forms automatically. It is to ask what each alleged counterexample actually establishes. The conclusion rests on the quality and independence of the resulting adjudications.

### 6.3 The graphic alternative

On p. 14 the authors consider an alternative interpretation in which early back-mutation existed but was not written, while smoothing remained variably recorded. They object that scribes who wrote short breaking diphthongs apparently had the graphic resources needed to record back-mutation too.

This is a positive argument against a specific spelling hypothesis, not a general declaration that scribes could never conceal a phonological change. The competing view would require an explanation of why one class of short diphthongs was systematically not represented while another was.

For ‘hay’, the analogous problem is whether a proposed spelling of /e.i/ or /ej/ has independent orthographic motivation. The comparison should concern actual scribal distributions, not merely graph–sound possibilities in the abstract.

## 7. The explanation of geolu: phonology and paradigm structure

### 7.1 The matched comparison with ‘gift’

The strongest point in the article is the opening of §4.2 (p. 15). If *gelu* and *gefu* are parallel inputs to West Saxon palatal diphthongization and back-mutation, why is ‘yellow’ *geolu* and ‘gift’ *giefu*, with later high-vowel variants?

The comparison identifies a hidden difference. The reconstructed forms appear similar when only the direct form is considered. But ‘yellow’ is a *wa*-stem adjective; ‘gift’ is not. The protected adjectival forms contain the cluster *lw*, which supplies a distinct breaking environment.

The central proposed derivation may be summarized as follows (schematic, not a complete cascade):

| Historical operation | ‘Yellow’ direct form | Protected forms |
|---|---|---|
| Earlier paradigm | *\*gelu* | *\*gelw-* |
| Breaking before *lw* | no breaking here | *\*geolw-* |
| Generalization of the broken stem | *\*geolu* | *\*geolw-* |
| Alternative without early generalization | *gelu*, subsequently subject to dialectal vowel changes | broken stem persists |

The authors propose that West Saxon would otherwise have developed direct *\*gielu* by palatal diphthongization. The mixed paradigm *\*gielu* ~ *\*geolw-* was, they argue, regularized prehistorically. They tentatively place the regularization before i-mutation. The early Anglian *gelu* spellings reflect forms without analogical introduction of the breaking diphthong; those forms can undergo later back-mutation independently.

The crucial point is that **the same written form geolu can arise by more than one historical route**. An early occurrence can result from analogy with a regularly broken stem, and a later occurrence can result from back-mutation of an unlevelled direct form. Accordingly, the form alone does not date back-mutation.

### 7.2 Is breaking before lw adequately motivated?

The authors do not introduce the environment merely to accommodate a problematic adjective. They reason that *lw* combines a lateral with a consonant independently associated with breaking or retraction; they compare the accepted development in *rw* stems, particularly *gearu* ~ *gearwes*. They also adduce related derivatives *geolwe* and *geolwian*, whose clusters supply the environment directly, and the noun ‘meal’ with *melu* ~ *melwes* and *meolu* ~ *meolwes* (§§4.2–3).

This is a proposed refinement of the sound laws, not simply the rearrangement of already familiar rules. It gains support from the conjunction of phonetic conditioning, inherited morphology and independently relevant lexical variation.

There are limits. The derivatives belong to the same lexical family and are potentially susceptible to analogical influence. The rule is represented in few lexemes. The strength of the argument rests heavily on the comparison with ‘meal’ and ‘fat’, and on the structural case for the new environment. The authors themselves note that comparable *ilw* sequences appear to be lacking (p. 17, n. 16).

### 7.3 The mass-noun argument: immediate relevance to ‘hay’

The remark on p. 18 deserves separate treatment. It contrasts ‘yellow’, whose monophthongal forms are restricted to the early direct singular, with ‘meal, flour’, whose monophthongal and diphthongal forms persist across the paradigm. The authors attribute the difference to the semantics of the latter: an uncountable *singulare tantum*.

Their reasoning is that a frequently used direct singular *\*melu* may have supplied monophthongal variants of the protected stem *\*melw-* before back-mutation. The later regular back-mutation of the direct form produces *meolu*. Reciprocal influence of the variants then yields the double paradigm. They extend the same reasoning to *sme(o)ru* ‘fat’, another uncountable noun, whose inflected *smer(u)w-* forms are attested. The footnote on ‘tar’ qualifies the generalization: its distribution resembles ‘yellow’ more closely, and a reported genitive plural might indicate a different semantic and grammatical profile. The authors acknowledge possible accidents of transmission.

The reasoning can be modelled as follows:

```text
Inherited stem alternation
   +-- regular direct stem without breaking
   +-- regular protected stem with breaking
       |
       +-- adjective with numerous inflectional contexts
       |   generalization of broken stem is plausible
       |
       +-- mass noun with frequent direct singular
           generalization of unbroken stem into protected contexts is plausible
           subsequent back-mutation and interaction produce doublets
```

This is not a statistical demonstration of prehistoric frequency. The semantic classification and attested distribution motivate an inference about the relative frequency of the competing stems. The authors' confidence in the causal explanation exceeds what can be independently measured from the evidence. But the historical proposal is explicit, comparative, and testable against actual inflected forms.

For our article, this is stronger than merely invoking a general typological expectation that frequent forms resist analogy. Ball and Stiles offer an Old English case involving glide-stem morphology, direct/protected alternation, mass-noun semantics and possible direct-to-oblique levelling. It should be cited with the exact page and assessed on its own merits. Its relevance is morphological, not proof of the specific phonological development *\*he.i* > *\*hej*. The stem classes also differ: *wa*-stems are not neuter *ja*-stems.

## 8. The comparative appendix: why bearug matters

The appendix is an example of classical philological argumentation in concentrated form. Its purpose is to assess the inherited structure of ‘barrow-pig’. If the unusual *bearug* preserves inherited medial *u*, it can serve as early evidence for back-mutation. If *u* is anaptyctic, a breaking diphthong supplies the more economical interpretation.

The argument proceeds in a strict sequence:

1. **Internal Old English evidence (p. 22):** four *bearug* attestations belong to one glossary tradition and derive from a single original gloss. Elsewhere the word lacks the medial vowel. The older Leiden glossary tradition has the form without *u*. Inherited medial *u* would require an explanation of the widespread syncope; a secondary vowel explains the isolated variants.
2. **Frisian (p. 22):** a cited Old Frisian form cannot be found in the dictionaries checked by the authors; modern Frisian forms provide evidence for the consonantal root structure but not inherited medial *u*.
3. **Old Saxon (p. 23):** a single *barug* gloss looks independent until its textual context is investigated. Many glosses in the same group are related to the English glossary tradition. Orthographic Germanization of an English exemplar is a serious possibility.
4. **Old High German (p. 23):** variation in the presence and quality of the medial vowel, including a parallel example with independently known absence of an inherited vowel, supports anaptyxis as a possible explanation.
5. **Lex Salica (p. 23):** difficult spellings do not require the inherited medial vowel.
6. **Old Norse (pp. 23–24):** the attested forms do not require *\*barugaz*. The authors argue that this input would predict an unsyncopated nominative, using adjectives with the relevant structure as controls. A dictionary comparison that appeared to support the proposed syncope is challenged as a reconstructed ghost nominative based on inflected forms.
7. **Reconstruction (p. 24):** a root *\*barg-* plus a labial element can account for Norse mutation without medial *u*; the authors leave the exact stem formation open between alternatives and tentatively suggest a deeper ablaut paradigm.

The strongest operation is the separation of a comparative form from a genuinely independent comparative witness. An Old Saxon gloss derived from an English exemplar cannot simply be counted as confirmation of the English reconstruction. Nor can an editorially reconstructed Old Norse headword count as evidence for its own historical derivation.

The appendix does not need to settle the deeper Indo-European reconstruction to establish its immediate conclusion. It needs only to show that the supposed inherited *u* is not required and that the English form is not reliable proof of early back-mutation. The alternative reconstruction is stronger than a bare assertion of uncertainty because it has independent comparative motivation.

## 9. Evidence architecture and standards of proof

| Evidence type | Function in the paper | Principal limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Manuscript reading versus emendation | Establish the actual problem at Leiden *goelu* | Editorial correction not identical to observed pronunciation |
| Early texts in relevant environments | Test relative chronology | Absence of diagnostic contexts; manuscript/exemplar differences |
| Parallel glosses | Separate innovation from retention and corruption | Related copies are not independent language samples |
| Comparative phonological controls | Motivate *lw* breaking and reject *barugaz* | Small sets; historical reconstruction conditional |
| Inflected forms | Establish protected environment and stem variation | Late or rare forms cannot directly date prehistoric analogy |
| Mass-noun semantics | Motivate differential direction of levelling | Historical frequencies not directly observed |
| Independent dating diagnostics | Constrain interpretation of Ruthwell | Linguistic changes can vary in rate across regions |
| Prior scholarship | Formulate rival models and establish known rules | Citation consensus does not establish historical fact |

An important property of the paper is that it assigns different jobs to different evidence. The comparative reconstruction of *bearug* is not evidence for the timing of smoothing on its own; it removes an alleged early instance of back-mutation. The morphology of ‘yellow’ motivates an alternative etymology; the overall distribution dates the two changes. The inference is strongest when each argument is used for the particular conclusion it supports.

## 10. Prose style and expository techniques

### 10.1 Economy of the opening

The paper begins immediately with the disputed form, the relevant manuscript and the alternative reading. It does not begin with a general account of historical linguistics, an abstract methodological declaration or a literature survey. Within the first paragraph the reader knows which word, which witnesses and which historical assumption are at stake. The transition to the general chronology in §2 is therefore motivated.

The paper is not short because it compresses the evidence. It is economical because almost every section performs an identifiable inferential task. Seventeen printed pages of main argument plus a three-page comparative appendix are justified by the depth of the textual and comparative investigation. The paper's length is a consequence of the number of substantive problems, not a page target.

### 10.2 How prior scholarship is incorporated

The authors introduce Campbell and Luick at the point where they provide the two competing analyses. They do not create a separate chronology of scholarly opinions for its own sake. Campbell's formulation receives a direct quotation and a formal derivation because his argument is consequential; minor differences among editions and earlier opinions are usually handled in parentheses or notes.

The authors are also willing to say which earlier scholar was right about a particular item without accepting that scholar's complete chronology. On *bearug* they recover the analysis of Chadwick, Bülbring, Jordan and others against the more recent handbooks. This is intellectual engagement rather than a catalogue of authorities.

### 10.3 Paragraphs are units of reasoning

A useful pattern occurs in §4.2, pp. 15–16:

- Identify the comparable inputs and discordant outcomes.
- Establish that the apparent phonological match is real in the relevant dialect.
- Identify an independent morphological difference.
- Derive a different protected stem from that difference.
- Explain how paradigmatic replacement would produce the anomalous direct form.
- Test the proposed conditioning against additional words.

Each step is recoverable from the prose. The argument does not need a numbered proposition list in the publication because the paragraphs naturally encode the sequence of reasoning.

The authors frequently use direct contrast: if a specified reconstruction were correct, a particular form ought to occur; the actual evidence has another form. They follow this with an explanation of the discrepancy. This is more informative than a paragraph stating that competing histories are possible or that the evidence is complicated.

### 10.4 Confidence, qualification and argumentative force

The authors are assertive. They identify an explanation as inadequate, state their preference, and summarize four explicit conclusions. Qualifications are local: the scope of a particular text, the nature of a proposed form, the uncertain provenance of a gloss, or a possible deeper reconstruction. The result is not a paper whose principal conclusion is the existence of uncertainty.

At the same time, some phrases are more categorical than a modern referee might accept. The claim that Campbell's theoretical argument is disproved is stronger than the textual survey strictly licenses; the evidence weighs against his proposed ordering conditional on reasonable assumptions about spelling and transmission. The claim on p. 18 that the morphological divergence is attributable to countability is plausible but not directly demonstrated. We should learn the clarity of their conclusions without copying their degree of certainty indiscriminately.

### 10.5 Tables, derivations, remarks and footnotes

There are two principal numbered tables. The first presents actual diagnostic material from shorter sources, the second summarizes three relative stages based on the two changes. Both are naturally tabular: corresponding environments and diagnostic outcomes can be compared across sources. The phonological derivation in §2.2 is a compact staged display, not a prose-heavy matrix of hypotheses.

Extended `REMARK` paragraphs handle matters important to the immediate point but not essential to the narrative progression. Footnotes supply bibliographical qualifications, textual details and subsidiary analyses. The appendix handles a self-contained etymological argument of sufficient length to deserve its own section.

This hierarchy of presentation is useful for our article. An attestation table and parallel derivational table help the reader. A table assigning every scholar an evidential grade generally does not. The authors' sustained discussion of a disputed etymology is prose, even where the corresponding internal research inventory would naturally be tabular.

## 11. Adversarial assessment

### 11.1 Is the relative chronology proved?

Not in the strict sense. The distribution of early smoothed forms without back-mutation strongly favours the proposed ordering if the relevant forms are genuine and the scribal record is reasonably informative. But geographical differences, heterogeneous exemplars and variable rates of change could weaken the inference. A chronology in which some back-mutation preceded completion of smoothing in particular varieties is not excluded simply by the aggregate distribution.

The authors acknowledge subdialectal exceptions, and their conclusions concern the ordinary Anglian development. A precise modern formulation would distinguish the onset of the changes, their completion and their application in different contexts. Smoothing and back-mutation could have partially overlapping periods of productivity.

### 11.2 Are the exceptional forms excluded on consistent grounds?

The strongest exclusions rest on independent philological evidence, notably the textual dependence of *bearug* and its comparative reconstruction. Other exclusions are more conjectural, particularly where corruption or another sound change is posited. The burden is to show that the rival analysis is motivated outside the need to maintain the chronology. One should also test whether the alternative chronology needs comparable amounts of special pleading.

The authors' treatment of *scea-* recognizes a competing analysis and the uncertainty of provenance. A useful extension would be a systematic comparison of all candidate environments for palatal diphthongization and second fronting, rather than assuming that the alternative is correct because early back-mutation has lost a prominent example.

### 11.3 Is the rule of breaking before lw independent of analogy?

The structural argument is good, but the lexical evidence is limited. Related words of the ‘yellow’ family are not necessarily independent. ‘Meal’ and ‘fat’ give more valuable comparisons, although their variants likewise require historical interpretation. A modern evaluation might search exhaustively for relevant *elw* sequences, including negative controls, and distinguish rule application from lexical restoration in the predicted outputs.

### 11.4 Is the frequency argument too strong?

The claim about mass nouns is a serious explanatory proposal, not directly measured historical frequency. In the case of ‘meal’, the observed persistence of monophthongal protected forms is evidence in need of explanation; mass-noun semantics offers a motivation for the proposed direction of analogy. Alternative explanations, including dialect mixture, different chronological histories or inflectional remodelling, deserve consideration.

For ‘hay’, it would be especially important not to transfer the conclusion wholesale. The Old English record of ‘hay’ contains far fewer overt oblique forms than the paradigms used for ‘meal’ and ‘fat’. Thus the analogy proposal may be well motivated as a possibility while remaining weakly testable for this particular noun.

### 11.5 How strong is the Norse argument in the appendix?

The use of positive controls for the expected nominative is particularly impressive. Nevertheless, the conclusion depends on reconstructing the correct morphological class and treating the compared suffixes as equivalent. The rejection of a particular *\*barugaz* reconstruction does not by itself decide between *\*barguz* and *\*bargwaz*. The authors explicitly preserve that narrower uncertainty. The appendix is persuasive precisely because its principal conclusion does not depend on the tentative deeper ablaut hypothesis.

### 11.6 Overall referee verdict

The paper makes a coherent historical contribution: a motivated reanalysis of *geolu* removes a major apparent exception to the distribution on which the authors base a revised relative chronology. The new *lw*-breaking proposal, the morphological explanation of the doublets, and the etymology of *bearug* are themselves contributions. Some claims about chronological proof and frequency are stronger than warranted, but the central historical question, the proposed explanation and the evidence for it are unmistakable.

This assessment is based on the paper itself. It is not a comprehensive adjudication of the subsequent forty years of research on breaking, smoothing and back-mutation. Versloot's later alternative chronology, for example, requires an independent comparison rather than being assimilated to Ball and Stiles's conclusion.

## 12. Lessons for the hay article and the collection of argument models

### 12.1 A directly relevant piece of historical scholarship

Ball and Stiles (1983: 18) should be investigated as a source for the historical morphology of ‘hay’. Their treatment of ‘meal’ and ‘fat’ supplies an explicit Old English precedent for the hypothesis that frequent direct singular forms of mass nouns can affect the direction of stem levelling. Their comparison is with a different stem class and a different sound change, so the proper use is as an independently motivated morphological comparator, not as proof of our desired outcome.

A possible argument in our article would proceed from the attested stem distributions of their mass nouns to the historically reconstructible stem alternation of ‘hay’. It would then ask whether our actual attestation census supports or fails to distinguish the proposed direction of levelling. No assertion about the prehistoric frequency of ‘hay’ should be based mechanically on the biblical glosses.

### 12.2 Reconstruct the article around the explanatory discrepancy

The closest parallel is the contrast between *geolu* and the result predicted if it were treated exactly like ‘gift’. For ‘hay’, introduce the divergent regular predictions of the two apocope chronologies, followed immediately by the operation that Ringe and Taylor posit to obtain the conventional form. The exact frozen traces should establish the stages; the published analyses should establish the scholars' actual commitments. Then ask whether the attested spellings require that particular analogical operation.

This is the problem to solve. Source criticism, quantity and morphology are components of its solution, not independent mini-essays to be summarized in a cautious conclusion.

### 12.3 Distinguish positive results from removal of an objection

Ball and Stiles's reanalysis of *geolu* does not directly date smoothing. It shows why the form cannot safely be used to date back-mutation. The chronological result comes from the conjunction of that reanalysis and the wider distribution.

Similarly, establishing that *heiweg* preferably contains ‘hay’ and that its written first element corresponds to the early-apocope output is a positive philological observation. Showing that an ordinary Anglian spelling does not establish long quantity removes an alleged obstacle. Establishing a regular historical connection between *\*he.i* and /hej/ would be an additional phonological result. These claims should not be collapsed into one conclusion. Nor should a failure to establish the last negate the first two.

### 12.4 Learn from the appendix

The *bearug* appendix illustrates how a long investigation of one lexeme can support a precise premise without dominating the whole paper. A similarly structured appendix could house extended charter or attestation documentation for ‘hay’, but the substantive lexical adjudication of *heiweg* belongs in the main argument. Do not make the reader consult project-internal files to evaluate the published claims.

### 12.5 Complementarity of the three model papers

| Paper | Characteristic intellectual operation | Best use for CAPR |
|---|---|---|
| Laker (2007) | Expose hidden chronological premises and construct a viable rival derivation | Test whether an allegedly obligatory historical ordering is actually required |
| Versloot (2026) | Compare explicit systems against a common corpus, with separate analysis of disagreement cases | Evaluate competing cascade predictions and scrutinize benchmark methodology |
| Ball & Stiles (1983) | Solve a lexical anomaly through source criticism, phonology and morphology, then revisit general chronology | Model the actual research and writing structure of the ‘hay’ article |

Of the three, Ball and Stiles is the closest model for a focused etymological article with implications for the ordering of sound changes. It also contributes a concrete new comparative lead. Its most useful feature is the way the historical interpretation of the exceptional word, the regular sound change and the direction of analogy are made mutually intelligible. The resulting chronological argument is neither a computational score comparison nor a catalogue of textual uncertainties.

### 12.6 A reusable workflow derived from this paper

1. Open with a specific problematic attestation or contrast and the established interpretation.
2. Set out the competing historical derivations, including any analogy, and identify their different predictions.
3. Establish the diagnostic distribution from the relevant textual sources.
4. Examine apparently contrary forms individually, keeping reading, lexical identification, textual independence and historical interpretation separate.
5. Explain the residual lexical problem with a motivated historical reconstruction; test the explanation against independently relevant words.
6. Reconstruct the paradigm wherever analogy is invoked. Identify donor, recipient, conditioning, scope and probable chronology; consider the opposite direction of levelling.
7. Return to the broader chronological question and distinguish what the lexical result proves from what objection it removes.
8. Present the argument as continuous scholarly prose, with tables for comparable data, derivational displays for sequences, footnotes for subsidiary matters and appendices for substantial supporting investigations.

The internal proposition model should precede the writing and remain available as a control on the published argument. The paper itself should not expose that administrative apparatus. Ball and Stiles offer a particularly good demonstration of how explicit historical reasoning can remain readable without becoming a report on a research workflow.
