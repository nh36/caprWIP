# Ritt (2000): argument, evidence and scholarly prose

**Fourth comparative model of historical-linguistic argumentation for CAPR**

## 1. Bibliographical identification and scope

Ritt, Nikolaus. 2000. Theory, empiry, and textual witnesses: acutes in the ‘Lindisfarne Gospel’ and Early English vowel quantity. *Sprachwissenschaft* 25(4), 497–512.

Source: `Ritt2000.pdf`, the interlibrary-loan scan in the stored literature. The first two PDF pages are administrative matter; PDF pages 3–18 correspond to printed pp. 497–512. I read the article from the page images because the embedded text layer contains essentially only the delivery watermark. The main argument occupies pp. 497–510, followed by references. I inspected a higher-resolution crop of p. 508 to verify the numerical inconsistency discussed below. All references in this analysis are to printed pages. This analysis does not independently collate Lindisfarne or Skeat's edition, reproduce Ritt's accent census, or verify Jones's book, Ritt's earlier monograph and the other publications that Ritt cites. Statements about those works are therefore reports of Ritt's treatment of them.

Ritt offers a fourth, distinct kind of historical argument. Laker (2007) challenges the necessity of an alleged chronological restriction; Versloot (2026) compares alternative historical systems against a corpus; Ball and Stiles (1983) resolve a lexical anomaly in order to establish a broader chronology. Ritt examines the *interpretation of the observations themselves*. He identifies an inferential dependency between a historical theory and the meaning attributed to a scribal practice, then constructs a competing interpretation of that practice. The historical theory that seemed to be contradicted by the record turns out not to be excluded by it.

For the ‘hay’ article this is particularly pertinent. Ritt deals with precisely the kind of acute accents we have been considering as evidence for vowel quantity. His study is of the Lindisfarne Matthew material, not Farman's Rushworth gloss; its relevance to Farman is methodological, not a license to assume identical scribal conventions. It also demonstrates why an independent argument model needs to distinguish observations from the rules by which we interpret them.

## 2. Central thesis and argumentative hierarchy

### 2.1 The problem

The Northumbrian gloss to the Lindisfarne Gospels is unusual in its relatively frequent acute accents. Many occur over vowels reconstructed as historically long; others occur in environments where a historically short vowel is thought to have lengthened in English. Jones (1989), as reported by Ritt, interprets the second class as evidence that homorganic lengthening (HL) had occurred by the time of the gloss. Potential inputs to open-syllable lengthening (OSL), in contrast, lack accents and are interpreted as still short. The gloss thus appears to supply an early date for HL and a later date for OSL.

Ritt's own earlier account, Quantity Adjustment (1994), unifies those phenomena and certain shortenings as statistically conditioned developments. A reliable demonstration that HL and OSL occurred at different periods would be a problem for the unified process as presented here. The argument of the paper is that the manuscript does not independently supply that demonstration: it is the combination of the manuscript and a particular interpretation of the absence of accents that yields the chronological conclusion.

The alternative interpretation is that accents mark exceptional or otherwise unpredictable length. If open syllables already tended to contain long vowels, their vowels would not normally need explicit marking. This would explain why even etymologically long vowels in graphically open syllables are relatively rarely accented.

### 2.2 The entire argument as a dependency structure

```text
Historical issue: one process of quantity adjustment or two dated lengthenings?
|
+-- Received account (§I)
|   +-- comparative reconstruction identifies historical vowel quantities
|   +-- accented short-etymon vowels occur before homorganic clusters
|   +-- prospective OSL inputs lack accents
|   +-- interpret accent as evidence of length
|   +-- interpret absence of accent in OSL inputs as evidence of shortness
|   `-- infer HL earlier than OSL; Lindisfarne appears to separate the events
|
+-- Competing historical account (§II)
|   +-- syllable weight and coda structure motivate a unified process
|   +-- statistically conditioned lengthening and shortening
|   `-- different dates would challenge this account
|
+-- Reinterpretation of the witness (§III)
|   +-- count accents in Lindisfarne Matthew
|   +-- compare graphical closed and open syllables
|   +-- note relative avoidance of open syllables even with inherited length
|   +-- propose that the diacritic marks otherwise unexpected length
|   +-- classify exceptional environments and ambiguous examples
|   +-- check a second sample of CVC items
|   `-- absence of an accent in an open syllable need not imply shortness
|
`-- Conclusions (§IV)
    +-- the manuscript does not exclude unified quantity adjustment
    +-- alternative interpretation is compatible with earlier OSL
    +-- prefer a historical account on additional grounds, not this witness alone
    `-- philological interpretation of textual data is theory-dependent
```

The crucial branch is the interpretation of an *unmarked* form. Both accounts can accommodate accents on long vowels. They differ in what they assume about long vowels that lack an accent, particularly in open syllables. Ritt does not need to disprove the association between acute and length to challenge Jones's chronological inference.

### 2.3 Three levels of claim

1. **Negative historical conclusion:** Jones's interpretation does not uniquely establish different dates for HL and OSL.
2. **Positive alternative explanation:** context-sensitive marking of exceptional length can account for substantial features of the accent distribution.
3. **Stronger historical preference:** the pattern can be recruited in support of a unified process of quantity adjustment.

The first is weaker and more robust than the second; the third requires still more evidence. Ritt's conclusion on p. 509 allows the competing histories to be decided on other grounds. His general methodological argument is that even apparently especially informative orthographies require interpretation.

The paper's own positive suggestion is not simply that manuscripts are unreliable. Rather, the value of a witness depends on the specificity of the model connecting its graphic forms to linguistic structure. That model can be questioned and tested.

## 3. Expository structure, section by section

| Section | Pages | Work done in the argument |
|---|---:|---|
| I. Established interpretation and implications | 497–502 | Introduces the manuscript, explains the conventional chronological argument, and isolates the assumptions that make it work. |
| II. Competing account of quantity changes | 502–505 | Introduces unified Quantity Adjustment and gives the strongest objection to it. Establishes why the apparent manuscript evidence matters. |
| III. Reconsidering the evidence | 505–509 | Presents an accent inventory, summarizes distribution, formulates an alternative marking principle and offers two quantitative checks. |
| IV. Conclusions | 509–510 | Distinguishes historical implications from the general problem of inference from textual witnesses. |

The structure is remarkably economical. The author does not begin by explaining every difficulty of Old English quantity or by announcing a general philosophical programme. He introduces a specific purportedly decisive witness; then he makes clear what would be at stake if its conventional interpretation were correct. Only after that does he return to the data.

### Section I: reconstruct the opponent's inference before objecting

The introduction moves from the general difficulty of recovering vowel quantity to unusually informative writing systems. The *Ormulum* supplies a familiar example; the Lindisfarne gloss supplies the less thoroughly studied case that the article will examine. The contrast is useful because Orm's systematic consonant doubling is not the same phenomenon as sporadic accentuation in the gloss.

On p. 498 Ritt separates examples such as *tíd*, *lár*, *ríc*, *scíp*, whose vowel length he attributes to inheritance, from examples such as *góld*, *sáld*, *lónd*, *blínd*, *dórn*, whose historically short vowels occur in environments traditionally associated with lengthening. He gives the traditional conditioning of HL and notes disagreements concerning which clusters belong to it (p. 499). This shows exactly which part of the linguistic theory is independently comparative and which part relies on the accent.

On p. 500 he introduces OSL and the absence of accents on possible inputs. The traditional reconstruction treats the potential lengthening vowels as short. Ritt then makes explicit the assumptions concerning distinct phonological environments and the temporal coherence of changes (pp. 500–501). These assumptions are part of the inferential bridge, not new observations from the manuscript.

The decisive move comes at p. 502: an account that appeared to be supported by the witness already informed the reading of the witness. Ritt does not deny the actual accent distribution; he asks whether the inference from its omissions is warranted.

### Section II: formulate the rival theory fairly

Ritt does not pretend that his earlier Quantity Adjustment account is unproblematic. He sets out its motivation from syllable structure, the possible equivalence of homorganic clusters and single consonants in weight, and a statistical generalization extending over lengthenings and shortenings (pp. 502–503).

The formalization on p. 503 makes the probability of lengthening (and inversely of shortening) depend on stress, vowel backness, coda sonority, vowel height, syllable weight and the weight of the weak syllables of the foot. The paper does not re-estimate the earlier model; it uses it to establish the theoretical alternatives whose evidential compatibility is in question.

The self-criticism on p. 504 is important. Ritt acknowledges that his earlier unified account was constructed from comparisons of reconstructed Old English and modern forms rather than an extensive examination of intermediate stages, dialects and texts. Descriptive economy does not itself establish historical truth. The implication is that if Lindisfarne genuinely contradicted the chronology required by his account, that objection would have to be taken seriously.

The transition into §III is consequently well motivated: the paper must determine whether the manuscript really contradicts the theory or only appears to do so when interpreted under its rival.

### Section III: return to the evidence

Rather than introduce a wholly different historical corpus, Ritt returns to the disputed witness. He collects the accents in Matthew, provides an alphabetical inventory (pp. 505–506), separates graphically closed from open environments, and proposes a new account of their distribution. The crucial observation is the scarcity of accents in graphical open syllables irrespective of whether the vowel is etymologically long or a candidate for lengthening.

The alternative model treats marking as informative when the graphic context would otherwise suggest shortness. The author then accounts for exceptional environments, excludes ambiguous cases and tests the model against a small CVC sample. The numerical assessment is discussed critically in §7 below.

### Section IV: separate the empirical conclusion from the wider methodological claim

Ritt first states the historical implication: the manuscript is compatible with the unified account, and the two proposed histories must be evaluated on further grounds (p. 509). He then considers the possibility that his own expectations influenced the alternative interpretation, just as Jones's prior expectations influenced the received interpretation.

This is an unusually explicit recognition that a successful reinterpretation need not itself be true. He generalizes the point to the relationship between texts and reconstructed linguistic competence (p. 510). The article is not a wholesale rejection of textual evidence, but an argument for scrutinizing the theoretical assumptions required to extract linguistic information from it.

## 4. The two competing evidential models

The most useful formal distinction in this article is not a sound-change cascade but a model of the relationship between a linguistic state and a graphic observation.

Let:

- `L` = the vowel is phonologically long in the relevant historical state;
- `A` = an acute appears over the vowel in the witness;
- `C` = the vowel occupies a graphically closed syllable;
- `O` = the vowel occupies a graphically open syllable;
- `H` = an independently reconstructed potential input to homorganic lengthening;
- `S` = an independently reconstructed potential input to open-syllable lengthening.

This is my formalization of the argument, not Ritt's notation. The graphic categories are deliberately not equated with phonological syllable boundaries.

### 4.1 The received reading

```text
Historical reconstruction: H and S vowels were originally short.
Observed: accented examples occur in H environments.
Interpretation: A favours L.
Inference: HL has occurred for those examples.

Observed: no accents on prospective S inputs.
Additional interpretation: absence of A in S favours absence of L.
Inference: OSL has not occurred.

Combined historical conclusion: HL precedes OSL.
```

The second branch requires more than the first. Even if `A` were a highly reliable indication of `L`, the inverse inference from absence of `A` to absence of `L` would require evidence about how frequently long vowels are marked in the relevant environment. An implication is not a biconditional.

### 4.2 Ritt's alternative reading

```text
Assume long quantity is already predictable in many O environments.
An acute primarily indicates length where the written environment
would otherwise suggest a short vowel.

C + L: accent more useful, hence more likely.
O + L: accent less useful, hence less likely.

Consequently, S + no accent is compatible with S + L.
The absence of acute marking in S need not date OSL after the gloss.
```

Ritt's hypothesis concerns a writer's distribution of graphic information, not the absence of real vowel-length contrasts. It is consistent with many accents being associated with historically long vowels. It instead rejects the inference that the failure to mark length is evidence that length is absent in environments where it could already be predictable.

The two readings have different predictions for independent inherited-long controls in open syllables. These are potentially more informative than prospective OSL inputs: if their length can be reconstructed independently, their marking rate can be examined without assuming the disputed historical process has or has not occurred.

### 4.3 What a discriminating test would require

| Independently assigned quantity | Graphically closed | Graphically open |
|---|---|---|
| Long | Count accented and unaccented tokens | Count accented and unaccented tokens |
| Short | Count accented and unaccented tokens | Count accented and unaccented tokens |
| Historical lengthening candidate | Keep separate from independently long controls | Keep separate from independently long controls |

The pertinent comparison is between rates of marking conditional on independently assigned quantity and graphic environment. The frequency of closed environments *among accented tokens* is a different quantity. Both are useful, but they answer different questions.

An ideal test would also control for recurrent lexemes, formulaic occurrences, grammatical forms, manuscript hands and corrections, and any independently diagnosed scribal conventions. It would assess a prediction on evidence not used to formulate it. This more explicit design is my extension of Ritt's procedure, not a claim about analyses he has already undertaken.

### Dependency audit

The crucial dependency is `R08 = R05 + R06 + R07` (with the historical chronology assumptions). Without R07, the chronology cannot be inferred merely from R05 and R06. R14–R15 provide a competing explanation for R06. R17 and R19 are intended to substantiate R14, but neither is required to establish the purely logical point that R04 does not entail R07.

This distinction matters in adversarial review. If the numerical check on p. 508 fails, the evidence for Ritt's positive alternative becomes weaker. The negative finding that nonmarking cannot automatically establish shortness survives. If a complete matched study demonstrated that open-syllable long vowels were marked regularly and that the relevant prospective OSL inputs were almost invariably unmarked, the received inference would be considerably strengthened. If it instead showed context-dependent marking of independently long vowels, Ritt's reinterpretation would gain independent support.

## 6. How the evidence is used

### 6.1 Comparative evidence supplies a baseline

The first set of examples uses comparative Germanic reconstruction to identify inherited quantity. This is not decorative etymological information. It supplies the independent baseline that allows the acute to be interpreted as possible marking of a change in quantity. The distinction between inherited long vowels and lengthening candidates is thus logically prior to the historical inference.

### 6.2 Orthography is not treated as a transparent phonetic transcription

The expressions 'graphically' open and closed do considerable work in §III. Ritt can count a graphic pattern before adopting a particular phonological interpretation. This is particularly important because the wider controversy involves the treatment of homorganic consonant groups in syllable structure. His terms do not silently presuppose that a string of two consonant letters always represents a heavy coda.

### 6.3 The same observations can be evidence against one interpretation and for another

The relatively low incidence of marking in open syllables might support absence of OSL if unmarked vowels are presumed short. But independently long open-syllable vowels apparently share this tendency. Their existence motivates the alternative interpretation. This is an instance of finding an independent control *inside the disputed source*, rather than requiring a new manuscript or assuming that another scribe worked identically.

### 6.4 The list is a source inventory, not an independent test

The alphabetical accent list on pp. 505–506 gives readers a substantial part of the evidence on which the summary rests. It shows recurrent forms, often with counts in parentheses. It does not supply a row-by-row manuscript locator, a complete census of unaccented forms or an explicit coding of every token. Reproducing the result would require returning to the edition and specifying the counting unit. The list is useful, but it does not make the subsequent percentage self-validating.

### 6.5 Ambiguous examples are excluded explicitly

Ritt identifies 35 accented open-syllable cases with related closed-syllable forms and 15 occurrences of the relevant forms of *gé* (pp. 506–508). His rationale is that analogical influence or prosodic reduction makes these difficult to interpret. He then excludes them from the main percentage. The decision is visible, which allows a reader to recalculate the result. But exclusion on the basis of explanations developed after seeing the data can favour a model; the excluded examples should not disappear from an overall assessment.

## 7. Independent audit of the numerical claims

This is the most important adversarial finding from the paper. The counts below are transcribed from the original printed pages; they have not been independently replicated from Lindisfarne.

### 7.1 The 426 accents

| Quantity | Count | Derivation |
|---|---:|---|
| Total accents | 426 | Reported, p. 506 |
| Graphic closed environments | 318 | Reported, p. 506 |
| Graphic open environments | 108 | 426 − 318 |
| Antepenultimate cases accounted for | 10 | p. 507 |
| Related-form ambiguities | 35 | pp. 506–507 |
| *gé* ambiguities | 15 | pp. 507–508 |
| Total excluded | 50 | 35 + 15 |
| Remaining accents | 376 | 426 − 50 |
| Accounted for | 328 | 318 + 10 |
| Exceptions | 48 | 376 − 328 |

The arithmetic of Table (7), p. 508, is coherent:

- 318/426 = 74.65% of the entire accented sample occurs in graphic closed environments.
- 328/376 = 87.23%, which rounds to 87.2%. The paper prints 87.3%, the sum of its separately rounded component percentages (84.6% + 2.7%). This minor rounding discrepancy is not the substantive numerical error discussed below.
- 48/376 = 12.77% are the retained exceptions.
- 328/426 = 77.00% of all observed accents are positively accounted for by the two stated categories; 50/426 = 11.74% were excluded as ambiguous.

The last percentage is not offered as an alternative estimate of predictive accuracy. It simply makes the exclusions visible. The 87% result is a conditional descriptive coverage figure, not a measured probability of correctly predicting whether a vowel will receive an accent. There are no unaccented tokens in its denominator.

The substantive problem is the reversal of conditioning. Ritt establishes a distribution resembling `P(C | A)`. To know whether closed environments favour marking compared to open ones, the relevant quantities are `P(A | C,L)` and `P(A | O,L)`, with independent evidence of L. The printed counts alone do not establish those rates. If nearly all eligible long vowels in the text happened to occur in closed environments, a concentration of accented tokens in those environments would be unsurprising even if the scribe did not use differential marking. The paper's qualitative remarks about inherited long vowels in open syllables help motivate its interpretation, but the printed aggregate cannot fully test it.

### 7.2 The second test: the first 100 CVC items

On p. 508 Ritt reports:

| Category | Printed number |
|---|---:|
| Items examined | 100 |
| Short-vowel items | 79 |
| Long-vowel items | 21 |
| Long-vowel items with accented variants | 15 |
| Stated remaining items | 8 |
| Pronouns presumed reduced | 6 |
| Stated exceptions | 2 |

The problem is straightforward: 21 − 15 = **6**, not 8. If 15 accented-variant items, six reduced pronouns and two exceptions are disjoint classes, they sum to 23, not 21. Adding them to the 79 short items gives 102, not 100.

The image of printed p. 508 clearly reads both 15 and eight; this is not an artefact of automated text extraction. On p. 509 Ritt reports 98% predictive adequacy. The reported numbers do not support a unique reconstruction of that figure. One could hypothesize a misprint of 15 for 13, which would reconcile 21 = 13 + 6 + 2 and yield 98 successes out of 100 under the author's categories. But there is no warrant for silently adopting that correction. The original list of 100 items and their classification must be recovered or the test repeated. No corrected accuracy should be cited as established.

There are additional design questions. Ritt describes a prediction about unaccented CVC items, but credits long items with accented *variants*. It is unclear whether the unit is a particular token, a surface form or a lexeme with attestations elsewhere. A long unaccented token and a long lexeme with at least one accented token are different observations. Counting the latter as a correct prediction may not test the former. The reduction of pronouns is plausible, but requires independent prosodic evidence in their actual contexts; it cannot automatically be invoked solely to rescue a prediction.

### 7.3 Does the numerical problem invalidate the paper?

No. It invalidates confident reliance on the claimed 98% verification and weakens the positive case for the proposed marking rule. The main logical objection to the conventional reading remains intact: the absence of an accent is not necessarily evidence of short quantity. A full matched analysis might ultimately favour either interpretation, or a more complex rule that includes lexical and prosodic factors.

This distinction mirrors our earlier assessment of Versloot. A quantitative calculation can be internally defective without destroying the separate philological or historical insights of an article. Equally, a good qualitative insight does not license repeating an erroneous performance figure.

## 8. Prose, paragraph structure and presentation

### 8.1 Rhetorical design

Ritt's prose is more essayistic and methodological than Ball and Stiles's. The article is organized around a reversal of an apparently decisive inference. Its natural progression is received account, difficulty for rival theory, reinterpretation of data, and consequences. The reader knows throughout why the examination matters. The paper's title announces both the case study and the general problem rather than promising a comprehensive account of all Old English vowel quantity.

The most useful structural technique is to make the opponent's argument initially appear strong. Section I does not begin with a declaration that the received view is circular. It explains why the accent distribution appeared to date the changes. Section II then gives that argument real consequences for the alternative account. The objection on p. 505 is therefore substantive, not a token qualification inserted after the preferred view.

### 8.2 Paragraph-level functions

- **Expository paragraph:** introduces a particular evidence type and a known interpretation (pp. 497–499).
- **Inferential paragraph:** connects the interpretation to historical chronology (pp. 499–501).
- **Analytical reversal:** identifies the theory-dependent premise that had been overlooked (p. 502).
- **Comparative-theoretical paragraph:** gives independent reasons why the rival theory is worth considering (pp. 502–504).
- **Concession:** states the empirical weakness of the author's own earlier work (p. 504).
- **Evidence paragraph:** presents a pattern in the actual accent distribution (p. 506).
- **Explanatory paragraph:** proposes a marking principle that accounts for that pattern (p. 507).
- **Test and conclusion:** derives a consequence of the new principle and examines it (pp. 508–509).

This sequence is a model for constructing individual paragraphs in a historical paper. Each paragraph either supplies an observation, explains an inference, introduces a genuine objection or develops a test. The general conclusion follows from the demonstrated ambiguity of the particular witness.

### 8.3 Qualification and conviction

Ritt gives the strongest objection to his alternative rather directly and explicitly acknowledges the potential influence of his expectations (p. 509). That does not require him to retract his central finding. It clarifies the appropriate level of historical conclusion: the evidence is compatible with the unified account, not proof of it. The qualification is placed after the positive reinterpretation has been developed, so that it does not prevent the reader from seeing what has actually been accomplished.

For our article this is preferable to repeatedly qualifying the same claim in advance. One should first make the strongest defensible argument, then identify its precise unresolved dependency. If the historical claim is that a conventional objection is not independently established, it is possible to state that firmly without asserting that the alternative history is true.

There are aspects not to imitate. Some of the rhetorical questions, self-conscious asides and reflections on Popper, theoretical fashion and historical-linguistic methodology (pp. 504, 509–510) are suited to Ritt's methodological purpose but would be distracting in a narrowly philological article about a single noun. The ‘hay’ article needs considerably less commentary on the nature of historical linguistics and more actual analysis of the historical developments.

### 8.4 Tables, examples and footnotes

Ritt uses one compact numerical summary table (7), where tabular presentation is genuinely appropriate. He uses numbered example sets and formalizations (1–5) and an inventory (6) to present comparable evidence, rather than putting his evaluation of competing scholars into tables. Footnotes carry bibliographical references and contextual remarks. The main text contains the inferential work.

The table's usefulness is not a guarantee of its interpretation: the printed categories and denominators make it possible to challenge the resulting claim. That is a desirable property of scholarly presentation. Our article should similarly publish the relevant counts, not merely assign a verbal evidential rating to Farman's accents.

## 9. Adversarial historical assessment

### 9.1 The best criticism of the received chronology

Even if acutes reliably indicate length when present, the absence of a mark cannot automatically indicate shortness. The conventional interpretation relies on a second proposition about the completeness or distribution of marking. Ritt offers a coherent alternative to that proposition. The force of the chronological argument must therefore be assessed from the distribution of marking among independently established long and short vowels, not solely from the presence of acute in one group and absence in another.

### 9.2 The best criticism of Ritt's alternative

The marking rule is inferred partly from the very distribution it is intended to explain. Its formulation that length is marked where unexpected is flexible enough to accommodate different prosodic or analogical exceptions. Without independent knowledge of what was predictable to the scribe, it risks becoming difficult to falsify. Other explanations of the distribution are possible: lexical conventions, prosodic prominence, variation among glosses, or properties of the source material and transmission. These possibilities are not demonstrated here; they show why the alternative is a hypothesis rather than a direct reading of the manuscript.

The distinction between graphemic and phonological closure is especially important. A graphical open syllable may not straightforwardly represent a phonologically light syllable in every case. The historically disputed processes themselves can change syllable structure. The proposed marking principle needs a defensible account of what feature of the writing or phonology the scribe was sensitive to.

### 9.3 Historical unification versus temporal identity

The possibility of describing multiple developments by a single statistical formula is not the same as demonstrating that they occurred simultaneously. A unified conditioning principle could operate with different rates, at different stages or in different domains. Conversely, two phonologically distinguishable changes could overlap in time. Ritt's framing assumes that the unified historical process is challenged by different chronologies; this is understandable in relation to his earlier account but should not be generalized to all possible unified analyses. The actual temporal predictions of Quantity Adjustment would need to be specified and tested independently.

### 9.4 Dating and independent controls

The gloss supplies an approximate temporal limit for forms interpreted as contemporary with the glossator. Neither the history of the underlying text nor the historical origin of a spelling is automatically identical to the date of the manuscript. This is not the principal problem in Ritt's argument, but it is a limitation of using one scribal practice to date the full geographical spread of a sound change. A similar chronological qualification is central to the Vespasian, Farman and Eadwine evidence in our article.

### 9.5 Referee verdict

The paper makes a substantive contribution by identifying a specific unsupported inference in an established chronological argument and proposing a testable alternative to it. Its broad methodological point follows from the case study. The positive interpretation of the accent system is plausible, not conclusively established. The reported 98% test is defective as printed and the first percentage lacks the necessary exposure denominators to be interpreted as general predictive accuracy. A modern empirical reassessment would need a token-level census, explicit coding of independent quantity controls, matched comparisons and a transparent account of uncertainty and exclusions.

The most defensible verdict is not that Ritt has proved early OSL or that Jones's chronology is false. It is that the Lindisfarne accent pattern, interpreted without the additional assumptions of the received account, does not itself establish that chronology.

## 10. Specific implications for the ‘hay’ article

### 10.1 Do not transfer an accentuation model from Aldred to Farman

Ritt studies the Northumbrian Lindisfarne gloss. Our two acute-marked *hóeg* forms belong to Farman's Mercian Rushworth Matthew. Different hands, scribal practices and textual histories are involved. Ritt supplies a plausible methodological alternative: selective marking can depend on the expected quantity of a vowel in a graphic environment. He does not establish that Farman uses the same marking principle.

The primary question for Farman remains the practice of Farman himself. The existing hay study's bounded matched controls are more directly relevant to that question. They should be reported independently of the Lindisfarne counts. Ritt is a reason to treat the interpretation of those controls carefully, not a reason to ignore them.

### 10.2 Distinguish positive and negative evidence

The two Farman acutes can positively favour length if independently short vowel-plus-glide controls are much less often marked. The absence of comparable acutes elsewhere cannot, without calibration of the corresponding scribal systems, establish short quantity. The interpretation of ⟨oe⟩ is another line of evidence, not an additional acute observation. Its phonological motivation must be examined separately.

In particular, the following are distinct:

- `P(accent | long vowel, given environment and scribe)`;
- `P(accent | short vowel, given environment and scribe)`;
- `P(long vowel | accent, given environment and scribe)`.

The first two can inform a comparison of competing interpretations; the third depends additionally on the prevalence of the quantity categories and the source of their classification. We should not derive any of them from the proportion of long vowels in an accented-word list without the appropriate denominator.

The current Farman calibration does not contain a genuinely matched long-vowel-plus-glide monosyllabic control. It therefore gives a descriptive contrast, not a precise likelihood ratio for the length of *hóeg*. Ritt's paper is useful precisely because it makes the issue of context-sensitive marking visible. Its own defective 98% result should not be recruited to increase confidence in a quantity inference.

### 10.3 A controlled argument about the early Anglian forms

Our argument can adopt Ritt's distinction between the observation and its interpretation:

```text
Observation: the Vespasian Psalter has <heg>.
Observation: Farman has accented <hóeg> twice.
Independent phonological result: early apocope yields *he.i
  through fronting, umlaut and w-loss.
Conventional reconstruction: Anglian /heːj/ from a protected stem.
Alternative historical hypotheses: retained hiatus, desyllabification,
  contraction with restoration, protected-stem replacement.

Interpretive bridge required: which linguistic forms are represented
  by the actual spellings in each scribal tradition?

Historical conclusion depends on that bridge; conventional normalized
  quantity is not an independent observation.
```

No matter how strongly Farman's accents favour length in his own usage, the inference to ninth-century Vespasian requires another premise: that the quantity in the two traditions must be the same. This is precisely the sort of premise an independent argument model should make visible. If that premise lacks support, one may prefer a long interpretation of Farman without excluding an earlier short form elsewhere.

### 10.4 Use Ritt to clarify, not exaggerate, the paper's contribution

The ‘hay’ article cannot simply imitate Ritt's conclusion and declare that the two regular histories are observationally equivalent. The exact bridge from /he.i/ to written ⟨heg⟩ remains a separate historical phonological problem. Ritt does not supply evidence for resyllabification or the graphic representation of hiatus. What he supplies is a model of how to test an interpretive premise that has been treated as an empirical fact.

The analogous central historical claim would be that the conventional length assigned to the earliest Anglian forms is not established by their spelling, so an argument for obligatory protected-stem replacement cannot rest on that reconstructed length alone. The positive alternative requires a defensible history for the final segment and the relevant syllabification. These two conclusions should not be collapsed.

## 11. Reusable argumentative and editorial template

The distinctive Ritt pattern can be expressed as six steps.

1. Identify an allegedly decisive piece of textual evidence and the historical conclusion usually inferred from it.
2. Reconstruct the received inference explicitly, including the comparative and orthographic assumptions that connect the record with the historical conclusion.
3. Specify a rival historical account and explain precisely why the conventional inference would be an objection to it.
4. Test the interpretive bridge with independent controls from the same source, rather than merely reasserting the rival account.
5. Formulate an alternative interpretation, derive its consequences and test those consequences with transparent denominators and reproducible classifications.
6. Distinguish the rejection of an allegedly decisive objection, positive support for the alternative interpretation and positive support for the rival history itself.

For an independent argument model, distinguish nodes for primary observations, independently established phonological quantities, interpretation of a graphic convention, relative chronology and analogical hypotheses. A revision to the orthographic interpretation should not silently change the reconstructed phonological history or the interpretation of every other witness. Conversely, a revision to the historical theory should not be allowed to promote an expected quantity into a directly observed one.

For a written article, present the relevant controls where they bear on the argument. Do not reproduce the model's internal dependency labels as scholarly prose. A compact table of actual comparable counts is appropriate; extended epistemological evaluations are best written in prose and only when necessary to understand the evidence.

## 12. The four articles as complementary models

| Paper | Principal contribution to our method | Characteristic danger exposed |
|---|---|---|
| Laker (2007) | Expose an unproved dependency in a chronological argument by constructing an alternative derivation. | Treating two historically separable events as necessarily simultaneous. |
| Versloot (2026) | Evaluate alternative chronological systems against a controlled body of early evidence. | Confusing agreement with grouped distributions with correct lexical prediction. |
| Ball & Stiles (1983) | Resolve a lexical anomaly through phonology, historical morphology, textual criticism and comparison. | Treating one anomalous spelling or a set of dependent glosses as independently decisive. |
| Ritt (2000) | Reconstruct and test the interpretive bridge from written evidence to phonological history. | Treating nonmarking as evidence of absence, or reporting a descriptive coverage fraction as predictive accuracy. |

Together these papers suggest a particularly productive strategy for ‘hay’. Begin with the competing regular developments and a precise attestation problem (Ball and Stiles); distinguish the stages and predicted consequences of the chronological accounts (Versloot); identify whether the received need for analogy depends on an additional, disputable phonological premise (Laker); and test whether the actual written evidence independently establishes the quantity and syllabification assigned to it (Ritt).

The common feature is not a particular prose style. It is the presence of a well-defined historical inference that can be tested, challenged and, where necessary, reformulated. The four models help distinguish the independent structure of the argument from its presentation in a paper. They also demonstrate why a substantial conclusion can survive the failure of a subsidiary argument, provided the dependencies have been correctly identified.
