# Arjen Pieter Versloot, “Reconstructing the historical phonology of Old English”: anatomy of a historical-linguistic argument

## 1. Source, scope and purpose

**Source:** Versloot, Arjen Pieter. 2026. Reconstructing the historical phonology of Old English. *English Language and Linguistics* 30(1), 103–141. https://doi.org/10.1017/S1360674324000674. The supplied PDF carries a 2025 copyright notice; the citation here uses the issue year. Local source: `reconstructing_the_historical_phonology_of_old_english.pdf`.

This analysis is based on the entire 39-page article, including its statistical appendix. Page references below are the printed journal pagination. I have checked the appendix table against the PDF rendering and independently recalculated two different measures of fit from its published rows. I have not obtained the underlying lexical database, independently collated its manuscripts or checked all the works cited by Versloot. The distinction between Versloot's argument and my assessment of it is maintained throughout.

The aim is to reverse-engineer a disciplinary example of argumentation: what propositions the article establishes, how the evidence supports them, what the prose does, and how its method can inform the Old English ‘hay’ investigation. It is a companion to the analysis of Laker's paper on palatalization. Laker principally attacks purportedly decisive chronological arguments; Versloot constructs a rival explanatory system and compares its performance with the conventional system. These are complementary strategies.

## 2. The intellectual problem and the contribution

Versloot's problem is the reconstruction of the development of stressed Proto-Germanic (PGmc) *a* into early Old English. In the conventional chronology, a relatively general fronting creates *æ*, which undergoes breaking in certain environments. Retraction, i-mutation and Anglian Smoothing subsequently remove the effects of earlier changes from many of the relevant words. The reconstructed intermediates may therefore differ substantially from both the comparative starting forms and the earliest written outcomes.

Versloot does not discover that the conventional derivations are impossible. His criticism is that much of their complexity may be unnecessary. An alternative with more restrictive early fronting, mutation of unbroken vowels and later breaking may account for the same results while dispensing with several reversals. Old Frisian and Old Norse provide comparative motivation for this proposal.

The paper therefore addresses two different claims:

- **Comparative adequacy:** an alternative chronology accounts for the early written evidence at least approximately as well as the conventional chronology.
- **Historical preference:** the alternative is preferable because its critical processes are more narrowly conditioned, require fewer unattested reversals, and accord more closely with independently reconstructed developments in related languages.

The first is a prerequisite for the second, not a substitute for it. It would be possible for the conventional chronology to have better raw performance but for an alternative to be historically preferable on other grounds. Conversely, a simpler alternative with serious unexplained discrepancies would not deserve acceptance merely because it was simpler.

One subsidiary result is particularly interesting: the degree of i-mutation in the glossaries correlates with the quality of the input vowel, the nature of the mutation trigger, syllable weight and the manuscript. Another is that a later breaking chronology may explain some forms, notably *feormat*, that resist the standard derivation.

The broad historical proposal is considerably more ambitious than the direct evidence. The corpus principally concerns non-West-Saxon sources and one input vowel. Versloot recognizes this limitation on p. 107. The extension to a complete reconstruction of Old English phonology, the suggested geographic origin of breaking and the relationship between English and Frisian are further hypotheses, not equivalent in status to the observed distribution of written forms.

## 3. Architecture of the article

| Location | Function in the argument |
|---|---|
| Abstract and §1.1 (103–106) | Define the conventional system and isolate its controversial premise: nearly general fronting and early breaking of *a*. Cite earlier criticisms that motivate an alternative. |
| §1.2 (106–108) | Introduce the alternative chronology and specify a comparative test. Restrict the scope to stressed *a* and the earliest evidence. |
| §2 (108–111) | Establish the corpus, manuscript transmission problem, etymological input and exclusion policy. |
| §3.1–3.3 (111–117) | Develop the alternative from the distribution of *a*, *æ* and *ea*, the chronological evidence and comparative phonetics. |
| §3.4–3.7 (117–121) | Examine additional assumptions: *x*, smoothing, the strength of mutation, palatal diphthongization and problematic glide sequences. |
| §4 (121–128) | Specify both rule systems, test them against the same glosses, compare errors and introduce two alternatives for the exact mechanism of breaking. |
| §5 (128–133) | Analyse the disagreements between models by historical process; discuss individual discriminating forms. |
| §6 (133–134) | Test the plausibility of the proposed history against the limited early Northumbrian evidence. |
| §7 (134–135) | State the resulting revised chronology and the grounds for preferring it. |
| Appendix (139–141) | Document the variables, coefficients and grouped observations of the i-mutation model. |

This is not a sequence of increasingly extensive literature reviews. It moves from a problem to a proposed alternative, from the alternative to operational predictions, from predictions to discrepancies, and from discrepancies to a historical preference.

The most important structural decision is that the reader encounters the rival systems before the corpus results. The subsequent philological details have a function because the reader already knows which historical question they address.

## 4. Hierarchical argument model

The following is my reconstruction, not a diagram supplied by Versloot. It distinguishes independent observations from assumptions that enter the historical model.

```text
CENTRAL PROBLEM
Does the early Old English evidence require widespread early
fronting and breaking followed by multiple reversals?
|
+-- A. Establish what the traditional chronology predicts
|   +-- A1. Define a near-general fronting rule and its exceptions
|   +-- A2. Specify early breaking and later retraction/mutation/smoothing
|   +-- A3. Identify outputs, including otherwise unattested intermediates
|   `-- Conclusion: a testable but potentially overcomplicated system
|
+-- B. Develop a historically motivated alternative
|   +-- B1. Establish the observed distribution of a, æ, ea
|   +-- B2. Examine chronological and dialectal variation
|   +-- B3. Compare Frisian fronting and North Germanic breaking
|   +-- B4. Investigate phonetic mechanisms for the origin of ea
|   |   +-- B4a. Lengthening followed by diphthongization
|   |   `-- B4b. Restricted early fronting followed by late breaking
|   +-- B5. Explain apparent smoothing contexts without early breaking
|   `-- Conclusion: two plausible versions of a late-breaking chronology
|
+-- C. Establish the admissible evidence
|   +-- C1. Identify source manuscripts and transmission layers
|   +-- C2. Establish etymological inputs and exclusions
|   `-- C3. Define predicted and observed vowel categories
|
+-- D. Compare the rival systems
|   +-- D1. Categorical predictions on Épinal and Erfurt
|   +-- D2. Separate analysis of i-mutation variability
|   +-- D3. Compare agreements, failures and discriminating words
|   +-- D4. Test on limited early Northumbrian data
|   `-- Conclusion: similar categorical fit; alternative merits examination
|
+-- E. Decide what the comparative results mean
|   +-- E1. Which discrepancies bear directly on chronology?
|   +-- E2. Which proposed changes have comparative motivation?
|   +-- E3. What does each system require beyond its observations?
|   `-- E4. Are the phonetic mechanism and direction of diffusion decided?
|
`-- OVERALL CONCLUSION
    Prefer a restrictive-fronting, late-breaking account for the
    early sources on comparative and explanatory grounds.
    Exact mechanism of breaking remains open; wider generalization
    requires independent investigation.
```

Logical dependency: A and C are necessary for D; B supplies the rival model for D. D establishes comparative fit, not the truth of B. E combines the comparison with comparative plausibility and economy to establish the author's preference. B4a and B4b are alternatives, not conjunctive requirements. The thesis that breaking is late does not require a definitive choice between them.

This last distinction is essential. A paper can have a positive thesis at one level of historical explanation while remaining undecided at a more detailed level.

## 6. Close analysis of the principal historical arguments

### 6.1 Distribution before mechanism: the relation between *a*, *æ* and *ea* (pp. 111–114)

Versloot isolates a traditional assumption from Campbell: *æ* is regarded as a necessary intermediate between inherited *a* and the broken vowel. The article then examines whether the attestation distribution actually requires that intermediate.

Its most useful examples are not those that straightforwardly agree with either model. They are cases where earlier or less-breaking varieties have *a*, whereas later or more-breaking varieties have *ea*. The unbroken *-gard* in early Northumbrian evidence is a specific difficulty for the traditional fronting chronology as characterized in the paper; *-geard* is the alternative written form. The author connects this with older spellings in names and the wider correspondence *ald/eald*.

The argument is abductive:

```text
Observed distribution: a ~ ea, with relatively little intermediate æ
Comparative premise: velar environments can inhibit fronting
Hypothesis: some a remained unfronted until breaking
Alternative: fronting occurred, followed by reversal or hidden spelling
Question: which history better explains the distribution with independent support?
```

The useful point is that an unattested intermediate is not simply rejected because it is unattested. Its necessity is tested against an alternative derivation. This is also an instructive distinction for the hay article: lack of a macron in the manuscript does not prove short quantity, but a history requiring length must be compared with a history that does not.

The evidential limit is that geographical variation is not automatically temporal succession. Spellings can be retained in copies. A distribution lacking intermediate *æ* is suggestive rather than conclusive evidence that no such phonetic stage ever occurred.

### 6.2 Give the alternative a phonetic mechanism (pp. 114–117)

Removing fronting creates a genuine explanatory obligation: how could the proposed unfronted vowel develop into a front-back diphthong? Versloot confronts this difficulty rather than simply announcing a more economical chronology.

He compares the cluster environments for early English breaking with those for lengthening in Old East Frisian and with lengthening reported for Old English. The overlap motivates a conditional lengthening stage. Faroese supplies a precedent in which a short *a* becomes diphthongal only when lengthened; West Frisian provides another comparison. The proposed route is roughly:

`*a > *[a̟ˑ] > [ɛa]`

Here the intermediate phonetic lengthening need not establish a new phonemic long vowel. That is important because the traditional short broken vowels behave metrically as short vowels. The argument connects the conditioning environment, a phonetic mechanism and comparative precedents rather than relying on any one of them in isolation.

The paper then provides a second serious account, in §4.3: restricted early fronting before relevant *rC* environments followed by later breaking. This improves the comparison with Frisian and accounts for some *æ* spellings in Erfurt. The advantages of the two explanations differ. Lengthening accounts more naturally for the concentration of breaking in voiced environments, while the restricted-fronting account gives a more straightforward diphthongal input and handles some manuscript variation better.

An unresolved choice between these mechanisms does not force the author to abandon late breaking. Both share a more limited scope for early fronting, mutation of unbroken vowels and breaking after mutation. This is a particularly good example of defining the shared conclusion of rival subsidiary analyses.

### 6.3 How to exhibit an unnecessary reversal (pp. 117–119, 123)

The paper tests the environments in which the traditional model predicts breaking and subsequent smoothing. It proposes that early Anglian *x* could have been palatal [ç], allowing fronting without the intervening diphthong. It contrasts forms such as *naecht* and *fex* with broken forms in other clusters.

The clearest pedagogical example is *fristmearc*. The conventional implementation predicts a smoothed vowel in the relevant *rk* context; the alternative predicts breaking of retained *a* and gives the attested outcome. The conclusion is not that smoothing never occurred in every historical context. The author explicitly limits his account and allows smoothing for long/old diphthongs in n. 24. Rather, the short-*a* data need not require the whole proposed breaking-smoothing history.

Four examples on p. 123 are exceptionally effective. They cover all four combinations of success/failure:

| Etymon / attestation | Traditional history | Alternative history | Comparison |
|---|---|---|---|
| `*spadō(n)-`, ⟨spadan⟩ | Fronting then retraction | No fronting before relevant back vowel | Both correct |
| `*habuk-`, ⟨hebuc⟩ | Predicts retained/restored a | Predicts retained a | Both wrong |
| `*markō-`, ⟨fristmearc⟩ | Predicts smoothed *mærk-* | Predicts broken *meark-* | Alternative correct |
| `*hanjō-`, ⟨haen⟩ | Mutation predicts æ | Strong mutation predicts e | Traditional correct |

The forms and verdicts are condensed from Versloot's examples; they concern predicted vowel categories, not validation of complete word derivations. This display is a model of scholarly tabulation: the two models have a common input and common observable output, and the table makes the differences explicit. No prose argument is forced into a table cell.

### 6.4 The i-mutation cline (pp. 119–120, 139–141)

The traditional and alternative systems have a related problem: different manuscripts write *e* and *ae* for the products of i-mutation. Versloot does not treat this as arbitrary scribal noise. He seeks correlates in the reconstructed input vowel, mutation trigger, stem weight and manuscript.

For instance, in the Épinal subset with unambiguous triggers (Table 4), a fronted input combined with long *ī* or *j* yields *e* in 9/10 cases; an unfronted input with short *i* yields *e* in 2/9 cases. Erfurt has stronger overall raising and a different distribution (Table 5). The distinction between weak and strong mutation triggers is itself a potentially useful historical result, with comparisons from North Germanic, Old Frisian and Old Saxon.

The appendix specifies a logistic model using four coded variables. This is a more informative explanatory procedure than simply adopting a separate rule for every inconvenient lexical item. The model identifies structured variation. It does not, however, guarantee that individual tokens with identical feature values have identifiable distinct historical trajectories. The scoring issue is discussed in §9 below.

### 6.5 Discriminating lexical evidence: *feormat* and *ferwit* (pp. 131–132)

The form *feormat* is especially valuable. If its etymon is *farmjan-*, early fronting and mutation can produce *ferm-*. The occurrence of *eo* before *rm* can then be understood as breaking of the already mutated vowel. This would directly favor breaking after mutation.

Importantly, *feormat* is outside the four-category principal tally because *eo* is an irregular category for that comparison. Versloot nevertheless discusses it. A numerical exclusion from a particular test should not make a historically informative example disappear from the argument.

The second example uses morphological structure. Épinal and Erfurt have *ferwit-* in compounds glossing ‘curiosity’, whereas Corpus has *feorwit-*. The comparative evidence suggests an earlier prefix *fer-*; with a morpheme boundary the vowel does not stand before an internal root cluster *rC*. If the boundary became opaque later, the form could undergo breaking at the later stage. That yields a proposed date range for continuing productivity of breaking.

Versloot immediately recognizes alternative explanations involving copying error or influence from another prefix. This is an exemplary form of localized qualification: it concerns the specific premise that might fail, and it does not dissolve the entire historical argument into uncertainty.

## 7. Evidence architecture

Versloot assigns different evidence different tasks. The same observation is not automatically counted twice simply because it is useful at multiple points.

- The comparative reconstruction supplies the input; it prevents the written English form from being the sole basis for its own historical explanation.
- Early manuscript spellings supply the test endpoints. They are not assumed to transcribe phonemic categories without uncertainty.
- Manuscript relationships and orthographic variation constrain the historical interpretation of those endpoints.
- Comparative Germanic evidence supplies alternative phonetic mechanisms and plausible conditioning, rather than serving only as a collection of cognates.
- Distributions within the corpus motivate rule refinements and measure their descriptive fit.
- Individual difficult words test particular ordering relations, often more directly than a global percentage.
- The small Northumbrian sample checks whether the proposed chronology is confined to the two main glossaries.

Section 2 is important because it admits that the glossaries contain material of different dates, with different degrees of updating. Versloot nevertheless adopts a working decision to analyse manuscript-level distributions. He gives reasons for doing so and cautions against excluding forms as dialect mixture or contamination simply because they do not fit the model.

The lexical selection is similarly explicit. 303 of 336 etymological candidates are admitted after excluding insufficiently reliable input vowel or suffix reconstructions. Some *aw* and *ax(w)* cases are subsequently omitted from the main count because paradigm alternation makes their developments difficult to predict (§3.7). The author promises fuller data availability, but this analysis did not acquire the individual dataset. The published counts should be treated as reported results, not as independently replicated measurements.

One point of direct importance to ‘hay’: Versloot expressly treats the *aw* sequences as problematic and recognizes the role of the following vowel and paradigm levelling in their histories. His general argument for a revised chronology of short *a* therefore cannot simply be invoked as an independent solution to the historical development of *hawją*.

## 8. Prose and scholarly apparatus

### 8.1 The introduction identifies a controversy with consequences

The opening does three things in a limited space: identifies the conventional chronology, explains what its contentious feature is, and identifies previous criticism. It then proposes a testable alternative. The literature appears because it establishes the problem, rather than because the author wishes to display exhaustive reading.

The short comparison of the conventional and Frisian histories on p. 112 gives substance to the claim of unnecessary complexity. Readers can understand what the author means by an unnecessary reconstructed reversal without needing to accept his historical conclusions in advance.

### 8.2 A characteristic argumentative sequence

The discussion of the origin of *ea* in §3.3 illustrates how the prose proceeds in small steps. It begins with the difficulty posed by the new hypothesis, introduces a possible mechanism, establishes the overlap in conditioning environments, offers concrete comparative examples and only then states the historical reconstruction. It subsequently discusses whether the lengthening and the resulting diphthong were phonemic.

The paragraph sequence can be abstracted as:

```text
Explain why a proposed analysis creates a specific problem.
Identify an independently documented phenomenon that may solve it.
Give the relevant data and explain the similarity of environments.
State the historical hypothesis with its precise conditions.
Identify the consequential differences or uncertainties.
Explain what the hypothesis would remove from the older account.
```

For the hay article, this would be a better way to use the *clēa* comparison than announcing that it is either decisive or irrelevant. The question is whether the phonetic mechanism, conditioning and chronological environment can support the proposed treatment of final /e.i/.

### 8.3 Citations perform argumentative work

The major authorities have identifiable functions. Campbell supplies the traditional premise that the diphthong arose from a fronted vowel. Hogg supplies the formalizable conventional rules. Kortlandt supplies a criticism of reversing developments. The Frisian and North Germanic literature supplies alternative histories and phonetic conditioning. The edition and manuscript studies supply the basis for the empirical test.

A useful discipline for the hay article would be to ask of each citation: does it establish the observation, motivate a sound change, support the reconstructed input, or offer a rival interpretation? These are different functions. A reference to a handbook is not independent confirmation of all four.

### 8.4 The use of tables is justified by their contents

Table 1 gives a synoptic comparison across dialects. Tables 2–3 compare predicted versus observed vowel classes in the same environments. Tables 4–5 give the distribution of mutation outcomes. Tables 6–9 contain the overall model results and their overlap. Table 10 isolates breaking-specific errors. The appendix gives the grouped data used in the statistical analysis.

These tables contain genuine corresponding variables. They are not an administrative matrix of the author's views of previous scholarship. The text discusses the informative differences without reciting every cell. The large statistical table belongs in the appendix because it enables detailed scrutiny without interrupting the phonological argument.

Footnotes handle matters such as etymological complications, alternative bibliographical interpretations, additional comparisons and the limits of scope. Several are substantial, but the central derivations and data needed to follow the claim remain in the main text.

### 8.5 What not to imitate

Versloot is often more personally voiced than the style sought for CAPR. He uses first-person formulations, rhetorical questions, and evaluations of economy or plausibility. Some are useful because they mark a genuine decision; others could be expressed more analytically. The hay article should not imitate his wording, nor should it import his frequent methodological signposting.

The strongest passages are those in which precise forms and conditioning environments carry the reasoning. The weaker passages are those that claim a general historical preference without making the remaining comparison assumptions fully explicit. The best lesson is the architecture, not an imitation of the author's surface style.

## 9. Adversarial evaluation: how strong is the argument?

### 9.1 The overall categorical comparison does not establish a winner

The published results are:

| Manuscript | Traditional | Alternative, categorical | Alternative, grouped probabilistic score |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Épinal | 237/282 (84%) | 232/282 (82%) | 244/282 (87%) |
| Erfurt | 195/274 (71%) | 197/274 (72%) | 218/274 (80%) |
| Combined | 432/556 (77.7%) | 429/556 (77.2%) | 462/556 (83.1%) |

The first two columns show virtually equivalent descriptive performance. The strongest evidence is not an overall categorical advantage for the new chronology. Instead, the alternative predicts broken spellings somewhat better (Table 10: 7 versus 11 errors in Épinal, 12 versus 17 in Erfurt), and proposes a different historical account of why those outcomes arise.

Tables 8–9 are particularly illuminating. In Épinal, both systems are correct on 223 tokens and incorrect on 36; the traditional model alone succeeds on 14, the alternative alone on 9. Erfurt gives 184 and 66 agreements, with 11 traditional-only and 13 alternative-only successes. Their high agreement means most tokens cannot discriminate between these model implementations. The informative cases require close philological discussion.

### 9.2 A consequential problem with the probabilistic accuracy figure

I independently inspected the appendix's final grouped table (p. 141) and recalculated its fit measure. The published 87% figure is not ordinary per-token classification accuracy.

Consider the Épinal row with manuscript = 0, vowel = 0, syllable = 1, mutation = 0. It contains two observed *ae* and two observed *e*. The fitted probability of *e* is 0.30, giving predicted counts of three *ae* and one *e*. The table counts three correct predictions. This is the overlap between predicted and observed category totals. The model has not identified which of the four words has the predicted *e*.

In another row, with observed counts eight *ae* and fifteen *e* and fitted probability 0.65, the predicted group counts are eight and fifteen. The table awards 23 correct predictions. A classifier assigning the more probable outcome to each token would instead correctly classify fifteen, not twenty-three.

Using all 24 printed group rows and the fitted probabilities (threshold 0.5) gives the following independent recalculation:

| Subset | Tokens | Matched rounded group counts (published method) | Individual classification using most probable class |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Épinal | 101 | 85 (84.2%) | 72 (71.3%) |
| Erfurt | 98 | 89 (90.8%) | 71 (72.4%) |
| Combined | 199 | 174 (87.4%) | 143 (71.9%) |

The grouped measure is meaningful as a descriptive measure of distributional fit. It cannot be interpreted as having predicted 174 particular lexical outcomes correctly. The actual historical information in the groups is different from a determinate prediction for each lexical input.

Consequently, the comparison between the categorical scores and the improved probabilistic scores is not like-for-like. The p-value in §5.5 n. 32 compares the combined totals as if they measured equivalent successes. In addition, the two chronologies evaluate the same material, which calls for a paired comparison if using token-level classification tests; the glossaries also share textual material. The statistical model was fitted on the analysed observations, not reported as evaluated on a separately held-out lexical set. The odds ratios on p. 140 also quantify relative odds, not probability ratios.

None of these observations refutes the historical association between mutation strength and the listed factors. They do mean that the reported higher overall accuracy cannot establish that the probabilistic alternative is a superior predictor of individual tokens. A fairer evaluation would compare log loss or another probabilistic scoring rule on the same observations, or use common categorical prediction criteria, preferably with manuscript- and lexeme-aware validation. That would require the underlying token-level data.

This is an important caution for CAPR. A model that accounts for the aggregate distribution of spellings may be historically illuminating while not actually deriving the particular attested word forms. The two achievements must be reported separately.

### 9.3 Parsimony is not a rule count alone

The alternative removes reversals such as general fronting followed by retraction, and broad breaking followed by smoothing. This is a genuine explanatory advantage if the alternative's narrower environments and phonetic assumptions are independently motivated.

But the alternative introduces its own complexities: the proposed Anglian [ç], the different strength of mutation triggers, manuscript-sensitive conditioning, possible lexical diffusion, and alternative treatments of the input vowel for breaking. Whether it is simpler depends on the complexity of conditioning and auxiliary assumptions, not merely the number of named changes. A formal implementation could make the competing requirements more transparent.

The conventional model is also adjusted for the test: Versloot omits a retraction rule that would make numerous wrong predictions and optimizes certain mutation conditions (pp. 122–124). This is commendably explicit. It also means that both models are partly calibrated to these observations. The comparison is best regarded as an exercise in reconstructing viable histories, rather than an untouched predictive experiment.

### 9.4 A phonetic parallel establishes possibility and motivation, not identity

The Faroese and Frisian developments make length-conditioned diphthongization historically plausible. Their existence does not directly establish the timing, phonetic realization or distribution of Old English breaking. The second, restricted-fronting hypothesis is important precisely because it shows how the same main chronology can be maintained without requiring the first mechanism to be correct.

A skeptical reviewer would ask whether the positive evidence distinguishes the proposed late breaking from an earlier breaking process with differential subsequent development. The best answer must appeal to chronology-sensitive forms such as *feormat* and *ferwit/feorwit*, not merely to the economy of the preferred history. Those examples in turn require independently secure etymology and source history.

### 9.5 Scope of the conclusion

The broad claims about Old English and Frisian are more extensive than the directly evaluated corpus. The early Northumbrian evidence is limited, and the paper acknowledges the difficulty of distinguishing chronology from dialect differences and chance (p. 134). The conjecture of diffusion from West Saxon is plausible within the author's account, but it is not uniquely established by the glossaries.

My verdict: the paper makes a serious and useful case for reconsidering the standard chronology. It shows that an alternative is worth preferring or investigating on independent historical grounds. Its strongest contribution does not depend on the claimed statistical superiority, and the exact origin of the broken vowel remains unresolved.

## 10. Transfer to the article on Old English ‘hay’

### 10.1 Put the rival predictions on the table early

Versloot's structure suggests that our paper should begin by specifying what each history predicts. For ‘hay’, the appropriate comparison is the frozen regular early-apocope route, the protected-stem route and Ringe and Taylor's analogical substitution, and the regular late-apocope route. The precise stages must come from the checked CAPR traces, not an abbreviated remembered derivation.

The reader should know immediately that the formal early-apocope output *hei* contains syllabic /i/ at the end of the implemented cascade, whereas the conventional West Saxon *hīeġ* has a different nucleus and final /j/. This is not merely a difference in serialization. The disputed historical question concerns how those outputs relate to the attested spellings.

### 10.2 Give the proposed regular history independent phonetic motivation

The most useful comparison is Versloot's treatment of the origin of *ea*. He does not assume that removing a traditional stage solves the problem. He explicitly asks what historical mechanism must replace it and looks for structural comparative evidence.

For ‘hay’, the independently supported development through *he.i* is not the end of the argument. If the early Anglian spelling represents /hej/, the regular-survival hypothesis needs a motivated development of the second vowel to a glide. Hogg's back-vowel desyllabification examples and the retained hiatus examples are relevant at different points of the argument, not interchangeable exact controls. A proposal based on these parallels should identify what conditioning it extends, why that extension is defensible, and what its limitations are.

### 10.3 Use the discriminating observations rather than raw counts

The most valuable data in Versloot are sometimes exceptions to both models or excluded from the principal tally. Similarly, a numerical benchmark mismatch for *hei* should not settle the historical question. The exact spelling ⟨hei⟩ in the probably hay-related Kentish compound *heiweg* is noteworthy even if the history of its composition form is uncertain. The secure early Anglian spellings ⟨heg⟩ and ⟨heig⟩ provide a separate line of evidence. Farman's accents, West Saxon high vocalism and the rare overtly inflected forms are potentially discriminating in other respects.

The article needs to ask what each model predicts for each of these observations, including quantity, syllabification and spelling. It must distinguish an exact spelling match from demonstrated phonological identity. A form may warrant an independent philological discussion even when it cannot be admitted as a direct benchmark target.

### 10.4 Formulate a hierarchy of historical results

Versloot retains a general conclusion about the chronology of breaking even though its exact phonetic origin remains open. The parallel hierarchy for ‘hay’ might be:

```text
H1. The early-apocope chronology regularly generates a hiatus stage.
H2. A probably hay-related early compound has the exact written sequence hei.
H3. Ordinary early Anglian spelling does not establish long quantity.
H4. An independently motivated regular continuation of the direct stem exists.
H5. A particular attested form is best explained by that regular continuation.
H6. The direct stem need not have been replaced in every dialect.
H7. This alters the comparative explanatory burden of the two chronologies.
```

H1–H3 do not by themselves establish H4 or H5. H6 could receive support independently from a limited-restoration account, even if H4 fails. A claim about the scope of whole-stem levelling is not identical to a claim about completely analogy-free survival. H7 should state precisely which previously asserted disadvantage has been reduced, rather than infer that one chronology must now be correct.

### 10.5 Apply the same standards to phonology and analogy

Versloot's alternative must explain why a new phonetic development is defensible; the traditional chronology must explain why several reversing stages are necessary. Similarly, the proposed direct history of ‘hay’ requires phonetic and orthographic motivation, while the analogy hypothesis requires a reconstructible donor, a mechanism, an appropriate date, and some reason to prefer the proposed direction of replacement. The existence of a type of analogy in another word does not prove that it occurred here, just as a phonetic possibility does not establish a historical sound law.

A positive historical argument is a reasoned comparison of these explanatory requirements. It need not attain deductive certainty. But it must not treat the absence of contrary evidence as sufficient evidence for the favored hypothesis.

## 11. A reusable control on the article and its argument model

The two analyses (Laker and Versloot) suggest a common workflow that can be implemented without importing software documentation into the published paper.

1. Identify the received historical claim and specify its actual predictions.
2. Identify the assumption upon which its alleged necessity depends.
3. Specify a rival history, including any additional assumptions required.
4. Establish the evidential domain, source dating and input selection before comparing predictions.
5. Determine which observations both histories explain and which actually distinguish them.
6. Examine the divergent cases individually, including inconvenient counterexamples.
7. Decide which conclusion follows at each level of the argument; maintain rival subsidiary branches where appropriate.
8. State the strongest defensible positive result. Then state the specific remaining uncertainty that limits its extension.

For every substantive article paragraph, the internal argument model should identify an evidential input, an inference and the conclusion that the paragraph establishes. A purely expository paragraph can introduce necessary terminology or data. It should not pretend to establish a historical conclusion. A table should compare regularly structured data, not replace an inferential paragraph.

A useful adversarial test is to remove any one disputed premise from the argument model. Which published conclusions no longer follow? If the removal of one minor assumption destroys the entire paper, the hierarchy of claims may be badly organized. If the same conclusion survives through a genuinely independent route, the paper should make the existence of that route clear.

## 12. Overall judgement

Versloot is a particularly useful model for CAPR because he treats a historical chronology as a system with explicit predictions, compares that system with an alternative, and then uses philological analysis to interpret their disagreements. He understands that the historical significance of an individual word may be greater than its contribution to an overall score.

The methodological criticisms above are material. In particular, the probabilistic fit result must not be confused with individual predictive success. His strongest case survives this criticism: comparable categorical performance, better fit in specific breaking environments, a more restricted sequence of historical developments and positive comparative motivation make a rival chronology worthy of serious consideration.

The lesson for the hay article is not that we should adopt Versloot's actual reconstruction wholesale. It is that the comparison of explicit historical predictions should generate a substantive argument. The reconstruction of the argument must precede the prose; the prose must then make the crucial historical differences, the evidence bearing upon them and the resulting historical preference clear to the reader.
