# Stephen Laker, “Palatalization of Velars”: anatomy of a historical-linguistic argument

## 1. Source and purpose

Stephen Laker, “Palatalization of Velars: A Major Link of Old English and Old Frisian”, pp. 165–184. Source: the twenty-page article stored as `abag-article-p165_8.pdf` in the research library. Page references below are to the printed pagination, not PDF sheet numbers. The supplied extract does not include the volume's title page; the full publication metadata has not been independently verified here.

I selected this paper because its problem is particularly close to that of the proposed article on Old English ‘hay’: competing relative chronologies, potentially misleading appeals to analogy, the interpretation of dialectal variation, and the question whether an alleged objection to a historical reconstruction is actually valid. It is also a useful example of traditional Germanic philology in which a definite thesis is pursued without requiring every subsidiary historical question to be resolved.

The analysis below distinguishes Laker's claims from my reconstruction of their logical relationships and from my critical assessment. It is not a transcription of the paper. I read all twenty pages and inspected the comparative figure, map and derivational display on pp. 167, 170 and 175. Some phonetic symbols are corrupt in the extracted text; the analysis avoids relying on those corrupted symbols. I have not independently checked the publications cited in Laker's footnotes.

## 2. The intellectual problem and contribution

**Question.** Did palatalization of velars in Old English and Old Frisian originate in a shared continental development, or did it arise independently in the two languages?

**Earlier view.** Siebs, Luick and Campbell place the beginnings of palatalization before the Anglo-Saxon settlement of Britain. They distinguish the initial conditioning of velars from the later development of affricates.

**Challenge.** Hogg, Stiles and Nielsen argue that the relative chronologies of English and Frisian cannot be made to coincide up to palatalization. If the ordering of the relevant changes differs, they infer independent development. Fulk and Kortlandt have already attempted to defend shared origins by revising the chronology (pp. 165–166).

**Laker's thesis.** The continental origin of palatalization is preferable. Three of the four principal chronological objections to shared origins are inadequate; the fourth, concerning the Germanic diphthong *au*, remains inconclusive. Moreover, a genealogical inference from incompatible chronologies is not necessarily appropriate for a change that could spread through a dialect continuum (pp. 166, 173–174, 183–184).

Two contributions should be distinguished:

1. A positive historical proposal: early continental palatalization followed by later phonetic, phonological and dialectal developments; post-umlaut adaptation of British place-names need not imply the continuation of the original productive sound law.
2. A critical result: the particular arguments used to exclude common origins do not establish the conclusions claimed for them.

The second result does not logically entail the first. The article supplies independent arguments for the first and then removes purported obstacles. This distinction is fundamental to the paper's architecture.

## 3. Architecture of the article

| Printed pages | Section | Function in the argument |
|---|---|---|
| 165–166 | 1. Introduction | Identifies the controversy, attributes the opposing positions and states the author's proposed resolution. |
| 166–168 | 2. Palatalization in Old English and Old Frisian | Establishes the comparative distribution and distinguishes palatalization from assibilation. |
| 168–173 | 3. The history of palatalization | Develops positive chronological evidence, chiefly from umlaut and the adaptation of British place-names. |
| 173–174 | 4. The debated chronology of palatalization | Establishes the limitations of a simple genealogical interpretation and of treating a prolonged process as an instantaneous event. |
| 174–175 | 4.1 Breaking and palatal diphthongization | Demonstrates by competing derivations that the evidence does not fix the order of breaking and palatalization itself. |
| 176–177 | 4.2 Breaking and palatalization of the voiceless fricative | Challenges the assumption that different velars necessarily palatalized at the same time. |
| 177–180 | 4.3 Palatalization before the reflex of Germanic *au* | Compares four rival analyses of a genuinely difficult counterexample. |
| 180–183 | 4.4 Different conditioning factors | Reinterprets apparent English–Frisian differences as later dialectal outcomes and possible levelling. |
| 183–184 | 5. Conclusion | Restates the positive proposal and adjudicates each of the four objections. |

There is no large, autonomous literature-review section. The history of scholarship is introduced briefly in §1; subsequent scholarship appears where its claims become necessary to the analysis. The article's organization is determined by the historical problem, not by an inventory of everything read.

## 4. Hierarchical argument diagram

This diagram reconstructs the logical organization rather than the sequence of the printed sections. The connectors indicate inferential relationships, not sound changes.

```text
CENTRAL QUESTION
    Was the origin of English and Frisian palatalization shared?
    |
    +-- POSITIVE CHRONOLOGICAL CASE (§§2–3)
    |   +-- Comparable palatalization environments
    |   +-- Lack of palatalization before umlaut-derived vowels
    |   +-- Approximate date of umlaut
    |   +-- British place-names with later palatal outcomes
    |   `-- Distinguish productive sound change from loan adaptation
    |       CONCLUSION: early palatalization is historically plausible;
    |       later place-name evidence need not contradict it.
    |
    +-- CRITIQUE OF NECESSARY INDEPENDENCE (§4)
    |   +-- Objection 1: breaking vs palatal diphthongization
    |   |   `-- Separate palatalization from palatal diphthongization.
    |   +-- Objection 2: breaking before the voiceless fricative
    |   |   `-- Different velars need not palatalize simultaneously.
    |   +-- Objection 3: Germanic *au
    |   |   `-- Several histories fit; relative chronology unresolved.
    |   `-- Objection 4: differing conditioning
    |       `-- Separate early palatalization from later assibilation;
    |           distinguish dialects and consider paradigm levelling.
    |       CONCLUSION: three objections fail or are substantially
    |       weakened; one remains unresolved.
    |
    `-- INTERPRETIVE FRAME (§§1, 4)
        +-- A dialect continuum permits diffusion of changes.
        `-- A prolonged phonetic process may span other sound changes.
            CONCLUSION: incompatible later chronologies need not
            imply independent origins of the initial development.

SYNTHESIS
    Shared continental origins are preferable on the author's analysis.
    The exact development of *au and the mode of transmission remain open.
```

The three branches are not interchangeable. Showing that the anti-shared-origin arguments fail permits a shared origin; it does not by itself prove one. Evidence for early English palatalization makes the common-origin hypothesis more plausible, but early English and early Frisian innovations could in principle still have arisen independently. The geographical and dialect-continuum argument bridges part of that inferential gap.

| P15 | P10–P14 substantially undermine the necessity of independent origins. | Combined assessment of the four objections, p. 184. | Critical result; does not by itself establish common origins. |
| P16 | Shared early palatalization is compatible with later differences in chronology and outcome. | P01–P02, P08, P15 plus the dialect-continuum account, pp. 166, 173–174, 183–184. | Synthetic historical preference; diffusion, inheritance and independent parallelism not uniquely distinguished. |

A useful feature of such an argument ledger is that an objection to P12 need not affect P10. A new interpretation of British place-names might weaken P08 without restoring the alleged chronological objection removed in P10. The strength of the overall thesis can be reassessed locally rather than by rewriting the entire paper.

## 6. Close analysis of the four chronological objections

### 6.1 Breaking and palatal diphthongization (pp. 174–175)

This is the most directly transferable example for the ‘hay’ article.

Stiles uses the West Germanic input *kerl* and Old English *ċeorl* ‘churl’. If palatalization induced palatal diphthongization before breaking, one would expect the wrong result, **ċierl. Laker observes that this argument implicitly assumes that palatalization and palatal diphthongization must occur together or in immediate succession.

Here is a simplified reconstruction of Laker's three derivations (his display (1), p. 175). Asterisks denote reconstructed forms; the double asterisk on the rejected output is the author's convention, retained here only to explain his display.

| Stage | A: Breaking first | B: Palatalization and diphthongization first | C: Palatalization first, breaking next |
|---|---|---|---|
| Input | *kerl | *kerl | *kerl |
| First change | Breaking: *ceorl | Palatalization: *ċerl | Palatalization: *ċerl |
| Second change | Palatalization: *ċeorl | Palatal diphthongization: *ċierl | Breaking: *ċeorl |
| Predicted outcome | ċeorl | **ċierl (unattested prediction) | ċeorl |

The important result is not the mere existence of an alternative derivation. It is the identification of the concealed assumption that rules out the third derivation. Palatal diphthongization occurs in a more restricted set of dialects than velar palatalization (p. 175, n. 30), independently motivating their separation. Thus the observation constrains the order of breaking and palatal diphthongization, but not necessarily the order of breaking and the initial palatalization of velars.

**Argument pattern:** an opponent presents A versus B; B fails; the opponent concludes A. Laker introduces C and supplies independent grounds for permitting C. Since A and C have the same output but opposite orderings of the relevant pair, the output cannot establish that ordering.

**Critical qualification:** C demonstrates non-uniqueness, not the actual historical priority of palatalization. It is the combination of C with the independent dating case that supports the article's broader thesis.

### 6.2 The voiceless fricative (pp. 176–177)

Stiles argues that breaking before *h* requires this consonant to have remained non-palatalized at the relevant date; he treats this as evidence for the late palatalization of velars as a class. His examples include ‘idol’, ‘near’ and ‘laugh’ (p. 176).

Laker accepts the immediate implication concerning the fricative but disputes the extension to the other velars. Frisian provides comparisons in which the voiced velar and voiceless fricative have different histories: OFris *riucht/riocht* ‘right’ against *rein* ‘rain’, for example. Modern Frisian and other Germanic varieties display different distributions of palatal allophones of the voiceless fricative. The comparison motivates treating the palatalization of different consonants as separate historical processes.

The critical move is therefore to narrow the scope of the inference to the segment for which the observation is probative. The discussion also supplies a possible explanation for the contrast between English *feohtan* and *segl* (p. 177).

**Critical qualification:** modern typological and dialectal parallels establish the possibility of independent treatment, not its exact chronology in prehistoric English. This is sufficient to weaken the alleged necessary ordering but insufficient to fix the alternative ordering.

### 6.3 The problem of Germanic *au* (pp. 177–180)

The difference between English *ċēapian* and Old Frisian *kapia* is a stronger objection. The English initial velar has a palatal reflex before the vowel ultimately derived from *au*; Frisian does not. If fronting of short *a* and of *au* were one shared event, the contrast is difficult to explain.

Laker compares four previously proposed alternatives:

- Luick and Goblirsch: shared fronting of *au*, followed by Frisian monophthongization and an account of the absence of palatalization.
- Fulk: shared fronting and palatalization, followed by reversal of the diphthongal development and depalatalization in Frisian before assibilation.
- Page: early monophthongization in both languages, followed by renewed diphthongization in English.
- Krupatkin and others: the English development of *au* is later and need not be identified with fronting of short *a*.

The author considers the number and nature of the required changes, the chronology supplied by onomastic and runic evidence, and whether the relevant vowel developments are necessarily one process. He favours the first and fourth possibilities, which he suggests may be reconcilable. He nevertheless acknowledges that the problem remains unresolved (p. 180).

This section illustrates appropriate selective uncertainty. The author does not weaken the entire article because one of four objections is inconclusive. Nor does he claim that listing alternatives establishes the correct one.

**Critical qualification:** the preference for the first and fourth alternatives is not established by a complete, uniform comparative test. The relative economy of stage counts is not decisive if the proposed stages have different degrees of independent support. A new formal model of these alternatives would be a natural extension, not something Laker has already done.

### 6.4 Different conditioning environments (pp. 180–183)

Stiles adduces differences between English and Frisian in medial and final position. Laker separates two problems that can otherwise be confused:

1. A velar in historically absolute final position, following a front vowel.
2. An originally medial velar that becomes final after loss of a following vowel or glide.

For the first, he distinguishes palatalization from assibilation and argues that Northumbrian and parts of the Midlands did not develop the same final affricates as southern varieties. He re-examines the actual geographical provenance of place-name examples used by Pak to challenge Watson. Prestwich, Horwich and Reddish are not in the Northumbrian area proper on Laker's geographical definition; the remaining Lintz example has an alternative interpretation (pp. 181–182).

For the second, the author argues that Scandinavian influence cannot explain all the unassibilated forms, since such forms also occur in areas outside its main sphere. He develops Luick's paradigm-levelling proposal: loss of *j* can leave formerly palatalized velars before different following vowels in different grammatical cells, creating opportunities for generalization of one alternant. Frisian doublets such as *wigge/widze*, *rike/rize* and *breke/bretse* are important comparative data (pp. 182–183).

The argument is not simply that analogy is possible. Laker identifies the original conditioning environment, the morphological distribution of alternants, and the regional distribution of later outcomes.

**Critical qualification:** the historical directions and dates of individual levelling operations are not independently observed. The conclusion's claim that the Frisian evidence essentially proves Luick's analysis (p. 184) is stronger than the doublets alone warrant. The data support a plausible mechanism and weaken a purely Scandinavian explanation; other sources of lexical and dialectal variation still merit consideration.

## 7. How the evidence is deployed

Laker uses different sorts of evidence for different inferential tasks. They do not form a single undifferentiated mass of examples.

| Evidence | Where used | What it can establish | What it cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|---|
| English–Frisian comparative lexical forms | Figure 1, p. 167 | Distribution of contrasting reflexes by position and reconstructed conditioning | Precise prehistoric timing or common origin |
| Early inscriptions and names | pp. 168–169 | Approximate dates by which particular vowel developments are visible | Exact onset and end of a sound change; disputed dialect attribution |
| British place-names and map | pp. 169–172 | Geographical distribution and relation to settlement chronology | Whether each reflex reflects active sound change or substitution at borrowing |
| Loanword phonotactics | p. 172 | An independent mechanism for adapting forms to a recipient phonological system | Proof that all palatalized names were adapted by that mechanism |
| Alternative derivational displays | p. 175 | Non-unique ordering compatible with a given outcome | Which compatible historical ordering actually occurred |
| Modern Germanic dialect contrasts | pp. 177, 180–183 | Independence of processes and plausible dialectal variation | Direct reconstruction of the prehistoric distribution |
| Paradigmatic alternation and doublets | pp. 182–183 | Plausible morphological source of lexical variation | The direction and date of each proposed analogical change |

The evidence is primarily qualitative and comparative. It is not a corpus-frequency study. The map is meaningful because geographical distribution itself bears on the explanation. The derivational table is meaningful because its rows compare the operation of different ordered rules. Neither is an administrative status table.

The paper contains two particularly useful distinctions for future modelling: a productive historical process versus a subsequent phonotactic adaptation, and an initial phonetic process versus its later phonemic or dialectal outcomes. Both prevent evidence for a late reflex from being mistaken for evidence about the date of the initial innovation.

## 8. Prose and expository technique

### 8.1 The introduction makes a claim

The introduction establishes a genuine disagreement among named scholars in roughly two pages. It gives only enough intellectual history to understand that disagreement, then announces the author's judgement: three objections fail, one is inconclusive, and a continental origin is preferable. It does not spend several pages developing a neutral taxonomy of possible answers before stating a position.

The opening also credits predecessors. Fulk and Kortlandt are not treated as if they had never considered the problem. Laker's contribution is a further analysis of the evidence and reasoning, not the mere rediscovery of a controversy.

### 8.2 A characteristic argumentative paragraph

The passage on p. 175 is exemplary in its sequence:

1. Introduce a specific inference made by Stiles.
2. Identify the linguistic example on which it rests.
3. Reconstruct the competing derivations implicit in the inference.
4. Display a third derivation.
5. Identify the assumption that had excluded it.
6. Cite independent dialectal evidence against that assumption.
7. State the narrower ordering that the example actually establishes.

The third derivation is presented at the point where the reader needs it, rather than promised in an introduction and delivered several sections later. The argument becomes visible through the data and the formal alternatives.

### 8.3 Authorities are arguments, not a catalogue

The discussion of *au* (pp. 177–180) names individual scholars because they propose distinct historical mechanisms. It explains those mechanisms before evaluating them. A succession of names is not treated as evidence of consensus or correctness. The discussion of Watson and Pak (pp. 181–182) likewise asks whether a specific counterexample actually belongs to the geographical region for which it is claimed.

### 8.4 Qualifications are local and consequential

Laker distinguishes the uncertainty of the *au* problem from the other objections. The qualification affects a particular branch of the argument. He does not qualify every sentence with a reminder of the uncertainty of historical reconstruction. On the other hand, some of his positive judgements are more categorical than I would adopt: the final evaluation of levelling and the strength attributed to the geographical evidence deserve scrutiny.

### 8.5 Use of scholarly apparatus

The footnotes provide bibliographical detail, variant analyses, qualifications about dialects, source descriptions and occasional additional counterevidence. Notes 14, 19, 30, 34 and 59 are particularly instructive. Note 30 bounds the relevance of the main example to West Saxon; note 19 acknowledges British Latin influence as a possible alternative explanation; note 59 considers the uncertain interpretation of apparent palatalized spellings of ‘lark’.

These notes do not replace the main argument. The reader learns the principal evidence and conclusion in the body; details important for specialists remain traceable in the notes.

### 8.6 Style to emulate, and style not to emulate

The useful stylistic properties are explicit attribution, attention to the precise historical claim at issue, short chains of inference, actual lexical forms, and direct statements of conclusions. The technical terminology is introduced where needed. The article does not repeatedly advertise its methodological rigour.

The prose is not uniformly austere: it occasionally uses conversational phrases, rhetorical flourishes and emphatic judgements. These are not necessary to reproduce. For a CAPR-related article I would adopt the discipline of the argument without imitating such expressions, long author-by-author passages or the occasional overstatement. The aim is not to reproduce Laker's voice but to recover the effective structure of his historical reasoning.

## 9. Adversarial evaluation of the overall case

**Strongest result:** the *kerl* comparison, as analysed in §4.1, cannot uniquely order breaking and palatalization once palatal diphthongization is allowed to be distinct. This is an explicit demonstration of a defect in an earlier argument, and it survives disagreement about the broader continental-origin thesis.

**Strong secondary result:** segment-specific and dialect-specific reflexes cannot automatically be read as evidence that the initial innovations were unrelated. The paper provides concrete examples where a more differentiated historical account is independently warranted.

**Less secure positive result:** dating early phonemic palatalization on the basis of place-name adaptation and early umlaut involves assumptions about the donor pronunciations, the chronology of borrowing, recipient phonotactics and the distinction between active sound change and substitution. The alternative explanations in pp. 171–172 are not excluded by the place-name material alone. The geographical clustering is relevant but is not an independent observation of a continental process.

**Remaining historical question:** shared inheritance, early diffusion across a dialect continuum and separate innovations under similar conditions are not necessarily distinguishable from the evidence presented. The paper's broader historical preference is reasonable within its analysis, but its strongest results concern the failure of particular arguments for necessary independence.

The lesson is not that the conclusion should be weakened indiscriminately. Rather, the paper contains a definite demonstrable critical contribution alongside a less uniquely determined positive historical reconstruction. Keeping these levels distinct allows the author to make a firm contribution without pretending to have settled everything.

## 10. Transfer to the article on Old English ‘hay’

This section is my application of Laker's method to the ongoing research, not a claim that Laker discusses ‘hay’ or endorses the proposed chronology.

### 10.1 The structural analogy

The argument concerning ‘hay’ should be presented as a concrete dispute over the consequences of competing historical reconstructions. Under the early-apocope chronology, the inherited direct stem follows a different regular route from the protected stem. Under the late chronology, direct and protected forms can converge. The comparison should be shown before discussing any proposed analogy.

Like Laker's display of three *kerl* histories, a table can identify a concealed premise: the claim that a regular outcome of early apocope cannot be continued by any actual English form may depend on the assumption that the inherited hiatus necessarily contracted before the earliest witnesses, or on the assumption that the conventional Anglian length is an independently established datum.

The proposed alternatives should be explicit:

```text
A. Early apocope, inherited direct stem:
   *hawją > *hawi > *hæwi > *hewi > *he.i
   Further history: retained hiatus, possible resyllabification, or contraction.

B. Early apocope, protected-stem generalization:
   Regular direct and protected histories initially diverge;
   a separately specified analogical substitution supplies protected material.

C. Late apocope:
   Direct and protected histories converge by regular phonology.
```

These are schematic historical alternatives, not newly executed CAPR traces. The published derivational comparison must use verified traces and original source references. Unlike the *kerl* example, the crucial final resyllabification in ‘hay’ has not yet been established as an independently applicable rule. The two cases must not be conflated.

### 10.2 Establish a positive argument, then test the objections

A possible expository plan is:

1. State the chronological dispute and show the competing regular outputs and Ringe–Taylor's proposed stem substitution.
2. Establish that the written record includes forms resembling the ungeminated direct outcome, including the preferred hay interpretation of Kentish *heiweg*. Present the competing lexical explanations and their actual evidence.
3. Test the specific phonological objections: the fate of hiatus, the possible origin of the final glide, vowel quantity and West Saxon vocalism.
4. Test the morphological explanation: available protected stems, actual case forms, composition forms and the direction of levelling.
5. Adjudicate the claims separately: exact spelling correspondence, defensibility of regular continuation, necessity and scope of analogy, and the implications for the competing chronologies.

The article should not equate exact written correspondence with demonstrated phonological identity. But it should also not allow uncertainty about the compound's prehistoric grammatical stem to erase the positive fact of its probable lexical identification and orthographic correspondence.

### 10.3 Adopt Laker's local conclusions

The following are distinct possible results, requiring different evidence:

- Early apocope gives a regular intermediate *he.i*.
- The spelling ⟨hei⟩ is probably an early attestation of the hay lexeme in composition.
- The early Anglian spellings do not independently demonstrate long quantity.
- A regular continuation to /hej/ is independently defensible (still to be established).
- Particular attestations actually continue that regular form (stronger claim).
- Whole-stem levelling is not the only historically defensible account of the Anglian forms.

The failure of the fourth or fifth claim would not negate the first three. The article should articulate the consequences of those established results, much as Laker retains his critique of the first, second and fourth objections while acknowledging the remaining *au* problem.

### 10.4 A practical control for the agent's prose

For each substantive paragraph of the ‘hay’ article, require an identifiable historical function:

- **Claim:** what is being asserted?
- **Evidence:** what form, text, comparison or source supports it?
- **Warrant:** why does that evidence bear on the claim?
- **Consequence:** what alternative is excluded, weakened or supported?
- **Qualification:** what remains unresolved that could change this inference?

Not every paragraph needs all five parts. An attestation paragraph may supply evidence for a later inference, and a paragraph introducing a prior author's proposal need not adjudicate it immediately. The model should control the argument at the level of propositions and paragraphs, rather than force the published prose into a repetitive template.

For example, the model must not permit “the regular output is attested” to become an unqualified statement if the established proposition concerns a probably identified compound spelling. Conversely, it must not permit “the compound cannot prove retention of the direct stem” to become “the compound contributes no evidence”. Both transformations destroy distinctions that matter to the argument.

## 12. Overall judgement

Laker's article is valuable as a model because it takes a position, formulates the actual historical alternatives, identifies hidden assumptions and pursues specific philological objections rather than cataloguing possibilities indefinitely. Its strongest argumentative move is the demonstration that a sound-change ordering claimed to be necessary is not uniquely required by the evidence. It also shows how the rejection of several objections can coexist with an unresolved fourth question.

The most useful lesson for the ‘hay’ article is not stylistic imitation but architectural: establish the competing predictions, identify the actual observations, expose the inferential assumptions that connect them, and make a definite claim at the level the evidence warrants. The independent argument model should preserve the resulting dependencies; the published prose should make them intelligible without reproducing the internal ledger.
