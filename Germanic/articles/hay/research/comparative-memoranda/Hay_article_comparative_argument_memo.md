# The inherited direct singular of Old English ‘hay’: lessons from four historical-linguistic arguments

**Comparative research and editorial memorandum**  
25 November 2026

## 1. Assessment and recommendation

The current sixteen-page article is substantially better than the versions that preceded the four comparative analyses. It begins with the competing derivations, presents Ringe and Taylor's analogical substitution at the appropriate historical stage, separates manuscript spellings from normalized forms, and gives historical morphology a section of its own. The abstract now makes an actual historical claim: the analogical burden of ‘hay’ under early nasal apocope need not be the same as that of ‘bed’. These achievements should be retained.

Nevertheless, the article remains halfway between a collection of defensible local historical findings and a sustained demonstration of their consequences. It is particularly good at explaining why one piece of evidence is not decisive. It is less good at showing how the different pieces of evidence combine to support a preferable historical reconstruction, or why a limited conclusion would change the existing account of West Germanic chronology.

The four comparative studies provide a way forward. Ball and Stiles (1983) offer the closest model for the architecture of the paper; Laker supplies the clearest model of a criticism of an allegedly necessary chronological inference; Versloot (2026) demonstrates the value of comparing independently specified historical systems; and Ritt (2000) shows how to question an interpretation of manuscript evidence without rejecting the evidence itself. Ball and Stiles also supply a substantive piece of scholarship currently absent from the article: their explanation of the direction of levelling in Old English mass nouns (1983: 18).

**Recommendation:** do not commission another indiscriminate rewrite. First revise the model of the historical argument, giving it a definite hierarchy of conclusions. Then develop a small number of component arguments that remain inadequately substantiated, particularly the positive case for the inherited direct stem, the Kentish compound as evidence of an early stem shape, and the morphological comparison with ‘meal’ and ‘fat’. Rewrite only after deciding what each component contributes. The present paper's central result is potentially publishable as a reassessment of the necessity and scope of analogy; it does not yet demonstrate the actual survival of an unlevelled Old English simplex.

## 2. What the four papers do differently

| Paper | Problem that organizes the investigation | Principal intellectual operation | Strength of the result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Laker, *Palatalization of Velars*, pp. 165–184 | Do incompatible English and Frisian chronologies exclude a shared origin? | Separate processes conflated by earlier accounts; exhibit a third derivation; evaluate objections individually. | Demonstrates that particular objections do not establish necessary independence; supplies separate positive grounds for preferring shared origins. |
| Versloot 2026, pp. 103–141 | Do the earliest glosses require general fronting and early breaking followed by reversal? | Specify rival systems, compare their outputs over the same evidence, then investigate discriminating discrepancies. | Establishes a serious alternative and grounds for historical preference; the precise breaking mechanism remains open. |
| Ball & Stiles 1983, pp. 5–28 | Is the apparently early back-mutated *geolu* compatible with smoothing before back-mutation? | Establish the broader distribution, isolate the exceptional lexeme, reconstruct its paradigm and explain its development. | Proposes breaking before *lw*, explains the lexical anomaly and derives consequences for the chronology. |
| Ritt 2000, pp. 497–512 | Do Lindisfarne accents establish different dates for two lengthenings? | Expose assumptions connecting accents to quantity and construct a rival context-sensitive interpretation. | Removes a purportedly decisive objection to unified quantity adjustment without proving that account historically correct. |

All four papers make a definite contribution, but their contributions are not all of the same logical kind. This matters for the hay paper. A demonstration that an argument for necessary levelling is invalid, a historically motivated alternative to levelling, and a demonstration that the inherited direct stem actually survives are three different achievements. The first can be valuable without the third. The second requires more than pointing to the limitations of the received interpretation.

The papers do not all attain the same standard of evidence. The earlier analyses identified problems in Versloot's grouped predictive score, Ritt's numerical validation, Laker's more extensive geographical inference, and Ball and Stiles's explanation of the direction of levelling by frequency. We should emulate their ability to articulate historical problems, specify hypotheses and identify consequential evidence, not assume that the confident statements of established specialists are automatically correct.

### 2.1 Their shared expository principle

Each paper gives the reader a problem before giving the reader a large quantity of evidence. Individual details then have a purpose. Laker's three derivations on p. 175 make sense because the reader understands which chronological claim is being challenged. Ball and Stiles's appendix on *bearug* matters because an inherited medial vowel would support an apparent counterexample to their chronology. Ritt's accent inventory matters because the conventional interpretation of its omissions has already been shown to have a particular historical consequence. Versloot's discrepancies are interesting because the two systems have been specified before their comparison.

The present hay article has largely adopted this principle at the level of the introduction. Table 1 and §1.3 (pp. 2–3) now establish the competing regular histories and the alternative analogical history. But the principle should also govern each of its subsequent sections. At the end of a discussion of a manuscript, a comparative form or a grammatical cell, the reader ought to understand exactly which historical hypothesis has been strengthened, weakened, or left unchanged.

## 3. Diagnosis of the current article

### 3.1 What is already working

The most substantial advance is the opening comparison. Table 1 distinguishes early and late apocope, vocalization of stranded *j*, gemination, labial resolution, fronting, umlaut and *w*-loss. It explicitly identifies the early output as syllabic /he.i/ and the late output as a long nucleus followed by /j/. Section 1.3 correctly presents Ringe and Taylor's replacement of direct *hæwi by protected *hēaj before umlaut (citing their pp. 53, 173, 246). That is a much better exposition than presenting their eventual English outcomes as the regular direct-cell prediction of their chronology.

The evidence section also has real philological substance. Section 2.2 (pp. 4–5) evaluates the etymology of *heiweg* in prose, rather than mechanically setting out opinions in a matrix. Its conclusion that ‘hay-road’ is preferable is an identifiable historical judgement. Section 2.3 distinguishes the early compound from thirteenth-century Worcester *hei*. The later spelling is securely a hay-word but does not constitute a further early Old English simplex witness. The manuscript-based status of Eadwine ⟨hieg⟩ and the editorial status of conventional *hīeġ* are kept separate (p. 4).

Section 3 has made progress beyond merely listing imperfect parallels: it combines hiatus retention, desyllabification and an independently existing /Vj/ syllable structure (p. 6). Section 4 reconstructs the protected cells and provides a usable census of the actual grammatical evidence (Tables 5–6, pp. 8–9). Section 5 then distinguishes regular survival, contraction with restoration and protected-stem substitution, separately for Anglian and West Saxon. Finally, §6 explains why the contrast with ‘bed’ matters. These are the proper components of the article.

### 3.2 What is not yet fully achieved

The text states several versions of its main conclusion in the abstract, §1.3, §5.1, §6 and §7. Some formulations are more ambitious than others. The conclusion on pp. 12–13 is that the Anglian evidence does not require the transfer of the protected nucleus, while West Saxon high vocalism favours it under early apocope. That is a good thesis. But the opening describes the regular early history as “ending in disyllabic *he.i*”, which can be read as a prediction of the language of the ninth century rather than the endpoint of a specified implementation. In reality, the treatment of this sequence after *w*-loss is the very historical question the paper investigates. The word *output* must always be qualified by the temporal or computational endpoint.

A second problem is the statement on p. 11 that the most economical reading is that Anglian *e* “need not have been imported”. This is a negative claim about the necessity of replacement, not a comparative judgement that regular survival actually occurred. The paragraph then says that regular survival has a positive historical basis without ranking it above contraction and restoration. A reader needs to know what, exactly, is being preferred: a short-vowel history, an inherited nucleus regardless of quantity, or simply the rejection of obligatory full-stem substitution. These are not the same position.

A third problem is that the article has no sufficiently developed synthesis of its new morphological evidence and the independent phonological evidence. On pp. 9–10, the possibility of retention of a frequent direct stem is set against the plausibility of a protected donor, and the section ends without making much use of that comparison in §5. Ball and Stiles provide precisely the missing disciplinary precedent. Their historical account can turn the morphology from a statement about the incompleteness of the evidence into a reason to investigate the opposite direction of levelling.

The article should not become shorter or longer simply to satisfy an editorial target. In its present form, the substantive discussion occupies about twelve and a half pages, with an appendix and three pages of bibliography. The appropriate expansion is of the actual reasoning in §§3–5, not of the inventory of qualifications.

## 4. Four lessons applied to particular parts of the hay article

### 4.1 Laker: identify the assumption that makes the received inference appear necessary

Laker's most useful passage is his comparison of three derivations of ‘churl’ (p. 175). Stiles's objection assumes that palatalization and palatal diphthongization must be adjacent. Laker separates them on independent dialectological grounds, and the previously excluded chronology becomes possible. This is not just a demonstration that an additional formal sequence can be written down: the separation is motivated by the different distribution of the changes.

The corresponding operation for ‘hay’ is to distinguish the historical origin of its **nucleus** from the historical origin of its **final palatal segment**. The traditional substitution before umlaut changes both at once. But neither their phonological histories nor their subsequent analogical histories need be identical.

Under the early chronology with a consonantal reconstruction, the relevant derivation is:

```
*hawją > *hawj > *hawi > *hæwi > *hewi > *he.i
```

The protected stem independently develops towards *hēaj-. Ringe and Taylor posit substitution of this stem for *hæwi before umlaut. A later Anglian mid-vowel outcome can consequently originate in either historical nucleus. To infer that *all* Anglian direct forms must continue the protected stem, the reconstruction must show that the inherited nucleus cannot supply their vowel, or that an independently necessary operation also replaces the vowel when supplying the final consonant.

Three candidate early-apocope histories should therefore be distinguished:

- Regular survival of inherited *he.i, possibly through desyllabification to *hej.
- Regular contraction of inherited *he.i to *hē, followed by secondary addition or restoration of /j/.
- Ringe and Taylor's whole-stem substitution before umlaut.

These differ in the chronological origin of the stressed nucleus. The first two share a historical claim about the inherited vowel, even though they differ in the origin of the glide. The first need not be established for the second to constitute an alternative to obligatory whole-stem replacement.

This is already implicit in §5.1 of the current article, but it should become an explicit organizing argument. One should not use an assumed long vowel to rule out regular survival and then regard the exclusion of that hypothesis as confirmation of length. Likewise, contraction to *hē does not automatically show that the protected vowel was generalized. The crucial limitation of this argument is that the possibility of a smaller analogical operation is not, by itself, evidence that it happened. Campbell's interpretation of *glī/glīg* supplies a possible precedent; Brunner's rival interpretation prevents it from being treated as an independently demonstrated restoration (current §4.4, pp. 10–11).

**What to adopt from Laker:** present a branching historical comparison with explicitly different assumptions, then establish the narrower result that survives if a branch fails. If a critic rejects resyllabification, the argument against obligatory transfer of the Anglian nucleus may still be maintained through the contraction-and-restoration branch. If the latter also proves inadequately motivated, the historical output and the absence of independent evidence of long quantity remain relevant observations, but the case against whole-stem replacement is correspondingly weaker.

### 4.2 Versloot: a competing model has to predict something, not simply accommodate a spelling

Versloot's paper establishes two systems before comparing their performance, and it examines the words that distinguish the systems rather than relying on their overall success rates. The alternative does not have to settle whether early lengthening or restricted fronting created the input to later breaking in order to make a case for the later chronology. Its constituent hypotheses, however, must be specified and independently motivated.

The present hay article successfully reproduces the first feature in Table 1. Its next step should be to compare the predictions of the *complete candidate historical histories*, not merely their different formal endpoints. The following table is a proposed internal diagnostic, not a table to reproduce wholesale in the publication.

| Observation | Regular early survival | Early contraction + glide restoration | Early protected-stem substitution | Late regular development |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mid-ninth-century Kentish ⟨hei⟩, if ‘hay’ | Compatible with hiatus or /ej/, conditional on composition history | Compatible if restoration and appropriate spelling have occurred | Compatible with a Kentish mid vowel and palatal glide; long ⟨ei⟩ not independently calibrated here | Same spelling possibility as protected-stem substitution |
| Early Mercian ⟨heg⟩ in direct syntax | Requires desyllabification to /ej/ or independently established hiatus notation | Fits if contraction and restoration have occurred | Fits ordinary Anglian outcome | Fits ordinary Anglian outcome |
| Farman's accented ⟨hóeg⟩ | Less favourable to unlengthened /ej/ | Length and palatal predicted | Length and palatal predicted | Length and palatal predicted |
| Secure WS ⟨hig/hyg⟩ | No independently supported high-vowel outcome from the proposed *he.i route | Restoring /j/ does not explain high vocalism | High vocalism expected | High vocalism expected |
| Three Paris datives ⟨hege⟩ | Compatible with protected or subsequently remodelled obliques | Compatible | Compatible | Compatible |
| OHG *hewi* beside *houwi* | Inherited ungeminated direct possible under early apocope | Same | Same | Requires independent explanation of ungeminated form |

The table exposes two issues. First, there is presently no single English spelling that uniquely chooses early rather than late apocope. Secondly, the early chronology admits histories that differ across dialects. The West Saxon word is not an automatic refutation of the proposal for early Mercian; it is evidence about the West Saxon history. Conversely, the possibility of survival in a Kentish compound does not prove survival in the Mercian simplex.

The strongest positive evidence for a distinct regular history would be a secure early direct form with a demonstrably short vowel or retained hiatus, contrasted with a protected form or a dialectally diagnostic alternate stem. A securely identified exact ⟨hei⟩ simplex would be useful but would still require phonological interpretation. The present evidence gives a combination of motivation, spelling compatibility and reasons to reconsider the conventional universal account. A model should state which further observation would actually discriminate among the candidates.

The Versloot analysis also warns against confusing different units of evaluation. Its recalculation distinguishes 174/199 matched grouped outcomes (87.4%) from 143/199 individual majority-class classifications (71.9%) in the published mutation appendix. Similarly, a CAPR output matching the letters of a compound member is not a verified simplex derivation; 123 occurrences of a noun are not 123 independent witnesses to its historical stem; and a benchmark score against normalized West Saxon forms cannot settle a claim about possible Anglian survival. Keep these comparisons separate. The article need not discuss software scores at all.

### 4.3 Ball and Stiles: use the lexical anomaly to change the broader history

Ball and Stiles begin with the manuscript *goelu* of the Leiden Riddle, conventionally emended to *geolu*, and demonstrate why its historical interpretation matters to the ordering of smoothing and back-mutation. They first establish a distribution in the early texts; then they remove the principal apparent exception by reconstructing the adjective's *wa*-stem paradigm and proposing breaking before *lw*. Their comparison with *giefu* is diagnostic because the traditional account would predict comparable outcomes under comparable conditions (pp. 15–16). They return to the chronology after explaining the lexical problem. They do not merely offer an isolated alternative etymology.

The opening of the hay paper should have the same concreteness. The actual historical discrepancy is that the regular early chronology gives *hei* at the hiatus stage, whereas the conventional English citation form is *hīeġ*. An early Kentish text contains *hei-* in a likely hay-compound; early Anglian simplex witnesses give *heg*. Why should the former be dismissed as irrelevant to the inherited stem, and the latter automatically assigned to the protected stem? That question should connect the derivational comparison to the subsequent evidence, rather than appear only in §5 after several pages of description.

Ball and Stiles also show the value of an independently motivated comparison that supplies a positive explanation. In the hay case, Hogg's *clēa*, the evidence of *sīem* and the ordinary /Vj/ structure provide three distinct pieces of motivation. They should not be mistaken for a matched example of *e.i > ej*. But their combination gives a proposed mechanism to examine. The author should specify why the historical environment permits desyllabification, what would make that mechanism fail, and what distinguishes its prediction from contraction. Ball and Stiles's proposed breaking before *lw* is itself not beyond challenge: two of their supporting lexical examples belong to the same word family and the history of ‘meal’ involves competing analogical explanations. This is a warning against treating a related form as wholly independent confirmation.

#### The substantive morphological result to incorporate immediately

Ball & Stiles (1983: 18, §4.3) explicitly compare the distribution of monophthongal and diphthongal stems of ‘yellow’ with that of ‘meal, flour’. On their account, the direct singular *melu of the uncountable noun was sufficiently frequent relative to protected *meolw- for unbroken *melw- to spread into inflected forms before back-mutation. Subsequent back-mutation and reciprocal analogical influence produce the double paradigm. They extend this reasoning to ‘fat’, with its *smer(u)w-* forms, and qualify it with ‘tar’ in n. 18. This is particularly close to the historical possibility entertained for ‘hay’.

There are two advantages to citing this precedent. First, the suggestion that the direct singular of a mass noun might resist remodelling or spread into oblique functions is no longer presented as a general impression about likely usage. It is a specific, published hypothesis concerning stem alternation in Old English itself. Secondly, Ball and Stiles give us a way to formulate a genuine historical contrast between opposite directions of levelling.

The comparison has limits. ‘Meal’ and ‘fat’ are *wa*-stems, not the same light neuter *ja*-stem class as ‘hay’. Their attested variant oblique stems are more informative than the three undiagnostic datives of ‘hay’. The relative frequency of prehistoric stems is inferred rather than measured, and their account of the doublets itself depends on the proposed breaking chronology. We should not take a conjectured analogue as proof of the same operation in ‘hay’. Nevertheless, this is the single most promising new scholarly connection produced by the four analyses. It belongs in §4.2, not merely in a bibliography or a footnote, and should inform the synthesis in §5.

### 4.4 Ritt: distinguish what a manuscript records from the historical interpretation assigned to it

Ritt's important point is that the established account of Early English lengthening already influenced the interpretation of the Lindisfarne accents. The absence of a marking indicating length was interpreted as evidence for shortness in one context, thereby apparently confirming the chronology that motivated the interpretation. Ritt formulates an alternative in which accents mark otherwise unexpected length. The same distribution then ceases to be decisive.

The equivalent issue for ‘hay’ is not simply whether the noun is accented. Ordinary ⟨heg⟩ does not mark a quantity contrast; *hēġ* is a reconstructed citation form. If the long vowel follows from derivation of the protected stem, the normalized vowel cannot be counted again as independent evidence that the protected stem supplied the form. This is a clear and important argument and should be stated once, precisely, before the evidence is examined.

The present Table 4 (p. 7) is an improvement on the type-based reasoning criticized in the earlier cycles. Its data include unaccented controls. There are 2/2 accented target tokens, 1/26 among selected closed monosyllables with short vowel and glide, and 1/14 in the related long-ī noun/verb group. There is no genuinely matched long-vowel-plus-glide monosyllabic control. The short and long panels cannot establish a calibrated likelihood ratio for the target. The absence of an acute in Vespasian does not establish short quantity; the presence of both acutes and Farman's ⟨oe⟩ spelling favours length in his own gloss, but cannot date the development in all Mercian varieties. The lexical and textual dependence of the two target tokens also matters.

The Ritt analysis shows why we must avoid treating 318 of 426 accents in graphically closed syllables as an accentuation rate of closed syllables; it is the distribution *among accented items*. His second claimed validation is numerically inconsistent (p. 508: 21 long-vowel items, 15 with accented variants, but eight remaining). Neither issue negates his logical objection to an inference from lack of marking. Cite Ritt for that distinction, not for a supposed 98% reliability of accents. The present paper handles this reasonably well already. A short paragraph may be sufficient; there is no need to reproduce Ritt's entire argument or repeat the Farman calibration.

**What to adopt from Ritt:** treat vowel quantity as a distinct inference with an explicitly defined evidential basis. Do not impose a single interpretation of accentuation across Aldred, Farman and Vespasian. The positive result is that the evidence does not require the same historical quantity in all witnesses, not that any unaccented form has been shown to be short.

## 5. Where the historical contribution should now lie

The article should distinguish three levels of claim rather than oscillate between them.

**Level I: the established result.** The two chronologies make different regular predictions under the stated consonantal analysis. The early regular history gives *he.i before subsequent hiatus resolution; the late regular history produces the protected type of nucleus and glide. Ringe and Taylor's actual analysis of English introduces a substitution before umlaut. The early Kentish compound has an exact written counterpart to the early output, conditional on its preferable lexical interpretation as ‘hay’; the early Anglian simplex spellings do not independently establish the conventional long quantity. This is a concrete historical problem, not an invitation to select a preferred software output.

**Level II: the historical reassessment.** The Anglian mid vowel is independently available from the inherited direct history. Contraction with subsequent limited palatal restoration gives a possible history of a long form without transfer of the protected nucleus. Desyllabification supplies a historically motivated, but less securely established, history of a short form. The conventional full-stem replacement is therefore not uniquely required by the evidence in the way that a nonregular geminate requires explanation under early apocope for *bedd*. The argument does not establish that the smaller operation is what happened. Its force depends on whether the relevant alternative is sufficiently motivated, not merely conceivable. West Saxon high vocalism provides a real constraint and makes protected vocalism preferable there under early apocope.

**Level III: the stronger claim to investigate.** Some early Old English forms actually preserve the inherited direct stem without analogy. This requires a satisfactory historical account of /he.i/ and the written evidence and a reason to prefer that account over the remodelled or late-apocope alternatives. The current text does not yet establish this. It can be proposed as a serious historical possibility without promoting it to the conclusion. It is worth investigating whether *heiweg* retains an old composition form, but a composition form is not an attested nominative–accusative singular.

The paper's central contribution should presently be formulated at Level II, supported by Level I. This is an intellectually definite claim. Its implications are that the usual interpretation of ‘hay’ as a further instance of the same problem posed by ‘bed’ needs revision, and that the reconstruction of the relative analogical requirements of the two chronologies should be reconsidered. The claim does not entail that the early chronology is generally correct. In particular, continental ungeminated *beti*-type forms and *mene* retain their own historical problems, and Gothic *hawi* cannot adjudicate a specifically West Germanic chronology.

A suitable title would be **Old English ‘hay’ and the scope of paradigmatic levelling under early nasal apocope**. The existing title is acceptable but less explicit about the wider issue. Another possibility is **Did the inherited direct stem of Old English ‘hay’ survive?** The former more accurately reflects a paper whose strongest result concerns the scope of the analogy rather than demonstrating a particular unlevelled token.

## 6. Targeted substantive work, rather than another general research cycle

### 6.1 Give *heiweg* an appropriately positive, independent role

The current §2.2 has already reached a reasoned lexical preference. Preserve the conjunction of the meadow setting and local ⟨ei⟩ for ‘day’, the evaluation of Cooper's contextual and documentary concerns, and the lack of a matched local development from *hēah*. Do not claim that Langlands demonstrates the function of the S 1196 route: the Mower's Lane comparison concerns S 445, transmitted in a late copy. Nor should Cooper's exclusion of S 1196 be mistaken for ignorance of it. The current account largely makes these distinctions correctly.

The remaining problem is the *relative prediction* of the two histories. The early development supplies the written sequence ⟨ei⟩ naturally, but the local *dei* evidence specifically establishes its ability to represent /ej/, not disyllabic /e.i/. The protected history may also supply a Kentish mid-vowel reflex, as the article recognizes from *ċēgan*, and use ⟨ei⟩ for the palatal sequence. Neither long ⟨ei⟩ nor hiatus ⟨ei⟩ is calibrated by a directly matched local control in the paper. A comparison must not count the spelling as a unique success of the early model while treating all phonological details of the late model as assumptions and the corresponding details of the early model as observed.

This leaves a real positive result: if ‘hay-road’ is correct, ⟨hei⟩ is a probable ninth-century spelling of the lexical stem. It makes the regular early reconstruction worth investigating in the actual record. It is not just a hypothetical unattested string. The historical identity of that composition stem is a further question. Use the surviving mid-ninth-century sheet as the chronological reference; use the conventional transaction date with its qualification. The paper should retain the forthcoming Wallenberg and Brooks & Kelly checks as bounded uncertainties, not make their absence an excuse for another general highway survey. The current PDF reports no second early exact simplex; do not imply there is one.

### 6.2 Formulate a testable historical proposal for the final vowel

The key phonological argument in §3.2 is now more substantial than the earlier claim that /e.i/ > /ej/ is merely conceivable. Strengthen it by distinguishing the jobs done by each control:

| Comparison | Inference supported | Inference not supported |
|---|---|---|
| *glī* from *gliwi | Final identical-vowel hiatus can contract after *w*-loss. | Date of contraction of unlike /e.i/. |
| *ēde*, *streide*, Vespasian *gecēd* | Unlike /e.i/ can contract; some nonfinal hiatus persists in early texts. | Necessarily the same treatment and date for final /e.i/. |
| *sǣ*, *ǣ* | Final unlike-vowel contraction after *w*-loss has comparanda. | Exact development of a short stressed *e* in the target environment. |
| Vespasian *sīem* | Front-vowel hiatus could persist within this textual tradition. | Retention or desyllabification of final /e.i/. |
| *clēa*, *cnēo*, *trēo* | Desyllabification after consonant loss is a historically attested process in the cited analyses. | A front high vowel necessarily becomes coda /j/ in the target. |
| *weġ*, *dei/deg* | /Vj/ structures and their spelling conventions already exist. | The historical creation of /j/ from syllabic /i/. |

A candidate rule should specify the stressed nucleus, unstressed high vowel, word boundary and relative order with contraction, and distinguish desyllabification from the creation of a new phonemic diphthong. It must also specify its possible domain: is it a general Old English change, a late development in some Anglian varieties, or a lexical development? An otherwise unmatched hypothesis restricted only to ‘hay’ is weak. Identify relevant counterexamples and the conditions that would block the proposed development. Do not require a phonologically identical cognate to consider it motivated, but do not assign it the status of an established law without an independently justified environment. The nearest final unlike-vowel contraction comparanda deserve equal attention.

The evidence need not settle the precise mechanism for Level II of the thesis. If no new discriminating comparison is available after this tightly defined investigation, conclude that the alternative is motivated at the level of mechanism but not independently established in its precise environment. Do not repeat a general search or write another catalogue of possibilities.

### 6.3 Bring the morphology into the historical explanation

The grammatical census in Table 6 is one of the original achievements of the paper. It should be interpreted with greater precision. It contains 123 included occurrences; 66 have edition, apparatus or image verification; 51 are assigned syntactic case (36 verified). Of the 51 assigned, 47 are direct singular (2 nominatives and 45 accusatives), and four are datives (one endingless, three overt). There are 68 unresolved case assignments and four isolated glossary equivalents. The 24 distinguishable source groups are not 24 independent innovations. The earliest securely attested accusative is ninth-century Vespasian. The two secure nominatives are in related tenth-century John glosses. The three overt datives occur in one mid-eleventh-century manuscript; the secure endingless dative has overlapping manuscript dating. No secure genitive or plural appears in the census.

These figures do not demonstrate prehistoric frequency. In particular, the fraction 47/51 is a distribution among classified tokens, not among all Old English uses. The evidence does establish that handbook paradigms contain reconstructed cells for which no direct witness has been found. It also establishes the coexistence of an overt dative ending and an endingless use in the later record, rather than original indeclinability. The correct inference concerns the evidential status of the proposed donor: a protected stem is historically reconstructible, but its extension into the direct singular is not attested as an event.

Bring this result into contact with Ball and Stiles's argument. Their proposed direct-to-protected levelling provides a concrete comparative reason to consider survival or extension of the direct stem in an uncountable noun. The different semantic distributions of ‘yellow’, ‘meal’, ‘fat’ and possibly ‘tar’ are useful as a structured comparison. Discuss the alternative that multiple protected cells and pressure from related uniform paradigms might nevertheless favour the other direction. Acknowledge that the three *hege* datives have no diagnostic vowel length and cannot identify the donor's shape before umlaut.

A full idealized paradigm is useful for the reader, not as an attestation claim. Table 5 currently lists the direct singular, genitive singular, dative singular and direct plural because they are the relevant protected environments. If the aim is to compare the complete textbook paradigm to the record, label the table as a selected reconstruction or add the predicted genitive and dative plural after verifying the inflectional analysis. Use explicit unattested entries in the attestation table. The earlier request to show every cell should not be satisfied by silently implying that absent cells were never part of the language.

### 6.4 Keep the comparative ‘glee’ argument honest and useful

Section 4.4 correctly separates Campbell's re-formation of *glīg* from Brunner's alternative involving inherited palatal material. This is a useful example of the broader lesson from Laker: different reconstructions can produce observationally similar outcomes, but differ in the history of individual segments. It prevents us from presenting the *glī/glīg* doublet as incontrovertible evidence of final-glide restoration.

The next revision should ask what follows *under each analysis* for ‘hay’. Campbell's proposal establishes a scholarly precedent for a nucleus-preserving re-formation if its analysis is accepted. Brunner's proposal establishes an alternative way of preserving the palatal in a different inherited representation. It cannot be appended to a cascade in which *j has already become syllabic *i without changing that representation. Both demonstrate why the origin of the nucleus and palatal segment should be treated separately. Neither independently establishes restoration or desyllabification in ‘hay’. This is a more useful comparative result than claiming that the doublets prove any particular analogical operation.

## 7. Independent model of the historical argument

The argument model must not be a section list or a catalogue of observations. It should record the dependencies of the conclusions, alternative paths, the status of each premise, and the location of the evidence. The following is a proposed backbone to compare against the existing internal model. It is not a replacement for source-level verification or a prescribed historical conclusion.

```text
Q: Is universal protected-stem substitution necessary under early nasal apocope?
|
+-- P: Derive the distinct regular histories

|   +-- E3: ordinary spellings do not establish length

|

|   +-- M3: mass-noun comparison motivates possible direct-stem persistence

    +-- C3: R2 OR R3 permits retention of inherited nucleus (P3, P4, R)
```

The distinction between C5 and C6 is essential. C5 is an inferential reassessment of the necessity of whole-stem replacement within the early chronology. C6 asserts what actually happened. C7 depends on C5, not necessarily C6. The protected history remains possible. Neither C5 nor C7 independently establishes the early order itself.

For each premise in the complete model retain a stable identifier, exact source and locus, status, assumptions, counterevidence and dependencies. The following are the crucial points of failure to test:

| If this proposition fails… | What must change? | What survives? |
|---|---|---|
| S 1196 contains ‘hay’ | Remove the early exact spelling correspondence and the preference for an old hay composition form. | Regular early derivation; independently identified Anglian simplex evidence; the quantity and morphology arguments. |
| Desyllabification to /ej/ is historically inadmissible | Remove the proposed short-vowel regular simplex route. | Contraction with restoration may preserve the inherited nucleus; the spelling evidence still lacks an independent length determination. |
| Final contraction predates early Vespasian | Eliminate retained hiatus there unless independently justified lexical or dialectal variation exists. | A long inherited nucleus may survive; glide origin remains a separate question. |

This is the control the article has needed. It lets new evidence change only the dependent claims. It also prevents the argument from treating lack of proof of one route as proof of its rival. The internal model should be maintained in Markdown (or a structured format with a readable Markdown representation) separately from the article outline. Every major assertion in the abstract and conclusion should have a mapped conclusion in the model. For each such assertion, audit both the necessary premises and the location in the text where they are defended.

## 8. Proposed expository outline

Use the architecture of Ball and Stiles as the principal model, with Laker's critical alternatives and Versloot's comparison embedded within it. The existing seven-section organization is fundamentally sound. It needs a clearer progression, not another complete change in headings.

1. **Introduction: two chronologies and a lexical problem.** Begin with the exact divergent regular predictions and the ninth-century spelling that motivates the question. Present Table 1 and the third, analogical history. End by stating the thesis about the scope of replacement. Do not promise to solve every question of Old English hiatus or West Germanic chronology.
2. **The Old English evidence.** Present the principal simplex forms. Give *heiweg* its own sustained philological argument, then distinguish the later exact ⟨hei⟩ evidence. The reader should leave this section understanding why the Kentish compound and the Mercian simplex each matter for different reasons. Place lengthy source details in notes or Appendix A.
3. **Historical phonology.** Establish the regular direct history, consider the competing treatments of the resulting hiatus, and assess orthography and quantity separately. End with the independently established constraints on each dialectal history. Integrate Ritt's caution where it bears on a specific inference. Do not reiterate the source inventory from §2.
4. **Historical morphology.** Reconstruct the actual alternation, contrast the textbook and attested paradigms, then discuss direction of levelling with Ball and Stiles and compare *glee*. Evaluate composition morphology here. End by specifying what the evidence contributes to the proposed donor and its scope.
5. **Historical interpretation of ‘hay’.** This is the argumentative centre. Compare the plausible complete histories of the earliest forms using the results of §§2–4. Explain why the protected nucleus is not an independently necessary source of the Anglian vowel, and why West Saxon is different. State a comparative preference only where evidence warrants it. Make the distinction between a reasonable proposed survival and an established survival explicit once.
6. **Implications for the chronology.** Return to *bedd*, *nett*, *cynn*, *mene* and the continental direct-form candidates. Show why removal of an alleged requirement for nucleus replacement in ‘hay’ changes the explanatory comparison but does not decide the chronology. Keep Dahl's representational alternative distinct. The reader should understand the significance of the lexical research beyond a single etymology.
7. **Conclusion.** State what has been established, the narrower historical alternative that is now defensible and the principal remaining discriminator. Do not reproduce the entire discussion of manuscript limitations. A few precise paragraphs should suffice.

## 9. Prose and presentation: what to retain and what to change

The four comparison papers do not share a uniform style. Laker is openly argumentative, Versloot is explicit about the testing programme, Ball and Stiles use a traditional philological sequence of tightly linked problems and remarks, and Ritt writes a methodological essay around a manuscript case. The appropriate model is not to imitate their individual mannerisms, but to adopt the work performed by their prose.

Ball and Stiles are especially useful because each paragraph changes the historical problem. A reading is introduced; its expected history is explained; a contrast requires explanation; a new interpretation is supplied; a comparative precedent is tested. A paragraph has a historical conclusion rather than a general evaluation of the complexity of the evidence. Laker similarly presents the third derivation at the moment it is needed. This is the standard to apply to the present paper.

### Example: revise the opening argument rather than merely polish it

The following is an illustrative draft, not a final replacement. Its historical limits are deliberate:

> The relative chronology of West Germanic gemination and the loss of final nasal *-ą determines whether the nominative–accusative singular of a light neuter *ja*-stem underwent gemination. Under the order proposed by Ringe and Taylor (2014: 44–53, 173), *hawją ‘hay’ regularly developed through *hawi and *hewi to *he.i. Under the later order represented by Hogg and Fulk (2011: §2.23), gemination preceded loss of the ending, and the resulting stem supplies the vocalism and final palatal of conventional West Saxon *hīeġ*. The difference is illustrated in Table 1. Ringe and Taylor derive the latter type of stem by substituting the protected *hēaj- for the inherited direct *hæwi before umlaut (2014: 246).
>
> The Old English evidence does not uniformly demand the vocalism of the protected stem. In the mid-ninth-century Kentish charter S 1196, *heiweg*, preferably ‘hay-road’, contains the sequence ⟨hei⟩ predicted by the regular early development. The Vespasian Psalter writes *heg*, including in direct-object constructions, without independently indicating vowel length. This article examines whether the Anglian vowel can continue that of the inherited direct stem, and whether the West Saxon forms demand the same historical account. The distinction matters because the proposed analogy in ‘hay’ need not have the same scope as that invoked to explain the geminate in *bedd*.

This paragraph puts the actual facts and the named scholarly claim together. It avoids the suggestion that the frozen output is already a verified ninth-century pronunciation. In the final text, distinguish reconstructed forms typographically with one initial asterisk, give the exact model stages, and use `>` for historical development, not arrows. Represent analogy explicitly as stem substitution, not as an ordinary sound change.

### Example: strengthen the morphological paragraph

In §4.2, the existing discussion of mass-noun usage could develop as follows (subject to checking the comparative source and the precise forms):

> Ball and Stiles (1983: 18) distinguish the morphological history of *geolu* ‘yellow’ from that of *me(o)lu* ‘meal’. They attribute the coexistence of monophthongal and diphthongal stems of the latter noun to its use as an uncountable singular: the direct form *melu supplied an unbroken oblique *melw-, despite the regular breaking of the protected stem. They offer a similar account of ‘fat’. The proposed direction of levelling is the reverse of that invoked for ‘hay’ by Ringe and Taylor. Although the nouns belong to different stem classes, they illustrate the relevance of the relative frequency of the stem alternants. In the case of ‘hay’, the attested oblique forms do not identify an early protected donor, and the predominance of endingless singular forms is compatible with the persistence of the inherited direct stem. The comparison does not establish direct-to-oblique levelling in ‘hay’; it provides a morphological reason not to assume the opposite direction without further evidence.

The useful final sentence is a specific historical inference, not a general admonition about uncertainty. Check the account against Ball and Stiles's original text before incorporating it.

### Example: tighten the conclusion of §5.1

The current prose first describes an economical reading, then denies it preference over competing accounts. This can be sharpened by distinguishing the thesis that has been established from the rival explanations that remain.

> Both the regular direct history and the protected history can supply the Anglian mid vowel. If *he.i contracted, only the source of the final palatal remains to be explained; if the unstressed vowel became nonsyllabic, the entire direct form could have survived. The latter development has independent structural parallels, but is not established for this precise environment. No corresponding regular derivation of the high vocalism of West Saxon *hig/hyg* has been identified. Consequently, the evidence for transfer of protected vocalism in West Saxon cannot be extended to Anglian solely on the evidence of ⟨heg⟩.

This is not a claim that both histories have equal probability. It establishes a narrower result by identifying which feature discriminates and which does not. If further comparative work allows a preference, state that preference in an additional sentence and give the evidence that warrants it.

## 11. Final judgement

We do not need to write a new paper from scratch. The latest version now contains most of the right evidence and has the proper broad architecture. The main advance that the four comparative studies suggest is to make the article's historical inference cumulative and hierarchical, rather than continually qualifying the interpretation of one form after another.

Ball and Stiles are the most useful immediate model because their paper makes a lexical problem alter an argument about relative chronology. Their published explanation of mass-noun stem alternation also gives the hay article a specific morphological comparison it currently lacks. Laker supplies the formal structure for challenging the necessity of a historical inference without overclaiming the truth of a rival. Versloot supplies the comparison of predictions, with the reminder that explanatory success must be measured at the appropriate level. Ritt supplies the critique of reading reconstructed vowel quantity directly out of manuscript notation.

The strongest current contribution is not that early apocope has been proved correct, nor that the exact regular inherited simplex has been conclusively found. It is that the independent histories of nucleus and palatal in ‘hay’, the early written counterpart in the probably hay-related Kentish compound, and the non-diagnostic quantity of the earliest Anglian forms together justify reconsideration of the traditional uniform protected-stem analysis. A serious alternative history of the Anglian nucleus reduces the explanatory requirement of early apocope without requiring that the West Saxon forms be explained away. The next revision should demonstrate this result with conviction, and then make the stronger case for regular survival only to the degree that its actual historical premises warrant it.
