# ‘Couch’: witnesses and grammatical cells

## C1: identification and method

The test is whether the evidence supplies actual, reconstructible cells
for an independent nasal control rather than a convenient hypothetical
direct singular. This checkpoint collates the attestation and grammatical
evidence; etymological preference and executable reconstructions are
separate decisions. See [the witness inventory](couch-attestations.tsv)
for edition/manuscript status. A blank manuscript reading means that
the target was not checked in a manuscript image, not that it was
missing in the witness.

## Vespasian: four loci, one tradition

Sweet's *Oldest English Texts* (1885), pp. 192, 243, 272 and 379,
prints **strene at all four loci**: Pss 6:7, 40:4, 62:7 and 131:3
(Roman Psalter numbering). All four were checked in page images.
The short previous inventory both omitted 62:7 and used the glossary
shorthand for 131:3 rather than the running transcription. At p. 608
the long-vowel headword is editorial; the shorthand at 131:3 lists
the headword without *-e*, in conflict with p. 379. The earlier
edition form quoted by Bosworth–Toller, under *streówen*, also has
*-e* at 131:3. Pending direct collation, **there is no secure bare
Vespasian token**. Do not silently normalize the running text in
either direction.

- **6:7 and 40:4:** the glossed noun is the object of an English
  transitive verb, so an English accusative does not depend on
  Latin *stratum* being accusative. At 40:4 English *alle*
  supports a feminine singular noun in context. The dictionary
  shorthand associates *mine* with several loci, but Sweet's
  40:4 has *his*, not *mine*. Toller's Supplement prints an *eo*
  spelling with this line and cites “Ps. Vos.”; that is not direct
  verification of ⟨streone⟩ in Cotton Vespasian A I and must not
  silently displace Sweet's ⟨strene⟩.
- **62:7:** *ofer strene mine* gives another oblique in a local
  ‘on/upon my bed’ context. Parallel accusative Latin syntax
  supports but does not force the same analysis in English.
- **131:3:** Latin *stratus* is a genitive dependent on *lectum*.
  English *bed* is already the complement of *in*. The parallel
  analysis makes *strene minre* an English dependent genitive;
  locative or appositional reinterpretation is not impossible
  in an interlinear gloss. Unlike Latin gender or case, English
  *minre* is positive evidence for feminine singular agreement
  (genitive or dative).

The manuscript is London, British Library, Cotton MS Vespasian A I,
with an eighth-century Latin text and a ninth-century Mercian gloss.
The [historical facsimile link](https://www.bl.uk/manuscripts/FullDisplay.aspx?ref=Cotton_MS_Vespasian_A_I)
redirects to the Library's general page; its restored catalogue
search yielded the detached later leaf and Wright's separately held
1967 facsimile, not viewable images of the four target verses.
[ARCA](https://arca.irht.cnrs.fr/ark:/63955/md021c18dr7x) records
the complete microfilm but does not display its target leaves.
The online listing of Kuhn's 1965 edition was identifiable; its
target pages were not accessible. These checks do not substitute
for an original-leaf reading or establish the folio numbers.

## Other traditions and exclusions

**Bede.** Miller (1890: 114.12; II.6) prints ⟨streowne⟩ in
the command to prepare a bed for Laurentius. The [Tanner 10
image](https://iiif.bodleian.ox.ac.uk/iiif/image/e4819e5b-629c-48d3-bcc2-9182d02562bf/full/1500,/0/default.jpg)
confirms ⟨streoƿne⟩ in the first line of f. 13r (older number
23 on the leaf). It functions as the
accusative object of *gegearwian*, with *him* the beneficiary.
Bosworth–Toller reports that B (Cambridge, Corpus Christi
College MS 41) instead uses *bedd gewyrcian*. The lexical choice
in B is not an additional token of ‘couch’. The ⟨w⟩ and ⟨eo⟩
make the T form a serious labial-retaining comparison,
not another unqualified example of the Vespasian contraction.
The spellings are not in the same witness or necessarily the same
stage; they cannot by themselves determine the earlier quantity.

**Maxims I.** The Exeter Book reads ⟨streo | num⟩ across the
break at f. 90r, manuscript lines 5–6, directly checked in the
[Exeter digitisation](https://theexeterbook.exeter.ac.uk/viewer.html?image=90r).
The local reading is *hord in streonum bidan* (68b); the
dictionary rearranges the clause in citation. The ending and
locative *in* support dative plural irrespective of the lemma.
Bosworth–Toller's ‘resting places’ is plausible as a description
of where treasure stays. The independent neuter *strēon*
‘gain, property, treasure’ is homographic here; this is a
qualified third tradition, not a secure third ‘bed’ witness
or evidence for the gender of the poem's noun.

**Other sources.** Clark Hall (1960, s.vv. *strēon, strēowen,
strēwen*) separates the neuter ‘gain’ from its homographic
cross-reference to the feminine; his headword variants are not
necessarily distinct manuscript tokens. His *strēownes*
‘mattress’ (Blickling Glosses 227) and *strewung* ‘bedding’
(Lambeth Psalter 131:3) are independent formations and are
not counted. The Paris Psalter has *restbedd* at 131, line 9,
not the Vespasian noun. The Supplement's Ps. Vos. 40:4 citation
is kept as a reported variant pending witness-level verification.
A bounded spelling search tested `stren`, `strene`, `stræn(e)`,
`streon(e)`, `streow(e)n(e)`, `strewen` and the plural and genitive
endings in the available glossary/edition text. The existing DOE
handoffs are searches for ‘hay’, not a full lexeme search for
‘couch’. A fresh DOE Corpus search could not be verified and
must not be claimed as a negative result. No complete census of
all Psalter glosses or inaccessible editions is inferred.

## Morphological outcome

The strongest count is four edition-verified occurrences in
one early Mercian tradition, plus the manuscript-verified Bede
accusative and the Exeter dative plural if its
lexical identification holds. No securely observed nominative
or **bare** accusative can be extrapolated from this record.
The Vespasian distribution supports feminine grammatical gender,
independently of Sweet's and later dictionaries' labels.
Accusative and plausible genitive *-e* suit a strong feminine
paradigm; a productive feminine weak stem would instead require
an explanation for the lack of weak oblique *-an*. An earlier
stem-class transfer or inherited nasal extension is not excluded.
The recoverable English stem is ⟨stren-⟩ in Vespasian, with
possible ⟨streon-⟩ and ⟨streown-⟩ elsewhere. One plausible
segmentation of Bede ⟨streowne⟩ is stem medial syncope plus
accusative *-e*. Neither the precise earlier thematic vowel nor
whether *n* was derivational can be established from the
attestations alone. Quantity remains reconstructed.

For the eventual experiments, start with accusative *strene*,
test the conditional dependent genitive separately, and keep the
Bede accusative and possible Exeter dative plural separate from
the Mercian cells. No historic input should be selected just
because it gives a convenient bare *strēn*.

## C2: what the etymology actually predicts

| Source | Proposal and its limits |
|---|---|
| Luick, §243c, pp. 219–220 | Groups ‘couch’ with Anglian contraction in *e.wi* from older *a.wi* and contrasts the w-bearing form. His alternative loss of analogical *j* is especially relevant to the verbs; he does not derive the noun's nasal or specify an inflected input. |
| Wright & Wright (1914: 305, §599; cf. §254) | Explicitly includes *streowen* among concrete and abstract feminine formations in *-en*, alongside feminine personal derivatives. The heading gives both *-inī* and *-injō-* and the West Germanic geminated formation. This is an earlier explicit derivational classification, but it does not allocate ‘couch’ to one of the two sources, assign a quantity to the root, explain Vespasian, or give witness-level citations. |
| Campbell (1959: 100, §237.1b; 166–167, §406) | Treats Vespasian *strēn* as a member of the set with *w*-loss before *i* and contraction, with ‘call’ as an explicitly reconstructed comparison. He supplies neither a noun paradigm nor an independent demonstration of its first vowel length. The normalized lemma must not be read as a cited bare token. |
| Sievers–Brunner (1965: 111, §129 n. 2; 142, §173e; cf. §110 n. 2) | Allows *w*-loss in Anglian *e.wi* from umlauted *a.wi*, but also suggests influence from ‘strew’. Proposes that the *eo* of the w-bearing noun could have been remodelled after the class II verb, where back mutation has an independent setting; not a claim that back mutation must operate through suffixal *i* in the noun. The general geographical heading at §173e does not relocate Vespasian to Northumbria. |
| Ringe–Taylor (2014: 259–261, §6.6.4) | Explicit *stræwinu* > *streinu* for southwest Mercian *strēn*, derived from ‘spread out’. Short *æ*, intervocalic *w*, weak *i* and singleton *n* are all specified or implied by the input; the proposed *-u* is compatible with a feminine nominative, not evidence that such a token survives. The comparable past of ‘strew’, together with ‘call’, ‘ewe’ and the loan ‘pillow’, supports the phonological step independently of the noun. |
| Hogg (1992, §§5.14, 5.105, 6.20); Hogg–Fulk (2011, §§2.38–40, 2.46–49, 3.73–77) | Hogg's vowel and apocope environments, and the independently reconstructed strong feminine endings in Hogg–Fulk, constrain the assay. Hogg–Fulk's account separates two sources of *-en(n)*; it does not supply a new etymology for ‘couch’. |

Wright's discussion shows that the feminine suffix classification
predates Ringe–Taylor; the exact singleton, short-*i* input is
explicit in Ringe–Taylor. The earlier accounts do not constitute
several independent reconstructions of that exact formation. No
earlier source was verified for the full input, and the accessible
Holthausen listing did not permit verification of the relevant entry.
Bosworth–Toller, its Supplement, and Clark Hall establish lexical
and editorial comparisons, not the prehistory of the suffix.

### Reconstructions to keep separate

1. **Preferred working model: short root + singleton nasal.** A
   feminine stem *strawin-* built to ‘strew’ has independent
   morphological and lexical motivation, although neither the precise
   suffix allomorph nor the root quantity of the derived noun is
   independently attested. Shortness is supported by ordinary inheritance
   from the short base, conditional on that derivation, not by reading
   back from *strēn*. Pre-OE *strawina* (accusative) gives *stræwina* >
   *strewina* > *streina* under brightening, umlaut and *w*-loss; direct
   contraction and ordinary reduction of final *a* are then predicted to
   give *strēne*. Here *ei* in the intermediate denotes syllabic *e.i*,
   not an assumed phonetic diphthong. The alternative palatal and nuclear
   resolutions still require tests. If the short singleton formation
   is instead modelled with the early jō-stem transfer of the broader
   suffix class, jō-stem admission and the quantity consequences need
   separate justification; the *-ō* paradigm is a first working model,
   not a demonstration of the inherited stem class.
2. **Different suffix history.** Wright's geminating feminine
   *-innjō* and Hogg–Fulk's earlier *-īniz* followed by transfer are
   genuine alternatives to treating short *-in-* as established.
   The former predicts a different pre-loss nasal environment and
   potentially an oblique geminate; the latter does not independently
   supply the **short** weak vowel invoked by Ringe–Taylor. An
   earlier *ī* reduced before *w*-loss is a chronological hypothesis to
   justify, not a way to silently reuse the same input. Neither an
   originally weak n-stem nor an original bare direct singular is
   required by the evidence.
3. **Remodelling or labial retention.** Brunner's w-retaining,
   verb-influenced alternative can explain why Bede need not share the
   Mercian development. Bede ⟨streowne⟩ is compatible with a stem
   *streow(e)n-* plus an accusative ending, with syncope; the stem
   remodelling would precede the syncope, not be a respelling after it.
   A Mercian model based on analogical influence from verbal *streid-*
   or *strēd-* is possible, but must independently justify the direction
   of transfer and the surviving nasal. A long stem borrowed from
   another family member is not excluded, but neither a suitable
   independent nasal donor with an inherited long monophthong nor
   positive evidence for that transfer is known here. Do not recruit
   *strēon* ‘gain’ into the argument on spelling alone.

For the first model use the pre-OE ō-stem endings of Hogg–Fulk
§2.39. The nominative singular *-u* is a diagnostic only; its fate
can depend on the weight of the stem before high-vowel loss. The
accusative *-a* is a separate, attested target. Historical genitive
singular *-ō* and dative *-ai* are distinct inputs even though attested
strong obliques commonly converge in *-e*. In particular, expected
genitive *-a* > analogical *-e* (§2.40) is not a phonological output
criterion for the assay. Plural *-ō/-a* (nom./acc.), *-ǣ* (gen.)
and *-um* (dat.) complete the working paradigm; only the last has
a possible attestation, and it belongs to a different witness and
may belong to the ‘gain’ noun. Neither a short root by itself nor
surface *-e* determines when internal syncope and apocope applied.
The experimental test must therefore score the stem and the ending
separately, and track whether contraction changes the input to
either loss process.

The preferred account predicts post-labial front hiatus before
*n* and subsequent resolution, but the written Mercian form gives
neither an independent measurement of length nor a directly attested
hiatus intermediate. ‘Couch’ is a serious independent **lexical**
comparison to ‘hay’, conditional as evidence for one particular
phonological mechanism or wider nasal conditioning.
