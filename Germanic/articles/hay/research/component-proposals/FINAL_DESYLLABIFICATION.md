# C1 — What independently supports a final palatal from hiatus?

**Result:** Old English has independently supported changes from a syllabic
front vowel to a consonantal glide, and from a second syllabic vowel to a
nuclear offglide. They establish the segmental and prosodic mechanisms,
respectively, not their combination at the absolute word edge. The native
front-vowel evidence actually distinguishes the question of **what becomes
of the second vowel** from **whether the first vowel lengthens**.

## Comparisons and their historical roles

The bounded check revisited the relevant sections of Campbell and Hogg,
the weak-suffix and palatal material of Ringe & Taylor, and the previous
[priority register](../hiatus-cycle4/SCHOLARLY_PRIORITY.md). Searches covered
`syllabic`, `nonsyllabic`, `glide`, and `hiatus`, including alternatives to
post-labial hiatus. The new comparison with Anglian ‘live’ was checked
against Ringe & Taylor's held page 365 (PDF sheet 380) and Sweet's
edition text and glossary. The authors' reconstructions are not
transcriptions of the original scribes' pronunciations.

| Independent evidence | Historical value | Why it does not decide *he.i* |
|---|---|---|
| Campbell (1959: 168 §410), ‘free’: *frēo* beside accusative *frīgne*, genitive *frīges* and plural *frīge*. | His reconstruction explicitly uses consonantalization of long *ī* before a front vowel, contrasting with its nuclear combination with back vowels. The final direct form belongs to the latter history; the consonantal inflected outcomes are real precedents for changing syllabic status rather than deleting the front element. | The relevant consonantal episode is **before** a vowel, even if an ending subsequently changes; it is not reduction of the **last** element of short *e.i*. Neither the input quantity nor the direction of syllabification is matched. |
| Campbell (1959: §546), late loan and name pronunciations; e.g. *Gregorius* and *Bersabea*. | The potential three-syllable realizations motivate loss of syllabicity by a high or mid front vowel before a following vowel. The spelling substitution ⟨g/ig⟩ for a front vowel in other names is compatible with this mechanism. | Syllable counts are proposals, and many loans retain Latin spelling. In *Bersabea* the candidate element is **before** final *a*, not a final weak front vowel after *e*. |
| Hogg (2011 reprint: §§3.19, 5.135–137), early back-nucleus cases and later first singular *bēo*. | Here it is the **second** vowel of hiatus that loses syllabicity; in a final form the outcome can retain that element in the nucleus. Thus preservation of both qualities without counting two syllables is independently motivated. | It is nuclear, not consonantal, and Hogg's template and weight assumptions are explicit. The West Germanic early pattern is short vowel + *u*, not a rule for every high vowel. His later example is long. |
| Hogg (2011: 172–175 §§5.131–134), normal later front-hiatus resolution; final ‘magician’ from rounded long vowel plus *i*, beside nonfinal ‘higher’, ‘cover’, etc. | A real adverse comparison for **general** preservation of the second front vowel. Hogg posits length before loss of the unstressed front element. Even in final position an *i* need not be preserved as a palatal consonant. | The final example is long/rounded on his analysis and most of his material follows *h*-loss. His prior lengthening of the syllable-final nucleus gives a historical rival, not an independent measurement of the vowel of ‘hay’. |
| Ringe & Taylor (2014: 228, 365–366), Mercian *lifgu*, *lifgende*, *lifge* and Kentish *libgendes* (Ct 40.17). Sweet (1885: 360, Vespasian Ps 118:17) prints *ic lifgu* for Latin *vivam*. | In their analysis Anglian /j/ contrasts with syllabic /i/ of weak class II; a potentially useful regional distinction, with no necessary general ban on consonantal /j/ in inflected words. | They derive *lifg-* through morphological renewal and syncope before an **already consonantal** /j/. *Lifgu* is not a final exposed palatal and does not attest creation from syllabic *i*. |
| Campbell (1959: §§266–267, 411–412), early *Tīg* and comparable *-i(g)* against later or inflected *-iw/-ēow*. | Final ⟨ig⟩ is compatible with a single long vowel in his account; material can be transferred across cells. This removes a tempting apparent final ⟨g⟩ parallel. | The initial loss is final *w* after *ī*, not creation of a new final palatal. Neither spellings nor later alternants establish underlying final /j/. |

Two negative checks prevent a direction-of-change error. The inherited
palatal of *cǣġ/cǣġe* shows the consonantal endpoint is possible after
a long nucleus, not how it was created. Jones's short [heǰ] starts
with a **fricative**; his splitting analysis can add nuclear detail
before an existing consonant and therefore cannot be cited as prior
support for *he.i > hej*. The [joint priority dossier](../hiatus/DAHL_JONES.md)
records the remaining inaccessible or indirectly cited originals.
No new edition or priority status is claimed for them.

## Consequence for the lexical comparison

Desyllabification is not an ad hoc maneuver invented for ‘hay’: both
of its component possibilities are attested independently, but in
different structural environments. The short consonantal analysis
gets a regular direct cell and a palatal with an independently comparable
spelling in ‘way’, without requiring a morphological donor.
That is explanatory economy **if** the final segment moved to the coda
before the first nucleus lengthened and without a short-front template
forcing incorporation. The relative timing is a premise, not a result.
Short nuclear incorporation instead has the more direct precedent
for loss of syllabicity by the **second** element; to claim it lacks
a consonant one must not treat final ⟨g⟩ as a phonetic transcription.
Contraction predicts length in the ordinary later front-hiatus comparison,
but a contraction to bare *hē* needs a separate account of the palatal.
These are unequal operations with independently motivated pieces,
not equally attested complete histories.

**RETAIN** final consonantal desyllabification as a positively motivated,
testable proposal; **DEFER** its identification as the preferable final
sound law without independent edge or timing evidence. The two
comparisons that could move that verdict are an inherited final short
front hiatus whose output is independently consonantal and the
ancestry of the mutated ‘river’ genitive. The interval between *w*-loss
and *h*-loss could explain different outcomes without lexical-origin
conditioning, but these structural comparisons do not date an operation
to that interval. No FST or production target follows from this dossier.
