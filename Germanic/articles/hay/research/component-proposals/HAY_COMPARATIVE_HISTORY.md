# C5 — Which history best explains the Anglian direct form?

## Argument from the grammatical cell

The accusative ⟨heg⟩ in Vespasian Ps 103:14 (Sweet 335,
*faenum*) is the inherited **cell** in which early nasal apocope
predicts an ungeminated stem. It need not preserve that stem.
Gothic *hawi* and continental West Germanic singleton forms
justify reconstructing a direct alternant without guessing from
the English spelling. The alternating OHG *hewi/houwi* is
particularly valuable: Ringe & Taylor (2014: 44–47, 53, 173,
246) take *hewi* as inherited direct and *houwi* as evidence of
levelling from protected cells. Gothic independently supports
the original lexical shape, not the relative timing of *West
Germanic* gemination. Continental contrasts favour the early
order **if** their singleton forms indeed inherit rather than
renew the direct stem. English *bedd/nett/cynn* remain
independently adverse to universal early nasal loss.

On the early order, the derivation through short hiatus
combines independently motivated steps:
*hawją > hawi > hæwi > hewi > he.i*. Ringe & Taylor print short
*hæwi* on p. 246 but do not finish the regular direct route;
Campbell §579.4 independently gives *hewi* as the expected
ungeminated form. The loss of *w* before syllabic weak *i* is a
real English law, not inferred from ‘hay’. Thus an untransferred
direct nucleus is a historical explanation, not a string fitted
to ⟨heg⟩. What remains to be demonstrated is its **resolution**.

## A geographical test independent of the frozen cascade

The protected labial cluster gives *hawwj- > hauj- > hēaj-*:
Ringe & Taylor 53, 173 and 246 motivate the reversal of labial
gemination, tensing of the new diphthong and regional mutation
(cf. Hogg §3.18; may retain the labial for longer in his
notation). In their independent controls the same type gives
West Saxon *īeġ* versus Anglian *ēġ* ‘island’, West Saxon
*ċīeġan* versus Kentish/Mercian *ċēġan* ‘call’, and Anglian
*strēġan* ‘strew’. The West Saxon and Anglian vowel projections
are therefore **high *īe*, later *ī/ȳ*, versus long mid *ē***,
not two spellings of a West Saxon high output. For ‘hay’ these
authors explicitly give West Saxon *hīeġ* and Mercian/Northumbrian
*hēġ*. Regional mutation precedes any attempt to identify the
ordinary Anglian ⟨heg⟩ with a particular stem.

The late West Saxon high-vowel evidence provides positive
motivation for **protected-type** vocalism there. It does not
decide whether the protected type was inherited in the direct
cell or supplied from obliques. Eadwine's two ⟨hieg⟩ are a
separate later witness with uncertain exemplar/dialect, not the
sole basis for high West Saxon. In Anglian, short direct *e*
and long protected *ē* would give the same unmarked ⟨e⟩ in
Vespasian: short ‘way’ and independently long ‘flame’ actually do
so. Later Farman's ⟨hóeg⟩ has local, limited positive evidence
for length; Aldred/Owun need not copy his quantity or his
notation. Kentish compound ⟨heg⟩ before *f* and ⟨hei⟩ before *w*
are at best lexical comparisons; *heiweg* may mean ‘highway’.
None of these observations independently puts whole-stem
substitution into early Mercian.

## Costs of the rival histories

| Route | Independent motivation and what it must still prove |
|---|---|
| **Inherited short consonantal *hej*** | Preserves the direct stem and both vowel qualities; only final syllabicity changes. [C1](FINAL_DESYLLABIFICATION.md) finds genuine precedents for consonantalization and nuclear incorporation, but no independently dated short final front counterpart. Contraction of nonfinal ‘called’ limits the putative rule. Prefer inherited ‘river’ obliques: their contraction is adverse to uniform application. [C3](FINAL_HIATUS_CHRONOLOGY.md) gives a real interval for early gliding, but cannot date gliding within it. |
| **Inherited short complex nucleus *hei̯*** | Back-vowel incorporation motivates keeping both elements in one nucleus without creating a consonant. A short *ei̯* is not a heavy diphthong by definition. It still needs an independent account of why this short front nucleus formed, and of written ⟨g⟩ if the latter represented a separate palatal. Jones's later vowel splitting before a fricative is the reverse kind of evidence. |
| **Direct long nucleus with palatal retained or newly formed** | Long vowels can coexist with both inherited *j* (independent ‘key’, *cǣġ*) and a secondary palatal (‘flame’, *lēġ*). That excludes any blanket prohibition. But within *he.i* there is no inherited consonant waiting to be retained: a direct long-plus-palatal route requires both an independent account of length and a consonantal outcome of the second element. Heavy nuclear incorporation alone does not produce a coda. |
| **Contraction plus subsequent palatal supply** | ‘Called’ and inherited ‘river’ make actual contraction historically well motivated. Bare *hē* is an intermediate, not a verified direct form. Deriving long *hēġ* also requires a source for the consonant. The Paris Psalter *hege* datives prove later inflected availability, not transfer of only a consonant before Vespasian. In Anglian the donor and recipient vowels could coincide after contraction, making consonant-only and whole-stem transfer hard to distinguish; West Saxon high vocalism cannot come from an unchanged mid monophthong plus just the donor consonant. |
| **Whole protected-stem transfer after early loss** | Campbell §579.4 and Ringe & Taylor 246 offer a real donor and comparative support for the direction in OHG *houwi*. West Saxon high vocalism is positive motivation for the protected type if the early order is chosen. It is not direct evidence of transfer in Vespasian. Actual donor distribution and levelling date are not recovered from a late oblique; a universal mass-noun argument is insufficient and grammatical labels describe cell inheritance, not sound-law conditioning. |
| **Direct inheritance of protected type under late nasal loss** | Hogg §3.18 and Hogg & Fulk §2.23 motivate a route that needs no stem transfer in English and predicts the same regional vowel contrast as protected-stem substitution. English geminates are independent evidence. The hypothesis must still explain the independently ungeminated continental direct cognates; one orthographic endpoint cannot adjudicate those relative orders. |
| **Dahl's distinct diphthongal reconstruction** | Dahl 81–86, 96–97 does not start from the short hiatus or from English gemination of this cluster. Already diphthongal material yields a long vowel; the displayed direct derivation supplies the palatal from inflected forms after loss, while his prose allows analogical retention or restoration. An existing palatal and its proposed loss carry different obligations from creating one out of *i*. ‘Key’ blocks a blanket final-loss premise. Dahl's Kentish date is **859**; the lexically disputed compound proves neither this chronology nor quantity. |

## Historical preference and publishable limits

Prefer the direct short derivation **through hiatus** under the
early order. For a *short consonantal* outcome its continuation
by final desyllabification is preferable to importing a whole
long stem and then shortening it without evidence: it explains
the cell using independent premises and one identifiable
phonological operation. It is also less costly than complete
coalescence followed by selective restoration **if** the final
operation is independently applicable. This is a relative
historical preference, not an inference that early shortness is
already established or that all long mechanisms are ad hoc.

The evidence does **not** presently prefer short /hej/ over
every long history for the actual Vespasian tokens. The closer
contraction controls, the inherited ‘river’ preference and the
uncalibrated resolution interval constrain the short route,
while [C4](VESPASIAN_QUANTITY.md) shows that the orthography
cannot choose. A historically motivated early direct route now
removes the **necessity** of Anglian whole-stem substitution as
an inference from ⟨heg⟩. It does not exclude it, deny the stronger
protected-vowel argument for West Saxon, license a universal law,
or resolve the early-apocope controversy. The decisive leverage
would be a dated, exact final front control, independently
quantitative Anglian evidence, or materially better independent
evidence about the continental direct/oblique ancestry.
