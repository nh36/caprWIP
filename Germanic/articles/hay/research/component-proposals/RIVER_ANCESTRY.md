# C2 — ‘River’: inherited mutation and demonstrable renewal

The early mutated obliques are better explained as continuations of
root-noun inflection than as new mutations of the contracted nominative.
That preference strengthens ‘river’ as an adverse comparison for
**uniform** final gliding. It does not show that the two lexemes
encountered the same resolution at the same time.

## What the sources establish

The early edition-level evidence was previously checked against Toller;
this is not a new manuscript collation. The comparative assessment
adds a direct check of Bammesberger (1990: 199), Kroonen (2013: s.v.
*ahwō-*), Hogg & Fulk (2011: 65–66, 135–136), and Ringe & Taylor
(2014: 318, PDF sheet 333). Hogg & Fulk's PDF sheet 80 directly confirms
the early-root-stem argument and the contrast with plurals and renewed
genitives. The Seldeslachts citation is through Hogg & Fulk, not a newly
verified original.

## The comparative preference

Gothic *aƕa*, Old Saxon and Old High German *aha*, and Latin *aqua*
support a short root independently of OE; the thematic stem is well
motivated in comparative Germanic. Bammesberger emphasizes that the
**root-noun** inflection can be demonstrated only from English:
continental cognates and Old Norse *á* do not independently supply
this particular oblique ending. Hogg & Fulk counter the proposed
English innovation with positive evidence: the early mutated cells
are already present, and transfer **into** a declining, unproductive
root class is unlike the demonstrable later transfer **out** to
more productive declensions. The two claims are compatible: a
pre-English root variant is preferable for the mutated cells without
requiring every Germanic cognate to continue that variant.

On the regular alternative explicitly given by Ringe & Taylor
(2014: 318), an inherited singular oblique *eahi* develops to
southern *iehi* or Anglian *ehi* with a short root before *h*-loss.
The early distribution by **cell**, rather than simply by region,
supports inherited allomorphy. In their other analysis the mutated
form is made by rule to already contracted *ēa*. That can fit the
forms, but requires that a mutation or a surviving morphological
alternation remained available after the direct history had
contracted; neither *eas* nor *ean* demonstrates that operation.
In particular, chronology cannot be rescued by assigning the
mutated genitive a long *ēhi* input on the strength of its normalized
long outcome. This preference is for inheritance of the **oblique**,
not proof that the Chronicle scribe's source was itself early Anglian.

**RETAIN** ‘river’ as genuine adverse evidence against unconditional
consonantalization of final short *e.i*. Prefer the early inherited
oblique on the available independent historical evidence, while
retaining later derivation from *ēa* as a live alternative. The absence
of a written final palatal in the repeated genitive is significant
under the inherited analysis. The difference in the origin of hiatus
between ‘river’ and ‘hay’ can be historically decisive only if
resolution is separately dated or independently conditioned; the
model's ability to place it at two points is not such evidence.
