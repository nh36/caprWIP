# Could the inherited direct singular of ‘hay’ survive regularly?

Supplementary research, 26 November 2026, `research/hay-article`.
Initial local and remote head: `14293ff48df704bdda8a216ee2f87257129fd941`.
Published manuscript baseline: `bf9194f3c5bce2f767e447dbfb305ed57c8312e2`.
No tracked changes or Cycle 23 implementation at intake. Pre-existing

under the SC047 literature dossier, and
`Germanic/tests/test_sc047_finite_receipts.py`. None is part of this research.

The falsifiable question is whether an independently controlled history
of word-final short stressed /e/ plus unstressed syllabic /i/, or
independent evidence for short /ej/ in a secure early direct singular,
makes regular survival preferable. A secure early long nucleus would
exclude unchanged short /hej/ for that witness. A long nucleus would
not by itself prove replacement. The inherited path to hiatus is
conditional on A: \*hawją > \*hawj > \*hawi > \*hæwi > \*hewi > \*he.i.

**Assessment.** Dahl does propose a bare \*hē intermediate, but from his
own long diphthong and final-vowel loss. None of these checked ‘hay’
passages gives bare \*hē from contraction of A's short stressed /e/ and
unstressed final /i/. That is a candidate inference from controls, not
an inherited consensus. The conventional Anglian macron in *hēġ* is
explained by competing etymologies, not independent calibration of the
earliest spellings. This does not show the long reconstruction is wrong.
Retain the distinction in C01 and leave E33–E34 unestablished. Dal,
Wallenberg and Brooks and Kelly were not acquired.

Coverage: the named English controls, Hogg §§3.19, 5.129–154,
Campbell §§237.1b, 266–267, 406, 512, 553–557, Sievers–Brunner
§§127–135, Ringe and Taylor §§6.7.1, 6.9.3, and the held Old Frisian
chapters and continental cognate case studies. This is a bounded
grammar-and-lexeme search, not an exhaustive West Germanic corpus
negative. The controls distinguish retention, loss, identical and unlike
contraction, and diphthongal incorporation. None documents the exact
conversion to a consonantal coda with independently short /e/.
Word-finality could matter, but no independently paired final/nonfinal
/e.i/ establishes the proposed split. Hogg's open-syllable length
assumption and back-vowel diphthong examples do not show later lengthening
of *already monosyllabic* /ej/; that added history would need its own
dated rule and controls. Luick §252 lengthens via the loss of palatal
frication after /i/, not by keeping an inherited /j/ after /e/.

**Assessment.** E25 remains positively motivated as a mechanism but
undecided as an application. Contraction has the closer vowel-quality
match; the matching examples are nonfinal. Neither bare \*hē nor
\*hej is independently predicted for the exact final environment.

**Kentish scope.** Lowe (2001: 76–78, 85–86, Appendix I.7 and I.14)

Sweet's c. 831 dating is not used. Ct. 42 is S 1510 (ii.42,
mid-ninth-century, Christ Church, 845 × 853 according to Lowe Appendix
I.6, not Sweet's 837); its scribe 5 alternates *dege* and *dei* across
different cells. Ct. 41 (S 1482, ii.64, ninth-century Christ Church,

search (S 316, 328, 332, 344, 1195–97) supplies no independently long
mid-vowel monosyllable; *higum* ‘household’ and *hegforde* with unresolved
lexical identity cannot fill that gap. Scarcity of eligible comparisons
is not evidence of a categorical spelling convention.

**Vespasian scope.** The complete text layer of Sweet (1885: 188–420,
PDF sheets 202–434) was screened for simplex candidate variants of
‘day’, ‘way’, ‘play’, ‘island’, ‘clay’, ‘dry’, ‘path’ and ‘ascend’;
Sweet's glossary was cross-checked for spellings missed by the text
layer. This interlinear scan mixes Latin, English, apparatus and OCR
errors. Latin *Dei* is not Kentish-looking English *dei*, most hits
for *ege/egum* relate to ‘fear’, and *plaga* is not the English noun
‘play’. Sweet (525–526, 608–609, 631–632) gives ‘play’ primarily in
inflected verb forms (Ps 46:2, 67:26, 97:8) and ‘island’ in names
from other sources. No securely identified Vespasian simplex of
‘island’, ‘clay’, or noun ‘play’ emerged from the screen. ‘Dry’ and
the *stīg* family are structurally or qualitatively mismatched.
An image check confirmed the uncommon *dig* beside *deg* (Ps 55),
*weg* beside *styge* (77:50), and prefixed final *astig* (143:5).
These establish variation and overlap in the use of consonant letters,
not a same-environment long/short contrast. The panel reports marks
printed by Sweet; no comprehensive manuscript accent audit or
complete collation of candidate tokens was obtained. The additional
vowel-letter preference and any independent suprasegmental distinction
for early Mercian *heg* therefore remain untested, not absent.

**Gospel scope.** Skeat's paired Mk 1:2–3 columns allow small,
separate panels; the same verses are not independent phonological
events. The target contrast Aldred *heig* : Owun *heg* at Jn 6:10
likewise establishes separate written choices within a related gloss
tradition. Ritt (2000: 505–509) interprets Aldred's acutes by graphical
syllable shape as well as quantity. His reverse check on the first
100 graphically closed Matthew items credits an accented **variant**
of an otherwise unaccented historically long item and allows reduced
pronouns. It is neither a token-level rule that omission means short
nor a final-/j/ calibration. Aldred's unaccented target readings are
therefore not positive evidence for shortness.

**Assessment.** No studied witness supplies an independently calibrated
preference for short /ej/ in ‘hay’. The positive result is that
short controls can share the ordinary spelling patterns; that removes
an objection to shortness, not to length. Farman remains the limited
local exception to the general lack of positive quantity evidence.

## R4. Jones and the provenance of short-vowel interpretations

Jones (1989: 21, 55, 149–152; held PDF sheets 35, 69, 163–166) was
checked against page images, not the OCR's lost macrons and brackets.
In his account [j] is a voiced palatal **fricative**, not the
phonetic approximant implied by a literal modern IPA reading. The
relevant structural contrast is consonantal palatal vs. a
nonsyllabic portion of the stressed nucleus, not /he.i/ vs. /hej/
in A.

The ‘meadow’ row is not an independent securely identified token.
The verified Lindisfarne ⟨heig⟩ glosses Latin *faenum*, while the
paired ⟨heg⟩ forms are also in *faenum* contexts. This makes reuse of
the ‘hay’ spelling pair with a loose or mistaken English gloss more
plausible than treating the table as attestation of a separate
‘meadow’ item. The West Saxon ⟨hæg⟩ form and the gloss could instead
reflect conflation with the enclosure family (compare Clark Hall,
s.v. *hēg*, *hieg*, *hege*, and the distinct charter evidence in
Cycle 5). Jones gives no verse for the row, so neither a separate
enclosure reading nor that conflation can be demonstrated. Do not
add a token to either lexical inventory.

The search for independent short-vowel analyses was bounded to the
lexical treatments in R1, Campbell's palatal spelling discussion
(§§266–269), and Minkova (2014: 178–179). Bryan's *dei* comparison
calibrates the spelling but his ‘hay’ quantity comes from his own
etymology. Dahl's Anglian long vowel comes from a different input;
the grammar treatments derive length on the protected line, and
Minkova explicitly takes it as etymological before explaining why
the later merger obscures it. Jones supplies the explicit short
analysis found in this search, not a second independently controlled
early quantity. His late-Northumbrian short notation and comparative
table's long forms need not be a contradiction about the same token;
neither provides a reason to assign that length to ninth-century
Mercian.

**Assessment.** The short reconstruction has genuine scholarly
precedent, but no additional independent support for Vespasian
shortness was found. The long normalization remains historically
motivated, not an independent observation either.
