### Gloss identification and relative chronology

The Germanic *strawjan-* family has independent support from Gothic *strawidedun*, Old Norse *stráði* and Old High German *strewita* (Ringe & Taylor 2014: 259). Its sound history could yield *strawidē > stræwide > streide* in the preterite, or *strawidaz > stræwid > streid* in the participle. A comparable development explains why Ringe and Taylor group Northumbrian *stredun* with the English post-*w*-loss forms. But Latin *struere* describes putting or piling things together, not a uniquely diagnostic use of the native verb. Pheifer (§59.2 n. 1) also allows a weak preterite of *stregdan*, with vocalized *g*, not lost *w*; Hogg (1992: 174 §5.133 n. 2) relates that alternative to second fronting. Campbell (1959: 100 §237.1b, §§266, 406, 741, 753 n. 7) discusses the *w*-loss history but also provides the necessary independent palatal/spelling and conjugational comparisons. The Rushworth sense and Old Saxon parallel favour taking the *strawjan-* family seriously; neither resolves the glossary ambiguity. The two Old Saxon spellings independently reinforce the w-less comparison but cannot specify English chronology, syllable count or vowel quality. Ringe & Taylor (2014: 259–260) themselves distinguish parallel development from a single inherited English–Saxon innovation.

Even under the *strawjan-* analysis, Erfurt/Corpus ⟨ei⟩ could reflect *e.i*, monosyllabic *ei̯*, a sequence with /j/, or a transmitted older spelling. It is not a measurement of vowel quantity. If the lexical identification is instead *stregdan*, the forms are not observations on unlike hiatus after *w*-loss at all. The Épinal reading and the shared copying tradition make the published shorthand “early Épinal *streide*” misleading.

### The metrical discriminator

Sievers (1893: 132, §84.8 n. 5, his numbering 2437) considers a monosyllabic *strēd* reading, `lift–dip–secondary | lift` (rare E2), but prefers `lift–secondary | lift–dip`, with metrically compressed medial vocalism in *morðorbed* and two positions in *stré-id*. Fulk (1992: 79, 115 §§88, 120) lists the verse as 2A3b in the context of parasiting and identifies it as the sole nonanalogical metrical evidence he accepts for noncontraction after *w*-loss before *i*. In this reading *strēd*, or a monosyllabic diphthong or /j/-bearing output, does not fill the final dip; a syllabic second member does. The premise is the preferred analysis of the whole verse, not an absolute prohibition on E2, a reading directly encoded by ⟨e⟩ or a result independent of the assumed pronunciation of the compound. Hogg (1992: 174 §5.133) cautions that a disyllabic realization might instead reflect slow speech. An old verse pronunciation, a performance variant and ordinary spoken retention need not have the same date.

## Historical analysis and verdict

**DEFER** a registry chronology edge. Prefer taking the verse seriously as evidence that unlike hiatus could remain available to verse after *w*-loss. Do not equate that with the survival of an ordinary spoken *e.i* throughout the period or with a regional sound law. Regular identical-vowel contraction in *ni.i-* and the comparable ‘glee’ reconstruction is plausible (Ringe & Taylor 2014: 318); these outcomes do not establish that it was already complete at the date of the performance represented by 2436b. Nor does gloss transmission establish unchanged live syllabicity until the date of the surviving copies. The hypothesis **identical before unlike** is therefore motivated but not independently demonstrated here. A common late window after *h*-loss can coexist with the independently motivated post-HVD heavy resolution of [‘flock’](CHRONOLOGY.md) and contracted *stredun*, while the glosses and verse remain conditional adverse evidence rather than results it derives. Distinguishing a surviving performance variant from a change that genuinely had not reached ordinary speech still requires independent contemporary evidence.
