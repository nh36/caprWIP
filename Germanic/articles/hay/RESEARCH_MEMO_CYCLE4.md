# Research memorandum, cycle 4: is Model A's regular *hei* represented in the attested record?

> **Cycle 5 correction:** This memo preserves the cycle-4 state. Its S 1196
> discussion is superseded by `RESEARCH_MEMO_CYCLE5.md`, which qualifies
> the charter's date and transmission and excludes *Deimund* as a secure
> same-scribe control. The Farman census is corrected to 196 non-target
> types/299 tokens plus *hóeg* (one type/two tokens); its bounded matched
> calibration is descriptive, not a population-wide accentuation rate.

Date: 2026-11-24. Status: Stage A adjudication (precedes any revision of
DRAFT.md). Brief: test seriously whether the early-apocope chronology's
regular direct-case output — conventionally serialized *hei* — survives in
the manuscript record, before analogical levelling is invoked.

Method note. Every claim below distinguishes four layers: (1) manuscript
spelling, (2) phonemic interpretation, (3) historical derivation,
(4) grammatical cell. Each proposition states the strongest supporting
evidence, the strongest counterevidence, and a provisional verdict.

---

## Proposition 1 — The literal spelling ⟨hei⟩ is attested in material that continues Model A's *hei*

### 1.1 The charter: *heiweg*

New adjudication (this cycle):

- **Identity.** The charter is **Sawyer S 1196** (= Sweet, *Oldest English
  Texts*, Ct. 43; Kemble's "Ct 43/3" in Dahl), a Christ Church Canterbury
  single sheet, BL Cotton Augustus ii.16. Boundary clause: *Ab oriente
  cyniges heiweg. A meri[di]e stret to Scufeling forde* (Sweet OET p. 450
  prints *cyniges hei weg*).
- **Date.** **A.D. 859**, not 839. Dahl (1938) prints "A.D. 839" where he
  cites *heiweg* but dates the same charter 859 where he cites
  *Scufelingforde* from its very next phrase; Sweet (OET Ct. 43) and the
  Electronic Sawyer both give 859. The "839" in our concordance (id 15)
  and in DRAFT.md reproduced Dahl's slip and is corrected this cycle.
- **Transmission.** The surviving sheet is dated s. ix med. (BL Cotton
  Augustus ii.16); that does not establish that every textual layer is
  contemporary. Lowe gives the transaction date as ?859, notes that the
  date may be erroneous, and treats the witness-list as potentially
  separate; see the fuller qualification in `RESEARCH_MEMO_CYCLE5.md`.
- **Interpretation.** *cyniges heiweg* has been read as 'the king's
  hay-way', but this is not an uncontested identification. The hay
  interpretation is plausible; 'high-way' and an enclosure-related
  analysis remain possible, and the spelling alone does not choose among
  them. The first element is compound-internal, not a nominative singular
  token.

Strongest support. The spelling ⟨heiweg⟩ is securely on the surviving
sheet, but its lexical identity is disputed. It is not by itself an
attestation of the noun 'hay' or proof that Model A's independent direct
stem *hei* survives.

Strongest counterevidence. The same scribal milieu uses ⟨ei⟩ and ⟨eg⟩
interchangeably for a **certified short** vowel + /j/: *dæġ* appears as
**dei** in OET Ct. 39 (Ealhburg, 831) and Ct. 42 (Badanoth, 837), and
S 1196's own witness list has **Deimund** (= Dægmund), beside *deg*,
*dege* in Ct. 41 and 45. Ninth-century Kentish ⟨ei⟩ is therefore a normal
graph for /Vj/ and carries **no quantity information**: ⟨hei⟩ is exactly
as compatible with levelled /heːj/ as with unlevelled /hej/. (In this
small control corpus ⟨ei⟩ happens to be attested only over short V + /j/,
which if anything leans short; but no long-V + /j/ word occurs in the
same documents to test the converse, so the lean is weak.) Further, a
compound first element may preserve an older stem shape after the simplex
has been remodelled (compare the fossilized compound forms Dahl collects),
so even a secure short reading would not establish the contemporary
simplex nominative.

Verdict. **Attested, early, authentic — and indeterminate.** S 1196
guarantees the spelling, the date, and the lexeme, but not the phonology
and not the grammatical cell.

### 1.2 The glossary: *Fenum, hei, uel græs* (WW I.547)

New adjudication (this cycle):

- **Manuscript.** The "Semi-Saxon Vocabulary" (WW no. XIII, cols.
  536–552) survives as binding leaves of a Worcester register, discovered
  by Phillipps (1838); it follows the copy of Ælfric's *Grammar* in
  **Worcester Cathedral F.174, written by the Tremulous Hand of
  Worcester, s. xiii** (Ker). Wright–Wülcker's "c. 1150" is superseded:
  the language is consistently Early Middle English.
- **Transmission.** The vocabulary is an abridgement of the
  eleventh-century glossary printed as WW no. X. The corresponding entry
  sequence there (Uia weg … Foenum … Ager … Seges … Campus) reads
  *Foenum, **hig**, oðde gærs*. Textual descent is thus demonstrable:
  F.174's ⟨hei⟩ is a thirteenth-century respelling of an exemplar that
  wrote **hig**.
- **Orthographic control.** The same F.174 glossary writes *Uia, **wei***,
  *(buta)n weie*, *weibreode*, *(d)eieseien* — i.e. it systematically
  respells *weġ* (certified short) and *dæġ* with ⟨ei⟩. Its ⟨hei⟩ is the
  routine Early Middle English ⟨ei⟩ for /Vj/ of any origin, applied to
  whatever the exemplar transmitted.

Verdict. **No continuity.** The one independent-noun ⟨hei⟩ descends,
demonstrably, from an ⟨ig⟩-type exemplar through a scribe who wrote ⟨ei⟩
for *weġ* as well. It is evidence for thirteenth-century Worcester
orthography, not for survival of Model A's *hei*.

### 1.3 Required three-way distinction

1. *Is the literal spelling ⟨hei⟩ attested?* **Yes**: S 1196 (859,
   compound first element) and F.174 (s. xiii, independent noun).
2. *Is ⟨hei⟩ attested as an independent noun in the Old English period?*
   **No.** The only OE-period token is compound-internal; the only
   independent noun token is Early Middle English and descends from ⟨hig⟩.
3. *Do the attestations support continuity from regular inherited *hei*?*
   **Not demonstrably.** S 1196 is compatible with it but equally
   compatible with levelled *hēġ*; F.174 is positively excluded.

## Proposition 2 — /he.i/ → /hej/ is a regular Old English development

Strongest support. Final /i/ after a vowel has no stable disyllabic
parse in OE: the language tolerates [Vj] codas (*weġ*, *dæġ*, *dei* =
dæġ), and reinterpreting /he.i/ as /hej/ requires no new sound law, only
syllabification (DERIVATIONS A1-ii). The ⟨eg⟩~⟨ei⟩ interchange for *dæġ*
in the ninth-century Kentish charters shows that the output would be
spelled exactly ⟨heg⟩/⟨hei⟩ — i.e. Model A's output is **orthographically
invisible** next to levelled *hēġ* in Anglian/Kentish spelling.

Strongest counterevidence. The *regular* OE treatment of hiatus is not
glide formation but **contraction with a long output** (DERIVATIONS A1-i:
Hogg §§5.129–134): the stressed vowel lengthens positionally and the
unstressed front vowel is lost, type *scōs* — predicting *hē*, with no
final consonant, whose attested continuation would then need -ġ restored
from the inflected stem (Dahl's route). So the independently *motivated*
regular path through hiatus destroys precisely the [j] that ⟨heg⟩
requires, while the path that keeps [j] (early resyllabification before
hiatus resolution could apply) is phonetically trivial but not
independently *demonstrated* by parallel inherited /V.i/ words: the
candidate parallel *glīġ* is itself a contested re-formation
(Campbell §411; DERIVATIONS A1-iii), and no uncontested inherited */e.i/
simplex survives to exhibit the development.

Verdict. **Admissible, not demonstrated.** /hej/ is a possible regular
continuation and is spellable as ⟨heg/hei⟩; but the best-motivated hiatus
process yields long *hē* instead, and neither path has an independent
witness. No new sound law is introduced to rescue the form.

## Proposition 3 — The West Saxon spellings ⟨hieg⟩/⟨hig⟩/⟨hyg⟩ can continue *hei*

Manuscript-specific controls assembled this cycle (Skeat's parallel
gospels, base text CCCC 140, with Royal 1 A.xiv (A), Bodley 441,
Hatton 38):

- ***weġ*** (certified short /e/ + /j/, 31 tokens in Mark): always
  ⟨weg/wege/weges⟩, **never** ⟨wig⟩/⟨wyg⟩/⟨wieg⟩, in any witness.
- ***dæġ***: never ⟨dig⟩/⟨dyg⟩. The only deviant spellings are Hatton 38's
  late ⟨daig/daige⟩ — the same ME ⟨ai⟩ convention as its ⟨haig⟩ 'hay'.
- **The ⟨ig⟩/⟨yg⟩ convention**: Royal 1 A.xiv massively writes **hig**
  for *hī* 'they' and **hwig/hwyg** for *hwī* 'why' — inorganic ⟨g⟩
  attaches to **/iː/**, not to /ej/. So WS ⟨hig/hyg⟩ 'hay' presupposes
  high front vocalism, and ⟨hieg⟩ presupposes /iːe/ (īe).
- No WS control with short /e/ + /j/ ever surfaces with ⟨i/y/ie⟩
  vocalism; the e > i raising that *hei* → *hig* would require is
  refuted by *weġ* in the same manuscripts, written by the same scribes
  who wrote ⟨hig⟩ 'hay'.

The positive WS requirement: īe is the regular i-umlaut of ēa; the
protected oblique stem \*hēaj- (< \*hauj- with gemination/glide
protection) delivers *hīeġ(es)* exactly, with the *cīegan* < \*kaujan
(Anglian *cēgan*) equation as the structural parallel for *au + j*.

Verdict. **Outcome C for West Saxon — manuscript-grounded.** Inherited
*hei* cannot yield ⟨hieg/hig/hyg⟩ under the orthographic conventions those
manuscripts demonstrably follow. The WS direct form requires the
umlauted-diphthong stem, i.e. (under early apocope) the protected oblique
stem standing in the direct cell. **Under the early-apocope chronology,
the WS vocalism is itself the positive demonstration that levelling
occurred in WS** — analogy here is demonstrated by the vowel, not assumed
from the macron. (Under B2, the same surface forms are regular without
any levelling; this is why the lexeme still cannot discriminate the
chronologies.)

## Proposition 4 — Dialectally differentiated inheritance and levelling

Strongest support. (i) The non-WS record is *uniformly compatible* with
an unlevelled continuation: Anglian/Kentish ⟨heg⟩, ⟨hei-⟩ spellings are
quantity-neutral (Prop. 1, 2), VP *heg* sits beside VP *deg* (short,
second fronting) exactly as /hej/ would predict, and the earliest dated
token (S 1196, 859) is Kentish ⟨hei⟩. (ii) The comparative record
independently attests a split paradigm in this very word: OHG *hewi*
(ungeminated direct) beside *houwi* (geminate/oblique type), and Middle
Dutch **hoye** < \*hauj- (oblique stem) beside **houwe** < \*hawi (old
nominative stem) (Boutkan 1995:212–213, after Braune–Eggers and van
Loon) — doublet reflexes of nominative vs. oblique stems persisted and
levelled in *both* directions within West Germanic. A mixed OE paradigm
(direct *hei* ~ oblique \*hēaj- > *hēġe/hīeġes*) is therefore typologically
unexceptional, and levelling is **possible, not obligatory** (Hogg 1979's
hyll/hyse-type alternations; Adamczyk's *mete/mette*).

Strongest counterevidence. (i) Nothing *requires* the non-WS forms to be
unlevelled: levelled *hēġ* explains every non-WS spelling equally well,
and Farman's calibrated acutes (Prop. 5) are only suggestive of his own
long-vowel marking practice. (ii) The earliest printed quantity signal
we possess anywhere (Farman, s. x²) points long, but its manuscript
reading is unverified and the matched calibration is small and selected.
No positive short-vowel diagnostic (metre, lengthening, ME reflex of /ej/
distinct from /eːj/ — Minkova shows the position neutralized) survives
anywhere in the record. (iii) The S 1196 compound element may be a
compositional fossil regardless of the simplex paradigm.

Verdict. **Unfalsified but undemonstrated.** An Anglian/Kentish relic of
unlevelled *hei* remains strictly possible — ninth-century spelling could
not distinguish it — but no attestation requires it, and the only
quantity-sensitive witness leans the other way. The dialect-split
hypothesis (Outcome B) cannot be asserted as a finding; it survives as an
explicitly stated, currently untestable alternative.

## Proposition 5 — Farman's accents suggest a long nucleus in *hóeg*

The corrected accented-token census is in **FARMAN_ACCENTS.tsv**,
generated by `extract_farman_accents.py` from Tamoto's local full-text
extraction. The previous bounds mixed LF line numbers and Python
`splitlines()` indices; the corrected slice begins at the fol. 1r marker
and includes Farman's final *hleonadun* at Mk 2:15. It contains 196
non-target accented types/299 tokens plus *hóeg* (one type/two tokens),
197 types/301 Old English tokens total. The older 327-token figure and
Latin-token subtraction are withdrawn:

| class | types | tokens |
|---|---|---|
| etymologically long nucleus | 162 | 262 |
| short vowel before homorganic cluster (ld/nd/rd/ng) | 14 | 16 |
| etymologically short | 13 | 13 |
| uncertain | 7 | 8 |
| target (*hóeg*) | 1 | 2 |

The table classifies accented tokens only and cannot supply an
accentuation denominator. The bounded matched calibration in
`FARMAN_ACCENT_CALIBRATION.tsv` counts unaccented occurrences: short
V+glide *weg/dæg* 1/53 tokens accented (1/9 surface types; 1/2 lexical
types), long V+glide *stīg* 1/2 (1/2 surface types; one lexical type),
and short-HL controls 5/133 (5/23 surface types; 4/5 lexical types).
Eight ambiguous *gang*-family forms are reported separately and excluded.
This selected panel is small and descriptive, not a population estimate;
token repetitions are not independent. If the short-HL panel is counted
as short, the combined short rate is 6/186 tokens; if counted as
lengthened, the combined long-like rate is 6/135. The full census's
short-vowel counterexamples include *wég* (Mt 7:14), *stéfn* (Mt 2:18),
*mónige* (Mt 16:9), *ége*, *físcæs*, *scípa*, *wítga*, *wýrhtu*,
*ascákeþ*, and anomalous accents on function-word/prefix shorts (*ón*,
*ínn*, *únklene*, *ǽfter*).

The ⟨oe⟩ digraph, reassessed (correcting cycle 3). Kuhn (1945:646)
discusses *oele* (< Lat. *oleum*) as i-umlaut of **short** *o* retaining
a rounded spelling under Northumbrian influence: ⟨oe⟩ per se is evidence
about **quality/orthography, not length**. Farman's ⟨oe⟩ words divide
into (a) etymological œ̄ < \*ō + i (boéc, foérde, gemoéted, gedróefed,
fóedel-, geróefa — all long), (b) **inverted spellings of ē from other
sources**, crucially the *hēran* < \*hauzjan class (gehóerde, gehóerað,
gehóære), and (c) short œ (*oele*). Class (b) is a structural parallel to *hēġ* < \*hauj-: Farman treats
the unrounded ē of the \*au + j/z class as compatible with ⟨oe⟩. The
**acute** remains probabilistic evidence about his own quantity-marking
practice; the **⟨oe⟩** is evidence about his spelling/quality class, not
an independent measure of length. No likelihood ratio is calculated:
the two target occurrences are one word by one scribe, and the matched
sample is too small and selective for a precise probability. Both target
tokens postdate 950 and cannot establish ninth-century quantity.

Verdict. Farman's two printed *hóeg* acutes are suggestive of a long
nucleus in his late tenth-century witness, but the bounded calibration
does not quantify that suggestion reliably. Neither the acute nor ⟨oe⟩
alone establishes the ninth-century value.

## Proposition 6 — The attested dative stem is compatible with an inherited direct *hei*

Strongest support. The three manuscript-verified Paris Psalter datives
(*hege*, 101.4, 101.9, 102.14; BnF lat. 8824) attest the oblique stem
+ -e. In a mixed paradigm (direct *hei* ~ oblique protected stem), these
are exactly the expected oblique forms; they are not direct-cell
evidence, so they neither require nor exclude an inherited direct *hei*.
No standard of evidence may demand a pre-umlaut manuscript for the
reconstructed protected stem (brief §7).

Strongest counterevidence. The same tokens are equally the obliques of a
fully levelled *hēġ* paradigm — the eleventh-century attestation cannot
distinguish "mixed paradigm surviving" from "uniform levelled paradigm",
and every quantity-sensitive signal from the same period (Prop. 5) fits
the levelled reading. Moreover the PPs tradition itself writes *hig*
elsewhere (the WS-type vocalism), showing the levelled WS stem circulating
in the very witness that carries *hege*.

Verdict. **Compatible, and uninformative about the direct cell.** The
datives secure the oblique stem as a real OE form (cycle-3 finding,
retained); they say nothing about whether the direct cell ever housed
*hei*.

## Overall adjudication

1. **Is *hei* demonstrably regular?** As a serialization of the
   early-apocope output, yes (frozen Model A trace); as a *persisting* OE
   phonological form, only via the undemonstrated-but-admissible
   resyllabification (Prop. 2); the better-motivated hiatus path yields
   *hē*.
2. **Is *hei* attested?** The spelling, yes (S 1196; F.174). As an OE
   independent noun, no. As demonstrable continuity with Model A's
   output, no (Prop. 1.3).
3. **Can ⟨heg⟩ spell its continuation?** Yes — the dei/deg interchange
   proves ⟨eg⟩/⟨ei⟩ both spell /Vj/ regardless of quantity. This cuts
   both ways: the unlevelled form would be orthographically invisible,
   and its presence can never be read off non-WS spellings.
4. **West Saxon.** Cannot be accommodated without the umlauted-diphthong
   stem (Outcome C for WS, manuscript-grounded via the weg/dæg and
   hig-'they' controls). Under early apocope this *demonstrates*
   WS levelling rather than assuming it.
5. **Dialect-differentiated survival.** Unfalsified, undemonstrated
   (Prop. 4); to be stated in the article as an explicit open
   alternative, not a finding.
6. **Quantity.** Short /e/: no positive witness. Long /eː(j)/: Farman's
   acute is suggestive for his own s. x Mercian practice, but the
   calibration is bounded and non-decisive. Diphthong/disyllable: no
   direct witness; metre non-discriminating (PPs verse places the word in
   lifts/closed monosyllabic positions where resolution tests cannot
   apply — Fulk, Minkova; scansions in QUANTITY_EVIDENCE.md).
7. **PPs datives.** Oblique-stem attestations; neutral on the direct
   cell (Prop. 6).

**Consequence for the article (Stage B).** The central thesis sharpens
but its chronological conclusion stands: the WS vocalism demonstrates
levelling *if* apocope was early, B2 derives the same forms without
levelling, and the one form that could in principle have falsified
levelling — an unambiguous unlevelled direct form — is unrecoverable in
principle from non-WS orthography. The article should (i) correct 839 →
859/S 1196 and the F.174 dating, (ii) present the dei/wei/hig-'they'
controls, the calibration table, and the ⟨oe⟩ reassessment, (iii) state
Outcomes A/B/C explicitly and locate the evidence at C-for-WS with
B-unfalsified-elsewhere, and (iv) keep the negative chronological
conclusion on its now-stronger evidential footing.
