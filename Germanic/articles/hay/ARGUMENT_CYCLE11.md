# Does the inherited direct singular survive?

The test is whether the ungeminated nominative–accusative singular can
account for an attested English form without using material from another
cell. Case identifies the function of a token, not the ancestry of its stem.
Here *regular* means supported by independently motivated changes in the
requisite environment, not simply reproducible by a proposed rule.

## Adversarial assessment

**Strongest survival case.** The text with the earliest secure direct
uses has unaccented ⟨heg⟩, consistent with ordinary /hej/. The historical
starting sequence /he.i/ follows without a special change. *Clēa* shows
posttonic desyllabification after *w*-loss, while *sīem* rebuts a blanket
claim that front-vowel hiatus cannot persist in the Vespasian dialect.
Early *hei-* potentially adds a spelling with two vowel letters. A direct
route through retained hiatus followed by /i/ > /j/ before final
contraction is thus coherent and does not require length. Against it,
neither the conditioning nor the timing of the crucial syllabicity
change is supplied by these controls, and the compound is not a dated
simplex or an unambiguous reading of /e.i/. Directly reading ⟨heg⟩ as
hiatus adds an unsupported orthographic convention. The case makes an
alternative worth testing but does not independently motivate its
decisive step.

**Strongest protected-stem case.** The same reconstructible paradigm
offers a protected stem that supplies both /j/ and appropriate dialectal
vowels; the western high spellings, Farman's calibrated evidence for
length, and the absence of an unambiguous regular /e.i/ > /ej/ control
agree with the conventional outcome. The continental doublets and the
re-formation analysis of *glīg* make levelling credible even for a
mass noun. Brunner (§130 n. 3) offers an alternative analysis of
*glīg* with an inherited *j*; the word is not direct evidence
of restoration. Against a single generalized English history, Anglian *ē*
could equally come from contraction, and restoring one segment
requires less of the donor than supplying both nucleus and glide.
Neither long quantity (even if verified) nor overt *hege* would by
itself distinguish those two routes. Different copy dates and
dependent glosses do not supply a prehistoric trajectory.

**Discriminants.** Same-period final /e.i/ > /ej/ after *w*-loss, or a
secure local orthographic parallel for ⟨eg⟩ marking hiatus, would
strengthen survival; a dated, matching final contraction would weaken
it. An early contrast between a bare *\*hē* direct singular and a
glide-bearing protected form would support a donor analysis and
potentially segment-only restoration. A securely localized archaic
composition stem would only test a compound-specific history. Present
evidence supports the regular trajectory through hiatus and gives
positive reasons for the protected vocalism in West Saxon under early
apocope. It neither demonstrates survival nor establishes obligatory
whole-stem substitution in Anglian. For comparison with ‘bed’, the
English evidence therefore supports no simple count of required
analogies by chronology.
