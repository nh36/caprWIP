title: "Old English ‘hay’ and the scope of paradigmatic levelling"
abstract: |
  Early nasal apocope gives the inherited nominative–accusative singular
  of ‘hay’ an ungeminated history reaching disyllabic \emph{*he.i} after
  loss of *w*; later apocope predicts the long nucleus and palatal glide of conventional
  West Saxon *hīeġ*. Ninth-century Kentish *heiweg*, preferably
  ‘hay-road’, supplies an exact written counterpart to the first
  prediction, while early Anglian *heg* does not establish vowel
  length. The Anglian mid vowel can continue the inherited direct
  nucleus even if the final glide was restored. West Saxon high
  vocalism, by contrast, favours the protected stem if apocope was
  early. ‘Hay’ therefore does not impose the same analogical burden
  as light neuters with a directly attested geminate, such as ‘bed’.

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The chronological test

<!-- P-orders -->

Did West Germanic gemination precede or follow the loss of final
nasal \emph{*-ą}? The disagreement concerns the nasal ending, not
the early loss of the oral vowel in endings such as masculine
\emph{*-az}. Ringe and Taylor place both losses before gemination.
Hogg and Fulk retain \emph{*-ą} through gemination, though the
oral vowel has already been lost
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–53, 173; @HoggFulk2011, pp. 20–21 §2.23;
@Boutkan1995, pp. 209–213].

<!-- P-tradeoff -->

This difference changes the relation between the stems of light
neuter ja-nouns. If the nominative–accusative singular lost its
final vowel early, its \emph{*j} ceased to be prevocalic and the
stem escaped gemination. Forms such as the genitive and dative
retained a following vowel and so could supply a geminated
stem. On the late chronology the direct singular geminated too.
Thus Old English *bedd* ‘bed’ has a geminate predicted by the
late order, whereas the early order can explain it by extension
from the protected cells. Conversely, ungeminated continental
*beti*-type forms count against the late order if they continue
the inherited direct stem rather than a secondary formation
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–47; @HoggFulk2011, §2.23;
@Boutkan1995, pp. 435–436; @Stiles2013, pp. 15–16]. Agreement
with a normalized headword is therefore less informative than
knowing which cell supplied the stem and which of its features
would have developed without levelling.

<!-- P-hay-predictions -->

‘Hay’ makes that distinction especially useful because the two
orders differ in the source of the vowel and the final palatal
rather than in a surviving geminate. Under early loss, the
inherited direct singular \emph{*hawją} develops through
\emph{*hawi}, \emph{*hæwi} and \emph{*hewi} to \emph{*hei},
initially with hiatus. Under late loss the direct singular
develops like the protected cells, yielding the long nucleus
and glide conventionally represented by West Saxon *hīeġ*.
Ringe and Taylor adopt early loss but reach the protected-type
outcome by replacing direct \emph{*hæwi} with the stem
\emph{*hēaj-} before i-umlaut. From this substituted stem they
derive both the high West Saxon and the mid Anglian reflexes
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 173, 246].

<!-- P-motivation -->

Those treatments do not discuss the ninth-century Kentish
*heiweg* [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 173, 246; @Sweet1885, p. 450,
Ct. 43]. On the preferred interpretation ‘hay-road’, the
first element has exactly the letters ⟨hei⟩, corresponding
to the sequence predicted if the direct stem is allowed to
develop without substitution under their ordering. The
compound does not establish how the sequence was pronounced
or which cell supplied it. The early Anglian simplex
spellings ⟨heg⟩ and ⟨heig⟩ give a separate test: the noun
occurs in direct syntax, and the mid vowel can be compared
with the outcome of the unremodelled stem
[@Stevenson1843VP; @Skeat1878, p. 55].

<!-- P-thesis -->

The question, then, is whether the inherited direct singular
survived without analogy in any early Old English tradition.
Under early loss, the high West Saxon vowels favour the
protected stem, but the Anglian mid vowel need not have the
same source. Even if the inherited vowels contracted and a
palatal had to be supplied from another form, replacing the
vowel as well would not be necessary. In that respect Anglian
‘hay’ differs from ‘bed’: the latter cannot inherit its
geminate directly under the early order, whereas ‘hay’ can
retain the historical source of its vowel even if survival of
the whole stem cannot be demonstrated.

## 1.2 The two regular derivations

<!-- P-trace-diverge -->

Table 1 starts both derivations from the consonantal
\emph{*wj} of \emph{*hawją}. The first difference is whether
\emph{*j} is still followed by a vowel when the labial could
geminate. Early loss of \emph{*-ą} strands singleton \emph{*j}
after the labial of a light stem. It vocalizes to \emph{*i},
leaving a single intervocalic \emph{*w}. If \emph{*-ą}
instead remains through gemination, prevocalic \emph{*j}
triggers gemination before the ending is lost. Vocalization
of a stranded singleton cannot then apply: the geminated
labial supplies the second member of a diphthong and
\emph{*j} remains consonantal. The ordering thus determines
both the material available for the nucleus and the segment
that conditions umlaut.

| Gemination before prevocalic \emph{*j}, if the ending remains | Not yet applied | \emph{*hawją} > \emph{*hawwją} |
| Nasal apocope | \emph{*hawją} > \emph{*hawj} | \emph{*hawwją} > \emph{*hawwj} |

| Diphthong fronting and development | No diphthong | \emph{*hauj} > \emph{*aeuj} > \emph{*ēaj} |

<!-- P-trace-end -->

In the early order, fronting and i-umlaut give \emph{*hewi};
subsequent loss of \emph{*w} before unstressed \emph{*i}
leaves \emph{*hei}, with short stressed /e/ followed by
syllabic /i/. This is a regular stage feeding any later
resolution of hiatus, not a claim that two syllables
persisted into written Old English. In the late order the
diphthong develops to \emph{*ēa} before being mutated by
consonantal \emph{*j}. In the conventional West Saxon
representation \emph{*hīeġ}, the macron marks a long
nucleus and the dot marks /j/. It is not a transcription
of the regular outcome in every dialect.[^trace-notation]

These are regular predictions of the two orderings with the
stated treatment of the labials, not complete analyses attributed
to either grammar. Campbell and Hogg retain the labial glide
longer in their treatments of the geminated sequence. Ringe and
Taylor derive \emph{*auj} from a reversible gemination rather than
beginning with a diphthong. Dahl’s analysis starts with a
diphthongal representation and gives a separate account of final
*ġ* (Section 6) [@Campbell1959, pp. 45–46 §120.2;
@HoggGrammar1992, §3.18; @RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 173;
@Dahl1938, pp. 81–86 and Excursus].

## 1.3 Ringe and Taylor’s protected-stem analysis

<!-- P-substitution -->

Even under early loss, the protected cells have a following
vowel at gemination. In Ringe and Taylor’s analysis, their
stem develops along the line \emph{*[hawʲwʲ-]} >
\emph{*hauj-} > \emph{*hēaj-}. In Old High German they
identify *hewi* as the inherited direct singular and
*houwi* as an extension of the geminated stem. For English
they set out the substitution in their treatment of
umlaut [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 173, 246]:

**Substitution before i-umlaut:** direct \emph{*hæwi} is replaced by
protected \emph{*hēaj-}, as endingless \emph{*hēaj}.

This replacement abandons the continuation of \emph{*hæwi}
through \emph{*hewi} to hiatus: both the short stem vowel
and the final syllabic \emph{*i} are replaced. After the
substitution, i-umlaut conditioned by \emph{*j} and regular
dialectal development give West Saxon *hīeġ* and Mercian
and Northumbrian *hēġ*. Ringe and Taylor do not identify
the English donor cell or continue the unremodelled English
line beyond \emph{*hæwi} in these passages. Their account
shows how the protected stem can yield the attested dialectal
contrast; it does not show that the contrast requires
replacement in Anglian. To determine what the direct stem
could have supplied, the recorded forms must be examined
for what they establish about the vowel and the palatal.

[^trace-notation]: Initial *h* is conventional throughout. Both derivations front the initial fricative from [x] to [ç] before umlaut. The notation \emph{*aeu} is schematic for a fronted diphthong stage, not three syllabic vowels.

# 2. The Old English attestations

## 2.1 *Cyniges heiweg*

<!-- P-char-reading -->

The sequence ⟨hei⟩ occurs in *heiweg* in the bounds of the
Kentish charter S 1196. Ringe and Taylor do not discuss the
compound in their principal treatments of ‘hay’ (Section 1.1).
On the surviving mid-ninth-century
sheet (BL Cotton Augustus II 16), Sweet reads *cyniges hei
weg*, followed by *stret to scufelingforde*; the bounds mention
the burg-dwellers’ meadows and later *an cyninges strete*
[@Sweet1885, p. 450, Ct. 43].[^s1196] The reading and word
division are Sweet’s, not the result of a fresh collation.
If the first element is ‘hay’, the compound gives an early
written counterpart to the \emph{*hei} reached by the direct
stem under early apocope.

<!-- P-char-positive -->

The meadows give a reason independent of the derivation to take
the first element as ‘hay’: a road serving meadow land could
be named for the crop. Langlands’s comparison with S 445 makes
that function concrete: *higweges* occurs beside a meadow, and
a much later map calls a route there Mower’s Lane
[@Langlands2019, pp. 45–46, 109, 126]. S 445 survives in a
fifteenth-century copy, and neither its bounds nor the map
identifies the function of the S 1196 road.

The spelling also fits ‘hay’. Sweet lists the first element
under that word; Bryan gives the historical comparison with
Germanic \emph{*hauja-} and adduces Kentish *dei* ‘day’
[@Bryan1915, pp. 8, 23, 25].[^bryan] *Dei* establishes
⟨ei⟩ as a spelling for a vowel followed by a palatal glide,
not for disyllabic /e.i/; the two letters do not mark the
quantity distinction Bryan reconstructs. Dahl adopts ‘hay-road’
and includes it alongside *Hegforde*, without adding an
independent argument from the bounds [@Dahl1938, ‘hay’
discussion].[^dahl] Other potentially relevant *hig* or *heg*
road names are later, survive in later copies, or are disputed.
Setting and spelling thus support ‘hay-road’ together, but
a road by meadows could also be the king’s highway.

<!-- P-char-rival -->

If the first element is the adjective ‘high’, the compound
has no bearing on the development of ‘hay’. Bosworth–Toller
lists it under *hēah-weg*, with *hēah-strǣt* as a semantic
parallel; Clark Hall prints ‘highway?’, and Cooper adopts
that interpretation [@BosworthToller1898, s.v. *hēah-weg*;
@ClarkHall1960, p. 152; @Cooper2003, p. 61 nn. 151, 155].
If *cyniges hei weg* and *cyninges strete* refer to the same
route, the latter expression supports the identification
of a significant road. It does not show that the first
expression named it for its status rather than its use.

<!-- P-char-phon -->

The semantic parallel leaves a difference in form unexplained.
For ‘hay’, the early-apocope derivation gives the sequence
\emph{*he.i}, which could develop a palatal glide; a protected
stem already supplies a source for a palatal (see below).
Kentish *dei* illustrates how a vowel and glide could be
written ⟨ei⟩. The ‘highway’ interpretation must additionally
explain what became of the velar fricative of *hēah-* before
the *w* of *weg* and how that development yielded a form
written ⟨hei-⟩.
Neither dictionary supplies such a local correspondence, and
the charter comparison found no securely comparable
ninth-century Kentish example. This does not make the required
development impossible, but it does give ‘hay’ the better
phonological comparison. The spelling alone does not determine
the vowel’s quality or quantity.[^char-minor]

[^char-minor]: An enclosure term is semantically possible but likewise has no demonstrated local ⟨hei-⟩ reflex [@Wager2017, pp. 169–174].

<!-- P-char-document -->

Cooper excludes S 1196 from his survey of highway terminology
[@Cooper2003, p. 61 nn. 151, 155]. The uncertain date of
the transaction, the abrupt transition from Latin to English,
and the position of the *communis via* clause are problems
of date and transmission, whichever etymology is adopted.
The Latin phrase is not a gloss on *heiweg*; nor does it
settle how the two mentions of a king’s road are related.
A different problem arises only if *heiweg* means ‘highway’:
it would be an isolated ninth-century instance of that term
in the material under comparison. On the ‘hay-road’ reading
there is no such early attestation to explain. That removes
the chronological difficulty for ‘highway’ terminology, not
the irregularities of the document, and is not an independent
reason to prefer ‘hay-road’.

<!-- P-char-verdict -->

‘Hay-road’ is preferable because it accounts both for the
setting and for the written form. The meadows and the S 445
comparison give a reason for naming the road after hay; the
Kentish spelling convention and the predicted sequence
\emph{*he.i} make ⟨hei⟩ intelligible for that word.
‘Highway’ can account for the road’s significance, but still
lacks a local parallel for the required reflex of *hēah-*.
The edition therefore gives good reason to recognize ⟨hei⟩ in
a ninth-century form of ‘hay’. Establishing the more likely
lexeme, however, does not identify which historical stem
supplied the first element.

<!-- P-char-compound -->

The hay-house compounds show that a stem shaped like the
direct singular could be used in composition. Glossarial
*highus* and *heghus* render ‘hay store’; their first elements
match simplex *hig* and *heg*, with no visible connector.
Other ja-stems show the same pattern, but *bedtīd*, for
example, already has the geminate of simplex *bedd*.[^compound-forms]
Agreement between the compound and the direct form thus tells
us which stem shape was available for composition, not whether
that shape antedates levelling. An old compound could preserve
an earlier direct-shaped stem after the simplex was remodelled;
a later formation could use the stem then current.

Nor need an inherited compound have had a direct-shaped first
member. Campbell’s examples of connecting vowels, including
*rīcedōm*, show why the word-final development cannot simply be
assumed in composition [@Campbell1959, §359]. If a vowel
followed \emph{*j}, it could preserve the environment for
gemination. That history would require evidence of the connector,
the age of the compound and the subsequent loss of the vowel.
Neither *heiweg* nor the hay-house forms preserves such a connector.

A protected stem could also give ⟨hei⟩. Kentish *ċēgan* ‘call’
independently illustrates a mid-vowel outcome of
i-umlaut of \emph{*ēa}. For a stem of that type with a final palatal,
*dei* supplies a plausible spelling convention
[@RingeTaylor2014, p. 246; @Bryan1915, pp. 23, 25]. No
independently long local control establishes ⟨ei⟩ for /eːj/,
but neither is the spelling known to be restricted to a
short vowel. A secure contrast between a Kentish compound
and its contemporary simplex, or evidence for a connector,
could distinguish histories that the letters alone leave open.

[^compound-forms]: *Highus* glosses *faenile*, *heghus* glosses *fenile* [@WrightWuelcker1884, I, cols. 186.13, 237.36]. For direct-shaped *hrycgmearh* and *cynlīc*, see Campbell [-@Campbell1959, §§348–349 n. 3]; for further connecting vowels (*endeleas*, *lǣcecræft*) and their absence or later loss in other compounds, see §359.

<!-- P-char-close -->

On the preferred reading of S 1196, the regular early-apocope
sequence has an exact written counterpart in a probable
ninth-century form of ‘hay’. In the sources examined, no
other early example of exact ⟨hei⟩ was found: ⟨heig⟩
is a different spelling.[^early-hei] The thirteenth-century
Worcester glossary F.174 has *Fenum, hei, uel græs* beside
*hig* in the older Julius parallel; Worcester also writes
*wei* ‘way’ [@WrightWuelcker1884, I, cols. 325.21, 547].
Worcester’s ⟨hei⟩ therefore need not preserve hiatus or
continue the Kentish form. Neither Worcester nor the later Middle
English variants supplies another Old English attestation of ⟨hei⟩
[@MiddleEnglishDictionaryHei, s.v. *hei*].

The early correspondence calls for explanation, but it does
not establish short quantity, retained hiatus or descent
from the inherited direct singular. A compound can preserve
a stem shape without revealing its grammatical source.
To investigate the survival of the inherited direct singular
more directly, we turn to simplex attestations in which the
grammatical function of the noun can be established.

[^early-hei]: The comparison covered the *híg/hég* and road-name entries, the principal glossary loci, and the Kentish road comparanda [@BosworthToller1898, s.v. *hēah-weg*; @Toller1921, s.v. *híg*; @WrightWuelcker1884, I, cols. 289.45, 325.21, 402.36, 547; @Cooper2003, p. 61].

## 2.2 Simplex forms

<!-- P-simplex-anchor -->

What form did ‘hay’ have when used as a singular subject or direct
object in its own right? The earliest secure direct objects occur
in the ninth-century Mercian gloss of the Vespasian Psalter. It has
eleven instances of endingless *heg* in the Psalms and one in the
canticles. Four Psalm occurrences are secure direct objects
(103:14, 104:35, 105:20, 146:8). In *forð gelædes heg neatum*
‘brings forth hay for cattle’ (103:14), for example, *heg* is
the thing brought forth [@Stevenson1843VP; @Kuhn1965VP]. The
Old English Bede gives independent accusative evidence: in
*on sumera heg ne māweþ* ‘does not mow hay in summer’, *heg*
is the object of ‘mow’. This is the reading of Miller’s composite
edition, not a reading independently checked across the surviving
tenth-century copies [@Miller1890, I, p. 28].

The late tenth-century Gospel glosses add evidence from both
functions. At Mt 6:30 Aldred’s *gers ɫ heg* and Farman’s
*londes hóeg* belong to the object of ‘clothe’. At Jn 6:10
Aldred’s *gærs ɫ heig micil* is the subject of the existential
clause; Owun has *heg ɫ gers micel* in the corresponding
gloss [@Skeat1887, p. 59; @Tamoto2013, p. 19; @Skeat1878, p. 55].
These syntactic contexts identify the direct function, not the
ancestry of the stem or the sounds represented by its letters.
The dates are those of the surviving English writing: the Latin
manuscripts may be older than their glosses, while a copy may
postdate the English translation or gloss it transmits. The
date of copying does not by itself establish the age of the
exemplar.[^simplex-case]

[^simplex-case]: Neither Aldred’s *ofer heg* nor Farman’s *on hóeg* at Mt 14:19 has distinctive case marking [@Skeat1887, p. 123; @Tamoto2013, p. 47]. A positional reading of *gesittan on hóeg* favours the dative; taking the hay as the goal of sitting down permits an accusative. Position does not require the dative after *ofer*: CCCC 140, Mk 6:39, has accusative agreement in *sæte ofer þæt grene hig* despite the seated posture [@Skeat1871, pp. 48–49]. In Vespasian Hymn 7:4, snow falls *ofer heg* in a comparison with rain falling *ofer græs*: motion favours the accusative, but the English does not determine it. Nor does *swa swa* assign case: in withering similes such as *spe spe heg eorðan* (Ps 71:16), an elliptical comparison with the subject is plausible, but there is no separate subject agreement with the noun [@Stevenson1843VP; @Kuhn1965VP]. The corresponding Latin cases do not resolve these English ambiguities.

<!-- P-simplex-distribution -->

The early Anglian witnesses give mid-vowel spellings: Vespasian
*heg* and the Northumbrian *heg* and *heig* of Aldred and Owun.
Farman’s *hóeg* belongs to his own Mercian Matthew gloss; its
acute is evidence to be assessed for that witness, not a mark
carried by the unaccented spellings. The Lindisfarne *hehg*
and corrected *heg* at Mt 14:19 are known from the printed
editions and apparatus, not from inspection of the manuscript
alteration, and are readings of a single occurrence.[^simplex-editions]
Owun’s *heg* is separately written, but his John gloss and
Aldred’s transmit closely related material; copying directly
from the surviving Lindisfarne manuscript is not established
[@Kotake2016, pp. 377–396].

The West Saxon evidence instead includes high-vowel spellings:
*hig* in the West Saxon Gospels (Mk 6:39) and *hyg* in the
Lambeth gloss (e.g. Ps 101:5) [@Skeat1871, pp. 48–49;
@Lindeloef1909, p. 158]. These spellings pose a different
problem of vowel quality, not a contrast in measured length.
Table 2 gives the principal witnesses and Appendix A compares
the Psalter glosses by locus. Later witnesses do not sort into
two uniform groups: the metrical Paris Psalter has both mid-
and high-vowel spellings, and manuscripts of the *Dialogues*
vary in spelling and sometimes in the word used. Neither the
provenance of a manuscript nor the origin of its scribe by itself
assigns a dialect to the transmitted reading. Nor does recurrence
in related copies or glosses count as independent evidence of the
same development: the Junius Psalter gloss, for instance, is
textually related to Vespasian’s.

which layer supplied Eadwine’s ⟨i⟩. The readings are neither evidence
of a local twelfth-century Kentish pronunciation nor securely West
Saxon. The usual citation form *hīeġ*, with its quantity and palatal
marking, is a normalization.

# 3. Historical phonology

## 3.1 Development to hiatus

On the early-apocope and consonantal analyses the direct form
develops as follows:

\emph{*hawją} > \emph{*hawi} > \emph{*hæwi} > \emph{*hewi} > \emph{*he.i}.

Once \emph{*-ą} is lost, stranded \emph{*j} is syllabic. The \emph{*wi} sequence
permits Anglo-Frisian fronting, followed by i-umlaut; Ringe and
Taylor’s parallels are \emph{*awi} ‘ewe’ and \emph{*klawiþō} ‘itch’
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 148, 237; @Campbell1959, §579(4)].
After umlaut and before general syncope, noninitial \emph{*w} was lost
before fully unstressed \emph{*i}. The unstressed vowel was still
syllabic at the point of loss [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 257–261;
@Campbell1959, §§237.1b, 406].

The loss is not peculiar to this noun. It appears in \emph{*gliwi}
> *glī* ‘merrymaking’, in \emph{*niwi-} > *nī-* ‘new’ in compounds,
and in \emph{*pylwī} ‘pillow’ > *pyle*. In the weak verbs it is
conditioned by the vowel of the ending: compare *giereþ* ‘prepares’
and its infinitive *gierwan*. Later restoration is possible where
other parts of a paradigm retained the consonant; forms such as
*streide* ‘strewed’ and *gecēd* ‘calls’ preserve evidence of the
loss. *Eowu* ‘ewe’ does not provide retention in the same environment
if it had already moved to the ō-stems. Ringe and Taylor treat
*cleweþa* ‘itch’ as exceptional and suggest lexical analogy or
competing suffix formations [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 258–261;
@Campbell1959, pp. 166–167, 327–328].

## 3.2 Contraction and glide formation

The outcome \emph{*he.i} is disyllabic. Table 3 distinguishes the
positions and vowel sequences represented by the principal controls.
The identical final vowels in *glī* do not establish the chronology
of unlike final vowels. Vespasian *gecēd* is especially useful for
dating contraction in the tradition which writes *heg*: \emph{*e.i}
contracted before the dental in that verb. It does not show that the
same contraction preceded the recording of a word with final \emph{*e.i}.
*Sǣ* and *ǣ* have final unlike vowels in the reconstructed input,
but their first vowel was already long; loss of *i* gives the same
observable result [@Campbell1959, §237.1b; @RingeTaylor2014,
pp. 259–260, 318].

The early glosses write *strēidæ/strēide* (Érfurt 899, Corpus 1910),
and the verse at *Beowulf* 2436 scans with disyllabic *strēd*.
These are independent indications that a sequence produced by
*w*-loss could remain disyllabic nonfinally; a copied spelling does
not date a local pronunciation. Vespasian *hīe* and *sīe* offer
comparisons within the Mercian tradition, with *sīem* at Ps 26:4
supporting two syllables. They contain *i.e*, not *e.i*, and
do not use ⟨g⟩ to write the hiatus [@HoggGrammar1992,
§§5.132–134, 5.146]. These examples permit coexistence of contracted and uncontracted
sequences in different environments. In particular, *gecēd* and
*sīem* make it unnecessary to assume a single date for the
disappearance of hiatus even in the same gloss tradition.
Persistence is the historical inference here: it gives a sequence
created by *w*-loss an interval in which the treatment of an
unstressed final vowel could differ from that of a vowel before
a consonant.

Desyllabification after consonant loss is independently attested
in a different setting. Hogg derives *clēa* ‘claw’ from *kla.wu*
through *kla.u* and monosyllabic *klau*; the plural forms
*cnēo* and *trēo* illustrate comparable treatment of back vowels
[@HoggGrammar1992, §3.19]. The outputs belong to the inherited
diphthong types *au* and *eu*. Campbell’s prevocalic gliding in
Latin loans and metrical synizesis are also distinct: the former
involves a following vowel, and the latter does not establish
ordinary lexical syllabification [@Campbell1959, §§512, 553–557].
The comparison supplies a mechanism, not the same output: when
*w* disappears, a following unstressed high vowel can lose
syllabicity instead of being deleted. The back vowel of *clēa*
could join an established diphthong class, whereas there is no
such class to appeal to for *hei*. But /ej/ needs no phonemic
falling diphthong /ei̯/. Old English already had nuclei followed
by /j/, as in *weġ* ‘way’. A shift of unstressed /i/ into the
coda after a stressed /e/ would use an existing syllable
structure. *Sīem* does not model that shift: it supports
retention of unlike front vowels, and its second vowel is not
the high vowel required by the proposal.

The retention and desyllabification comparisons motivate
\emph{*he.i} > \emph{*hej} as a candidate development: after
*w*-loss, fully unstressed final /i/ following stressed short
/e/ could become a coda while hiatus still existed. Prior
contraction would remove that input. The evidence does not
establish this development in Anglian, let alone throughout
Old English. Contraction has a closer control for the same
vowels before a dental and closer controls for final position
with different inputs. Neither process can be timed in the
precise environment from those comparisons.

## 3.3 Orthography and vowel quantity

The spelling ⟨eg⟩ regularly writes /Vj/ [@HoggGrammar1992, §2.76].
Comparanda such as *deg/dei* ‘day’ and Farman’s *weig* ‘way’
have an independent consonantal source for the glide. They tell
us how a glide could be written, not whether *i* in ‘hay’ became
a glide. Campbell discusses *streide* and Kentish *gecēide* near
examples of palatal vocalization; the two vowel letters alone do
not identify the direction of change. Vespasian *hefie* beside
⟨-ige⟩ illustrates vocalization, not the creation of a consonant
from syllabic *i* [@Campbell1959, §§237.1b, 266–267].
In Lindisfarne *heista/neista* beside *hesta/nesta* can reflect
uncontracted hiatus after *h*-loss, but the only apparent ⟨ig⟩
counterpart, *heigsta* (Brunner’s *hēiȝsta*), is isolated and he treats it as a
probable error [@SieversBrunner1965, p. 112 §129 n. 5]. It
concerns a nonfinal sequence from a different source. No independently
interpretable early Mercian, Northumbrian or Kentish spelling
examined here establishes ⟨eg⟩ or ⟨ig⟩ as notation for retained
final /e.i/.

The relevant contrasts are /he.i/ with a short stressed vowel
and two syllables, /hej/ with a short nucleus and a glide,
conventional Anglian /heːj/, and the conventional West Saxon
long diphthongal or high nucleus plus /j/. Contraction predicts
long *ē* but does not itself produce a final glide. Ordinary ⟨heg⟩ accommodates both
/ej/ and /eːj/, and that compatibility is not positive evidence
for either quantity. Nor does the additional ⟨i⟩ in Aldred’s
⟨heig⟩ establish an extra mora or syllable. His two spellings are
compatible with the same /Vj/ sequence, and the sources consulted
do not mark a quantity distinction in these instances
[@Jones1989, pp. 21, 55, 149–152]. Vespasian’s ⟨heg⟩ is
unaccented in the editions; Kentish *hei-* has no independent
quantity marker. West Saxon ⟨hig/hyg⟩ indicates high vocalism, not
its length. The West Saxon ⟨ie⟩ of the conventional reconstruction
must not be treated as an observed spelling in an early West Saxon
manuscript. The Eadwine readings do not independently supply one.

Farman provides more specific evidence. Three editions print
an acute in both instances of *hóeg* [@KembleHardwick1858,
pp. 55, 117; @Skeat1887, pp. 59, 123; @Tamoto2013, pp. 19, 47].
Neither folio is in the digitized selection consulted. The
accent calibration in Table 4 counts occurrences in Farman’s stint

ignoring the acute; an accented type has at least one accented

The metrical evidence does not settle the issue. If the written
direct forms are monosyllables closed by /j/, both vowel quantities
can scan as heavy. The two *hege gelic* lines in Paris (101:4,

short lift or with the second syllable of *hege* in the dip.
The verse tradition itself does not support a finer quantitative
inference [@Fulk1992Meter, pp. 410–414; @Toswell1990, ch. 6].
Later merger of long and short vowels before /j/ also deprives
Middle English *hay* and *way* of discriminatory value
[@Minkova2014, p. 178].

# 4. Historical morphology

## 4.1 Grammatical cells

develop before gemination (\emph{*-ai} > \emph{*-ē} and \emph{*-ō} > \emph{*-u}),

counterparts here. Thus the attested paradigm contains less
evidence for the prehistoric stem alternation than its handbook
representation suggests, but more evidence for inflection than
the isolated citation form would suggest.

## 4.2 The direction and scope of levelling

The protected genitive or dative could supply a stem even if
‘hay’ was seldom plural. The same stem in several grammatical
cells and pressure from uniform paradigms of other ja-stems
give reasons for extending it to the direct singular. But
related ja-stems show levelling in both directions
[@Hogg1979Analogy, pp. 68–73]. A protected stem in the
reconstruction does not tell us which alternant speakers
used as the basis for remodelling.

Ball and Stiles’s comparison of ‘yellow’ and ‘meal, flour’
shows what a directional argument would involve. They derive
the broken stem \emph{*geolw-} from \emph{*gelw-} in inflected
forms, including the weak adjective. The direct form
\emph{*gelu} lacked the \emph{*lw} cluster. Early glossary
forms still show the direct monophthong; later Anglian
back-mutation could give *geolu* without levelling. In West
Saxon, where they expect an unremodelled \emph{*gielu} by
palatal diphthongization, they infer instead the extension of
the broken stem, probably before i-mutation
[@BallStiles1983, pp. 15–18 §§4.2–4.3].

For ‘meal’ the recorded variants include *melu* beside
*meolu* and *melwes* beside *meolwes*. In Ball and Stiles’s
account, breaking regularly gives \emph{*meolw-} in the
inflected stem, while \emph{*melu} escapes breaking and can
later give *meolu* by back-mutation. Between these changes,
they propose that the more frequent direct \emph{*melu} of
the uncountable noun supplied its vowel to new inflected
\emph{*melw-} forms. Those inflected by-forms in turn
supported the direct monophthong against the new *meolu*.
They interpret the inflected *smer(u)w-* forms of ‘fat’,
including early Épinal–Érfurt datives, in the same way:
direct vocalism replaced the breaking diphthong before
back-mutation. ‘Tar’ limits the comparison. They find early
direct *teru/tero* but no inflected *terw-*; the reported
genitive plural *teorwena* may reflect different usage,
though the distribution could also reflect transmission
[@BallStiles1983, pp. 18–19 §4.3 and n. 18].

‘Meal’ and ‘fat’ have *wa*-stems and actual monophthongal
inflected variants. ‘Hay’ has neither that structural
alternation nor diagnostic vocalism in its recorded datives.
Ball and Stiles’s frequency explanation is inferred, and it
depends on their chronology of breaking. Its relevance is
the direction of transfer: an uncountable noun can supply a
reason for preserving direct vocalism, or for extending it
to inflected forms, rather than automatically taking the
protected stem as the donor. Of the 51 case-assigned ‘hay’
occurrences, 47 are direct singular; the distribution of
these glosses and the absence of a secure plural cannot
establish the relative frequency of prehistoric stems.
But the attested morphology supplies no contrary reason
to assume that protected vocalism had the advantage in
Anglian.

These are different operations. The inherited direct stem
could survive without spreading; its vocalism could spread
to a dative without eliminating *-e*; and an endingless dative
such as *mid hig* could reflect loss of inflection without
any prehistoric stem transfer. Neither *mid hig* nor the overt
Paris *hege* distinguishes these histories. Conversely,
Ringe and Taylor’s transfer replaces the vowel and palatal
together, whereas an inherited contracted vowel would need
only the palatal supplied. Wright’s secondary glide between
vowels and its subsequent extension to the direct singular
provides a precedent for separating the two, not a direct
derivation of /j/ from short /e.i/ [@Wright1914, §§270, 357].

## 4.3 ‘Glee’ and the continental comparisons

The light neuter ‘glee’ provides two different comparisons.
Ringe and Taylor’s inherited direct \emph{*gliwi} loses \emph{*w} and
contracts identical vowels: \emph{*gliwi} > \emph{*gli.i} > *glī*. Their
protected \emph{*[gliwwjas]} is represented by *glīowes*, with a
labial in a form with an overt ending. The first comparison
is unusually close at the point of loss of \emph{*w}*, but /i.i/
has no vowel-quality decision to make at contraction. It is
the coexistence of different stems, not the precise treatment
of hiatus, that more directly parallels the morphological
problem in ‘hay’ [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 259, 318].

Campbell treats *glīg* and *glīges* as re-formations beside
*glīw/glīow*. Existing alternations between *ī(g)* and
*īw/īow* elsewhere in the lexicon could give a new *g*-form
without changing the historical source of the direct nucleus.
This is not a claim that the *w*-bearing genitive *glīowes*
itself furnished a palatal consonant. Brunner instead allows
singleton *w* to combine with preceding *i* as *iu* while
*j* remains. Umlaut gives West Saxon *ie* or *ī*, with a
palatal segment; with a protected ending the labial can
geminate and persist, giving the competing *w*-forms. He
compares compounds with *nīġ-* ‘new’ and contrasts *glīg*
and its compounds with *glīw*, *glēo* and related compounds
[@Campbell1959, §§120.2, 411, 579(4);
@SieversBrunner1965, §§130 n. 3, 173, 247 n. 1].

On Campbell’s analysis the doublet is a precedent for
modifying the end of a word without replacing its nucleus.
On Brunner’s, some forms can have a palatal from the outset;
secondary extension remains possible, but is not necessary
to originate the segment. This alternative matters for ‘hay’
because the fate of a labial and the source of a palatal need
not be coupled. However, it cannot preserve inherited *j*
in a derivation that has already vocalized it to syllabic *i*
in \emph{*hawi}. Applying Brunner’s mechanism directly to ‘hay’
would require an independently justified reconstruction in
which *j* survived that stage. Neither interpretation of
‘glee’ proves the origin of the final glide of *heg*.

Old High German *hewi/houwi* and Middle Dutch *hoye/houwe* are
documented doublets. Ringe and Taylor identify *hewi* as the inherited
direct singular and *houwi* as the generalized protected stem. A
late-apocope account can derive *houwi* regularly but must explain
*hewi* independently. Boutkan’s assignment of the Dutch doublets
to grammatical stems depends on his reconstruction of the ordering
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 173; @Boutkan1995, pp. 212–213].
The doublets document the coexistence of different stem shapes;
they do not independently establish a direction of levelling in
English. Gothic *hawi*, outside West Germanic, cannot decide
whether a West Germanic form escaped gemination.

# 5. The interpretation of the Old English forms

## 5.1 Earliest Anglian and Kentish evidence

The two earliest written points of comparison have different
strengths. Kentish ⟨hei⟩, on the ‘hay-road’ reading, shows
the early sequence of letters but not its quantity or
syllabification. Mercian ⟨heg⟩ belongs securely to the noun
and occurs in direct syntax. It more naturally writes /Vj/
than hiatus, but it no more identifies the source of the
nucleus than the Kentish first element identifies a case form.

On the regular-survival account, *w*-loss leaves \emph{*he.i}, and
desyllabification gives /hej/ without borrowing either the
nucleus or the glide. The result uses the ordinary Mercian
spelling ⟨heg⟩. This is the best regular-survival reading of
that spelling: an interpretation with retained hiatus would
require an independently unestablished use of ⟨g⟩. The
desyllabification account instead has a structural precedent
in *clēa*, and the persistence of other hiatus sequences makes
it unnecessary to assume immediate contraction. Its added
hypothesis is the order and conditioning of final /i/ in this
environment. An independently dated contraction of a matched
sequence before Vespasian would weigh directly against it.

An alternative uses the independently motivated contraction
process, for which there is a closer control in the same vowel
sequence but a different position. If \emph{*he.i} contracted to \emph{*hē},
restoration of /j/ would supply Anglian ⟨heg⟩ without changing
the origin of the nucleus. *Glī/glīg* supports a repair of this
scope on Campbell’s account. The cost is positive: contraction
destroys the potential inherited source of /j/, and neither
bare \emph{*hē} ‘hay’ nor a contemporary contrast between that form
and a glide-bearing stem has been identified. This account is
well motivated where both length and a glide need explaining,
but it cannot be selected merely from an ordinary ⟨heg⟩.

Substitution of the protected stem has the independent support
of levelling in the class and gives the Anglian vowel and glide
in one operation followed by regular sound changes. It also
has two requirements not imposed by the spellings: a donor in
the relevant period and the extension of its nucleus as well
as its palatal segment. The attested datives show inflection
but do not establish either requirement. The regular late
derivation gives the same nucleus and glide without an English
donor; its cost is different, namely the need to account
for ungeminated continental comparanda under that chronology.

Rejecting final desyllabification would eliminate the
analogy-free account proposed here, not the inherited
source of the mid vowel. The nonfinal contraction parallels
motivate a long direct vowel that could take a glide from
a protected alternant. The morphology gives no independent
reason to include the donor’s vowel in that operation: the
protected stem is reconstructible, but the datives do not
reveal its nucleus, and the ‘meal’ comparison shows why a
mass noun need not favour protected vocalism. Neither the
phonology nor the morphology establishes actual retention.
Together they make importing the protected vowel unnecessary
to account for the earliest Anglian forms.

## 5.2 Farman and West Saxon

Where Farman’s combination of ⟨oe⟩ and accent favours length,
unlengthened /hej/ is a weaker account of his form. Contraction
plus restoration and protected-stem transfer both explain
length and a palatal; quantity, even if independently confirmed
there, would not choose between them. The case for one or
the other would have to rest on direct and protected alternants
or a closer historical control. Farman does not supply a
date by which contraction or transfer must have happened
in Mercian generally.

Secure West Saxon ⟨hig/hyg⟩ is better explained by borrowing
protected vocalism under the early consonantal reconstruction.
Palatal conditioning of *ē* requires a preceding palatal, and
West Saxon treatment of /i.e/ does not establish raising of
/e.i/ [@HoggGrammar1992, §§5.49, 5.53, 5.82–84, 5.146–147;
@Hogg2006, p. 400]. If the high forms pass through the usual
umlauted diphthong stage, transferring the nucleus and glide
would suffice. The spellings do not distinguish that operation
from early substitution of the whole stem, as in Ringe and
Taylor’s analysis, or identify its date. The dialectal distinction
rests on vowel quality, not on treating unassigned Eadwine
⟨hieg⟩ as early West Saxon.

# 6. Implications for West Germanic chronology

Dahl takes the pre-\emph{*j} sequence to be
diphthongal, not consonantal *w*. His regular account of the
vocalism is not the early hiatus derivation and explicitly
requires restoration of final *ġ*. Ringe and Taylor allow a
similarly shaped \emph{*auj} output from reversible gemination.
Convergence of these outputs does not establish that the inputs
or intermediate stages were identical [@Dahl1938, pp. 81–86
and ‘hay’ discussion; @RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–53, 173].

In *bedd* ‘bed’, *nett* ‘net’ and *cynn* ‘kin’, the geminate
directly expresses the feature at issue: under early loss of
the ending it needs an explanation other than regular
gemination of the direct singular. The protected cells give
a ready explanation by stem transfer; the late order predicts
the geminate without that operation. Conversely, ungeminated
*mene* ‘necklace’ and continental *beti*-type forms support
early apocope insofar as they can be independently identified
as inherited direct forms, rather than as the results of
secondary phonological or morphological change
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–47; @HoggFulk2011, §2.23;
@Stiles2013, pp. 15–16; @Boutkan1995, pp. 435–436].

‘Hay’ is not a simple counterpart to any one of these words.
Resolution of the labial sequence makes the protected stem
recognizable by its vocalism, not by a surviving geminate.
The early direct history can produce the same mid vowel
independently, and the glide is a separable historical
question. Thus a direct form of ‘hay’ can be compatible
with survival even where a direct *bedd* cannot be regular
under the same order. That distinction weakens one objection
to early apocope. It is not positive proof of early apocope
in the word, since the spelling and the distribution of
protected cells are also compatible with late apocope.

# 7. Conclusion

Early apocope does not require the earliest Anglian forms
of ‘hay’ to borrow their vocalism. Regular development
supplies \emph{*he.i}; contraction could preserve its mid
quality in a long vowel even if the glide had to be restored.
Desyllabification would go further by retaining both
components, but the final environment is not independently
secured. The Kentish compound corroborates the relevance of
the early sequence if its first element is ‘hay’; it does
not establish that stronger history.

West Saxon high vocalism gives a positive reason to invoke
the protected stem under early apocope; Anglian mid
vocalism does not. Thus ‘hay’ is not a second ‘bed’: the
Anglian vowel can have an inherited source even if the
palatal requires analogy, whereas the early direct history
of ‘bed’ cannot supply its geminate. A secure direct form
with independent quantity evidence and a distinct protected
alternant would sharpen the Anglian comparison. Without
it, the difference in explanatory burden stands, but
neither an analogy-free history nor the general chronology
follows.
