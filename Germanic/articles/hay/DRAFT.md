# 1. Introduction

The Old English word for 'hay' survives in the manuscripts as *heg*,
*heig*, *hig*, *hyg*, in the Northumbrian gospel glosses also *hehg*
(corrected by the glossator to *heg*) and Mercian *hóeg*, in the metrical
Paris Psalter as dative *hege*, and, in late copies, *haig*; grammars
normalize it as West Saxon *hīeġ*, non-West-Saxon *hēġ*. These are
historical citation forms, not uniform manuscript spellings; the status
of the direct edition readings ⟨hieg⟩ is examined below. Behind these
spellings lies
a Proto-Germanic neuter ja-stem \*hawją (cf. Gothic *hawi*, Old High
German *hewi* beside *houwi*, Old Norse *hey*), a derivative of the verb
'to hew': hay is 'that which is cut'. The word is short, concrete, well
attested across Germanic, and etymologically transparent. It has also
been treated, implicitly or explicitly, as a witness to one of the
most contested orderings in early West Germanic phonology: the relative
chronology of the loss of final short low vowels and West Germanic
consonant gemination before \*j. This article asks whether the word can
bear that weight.

If the inherited endings \*-az and \*-ą were lost before gemination, as
Ringe and Taylor maintain [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–47], the
nominative–accusative singular \*hawją lost its ending in time to escape
gemination altogether, and an ungeminated direct-case form developed —
the form that surfaces in Old High German as *hewi*. If instead the nasal
ending survived gemination, as Dahl, Hogg and Fulk hold [@Dahl1938,
pp. 81–86; @HoggFulk2011, §2.23], the direct singular geminated like its
oblique cases, and West Saxon *hīeġ* is its regular outcome. The first
account must then explain the attested Old English forms by analogical
replacement from the protected cases; the second needs no analogy for
this word, but must explain away the ungeminated forms of the continental
languages.

The problem has usually been treated as settled in one direction or the
other by general arguments about the noun classes concerned. This article
re-examines it from the word itself: a source-critical collection of the
actual Old English attestations and their grammatical cells, including
the previously unlocated Northumbrian and Mercian gospel-gloss tokens
and the overtly inflected datives of the metrical Paris Psalter; an
assessment of what the spellings do and do not establish about vowel
quantity and syllabification; an explicit, stage-by-stage comparison of
the competing derivations, including Dahl's neglected proposal that the
English sequence never geminated at all, and the question whether any
independently motivated sequence of regular changes could carry the
ungeminated direct form to an attested Old English shape without
analogical interference; and an evaluation of the analogical account
against the comparative evidence. The aim is not to vindicate a
chronology but to determine what the evidence of this word can, and
cannot, decide. The answer defended below is largely negative, and, we
will argue, instructive: the direct spellings and Farman's accents do
not determine the quantity or history of the direct singular. In
particular, the traditional West Saxon ⟨ie⟩ argument depends on the
status of normalized forms, the dialect of the direct ⟨hieg⟩ readings,
and the conditions on the several documented sources of West Saxon ⟨ie⟩.

# 2. Previous scholarship

## 2.1 The chronological problem

West Germanic gemination doubled a consonant before \*j (\*satjan >
OE *settan*), but the geminating \*j was itself subject to earlier loss in
certain endings. In the masculine ja-stems the nominative singular ended
in \*-jaz and the accusative in \*-ją; in the neuters, nominative and
accusative alike ended in \*-ją. Whether these endings were still present
when gemination operated determines whether the direct singular of a
light-stemmed ja-stem noun geminated at all.

Hogg and Fulk set out the alternatives with exemplary clarity. The
expected development, with nominative \*-az lost early and accusative
\*-ą lost late, would give a pre-Old-English masculine paradigm with
ungeminated nominative \*sagi beside geminated accusative \*saggi; "it is
noteworthy, however, that **\*sege**, the expected reflex of \*sagi, never
occurs in OE, the nom.sg. always being of the type *secg* ... We must
therefore assume, with Dahl (1938: 81–6), that only \*-az was lost prior
to gemination and that acc.sg. *secg*, neut.sg. cynn are regular forms",
the masculine nominative having been re-formed by the syncretism of
nominative and accusative; "[a]lternatively, one might suggest, following
Campbell (1977: §576), see also Dal (1934), that both inflexions were
lost prior to gemination, in which event all geminated forms in the
nom.acc.sg. would be re-formations from the oblique forms or the plural"
[@HoggFulk2011, pp. 20–21, §2.23]. The two alternatives named in that
paragraph — Dahl's split chronology and Dal's early loss, as reported by
Hogg–Fulk — remain the
live options; Ringe and Taylor's ordering is a modern restatement of the
second [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–47], and Boutkan's of the first
[@Boutkan1995, pp. 209–213]. For the neuter 'hay' the difference is
stark: under the split chronology the direct singular kept its nasal
ending through gemination and *hīeġ* is regular; under early loss the
direct singular was \*hawi, ungeminated, and the attested forms require
explanation.

## 2.2 The early-apocope treatments: Ringe–Taylor, Campbell, Wright

Ringe and Taylor identify \*hawi explicitly as "the inherited nom.-acc.
sg.", comparing Old High German *hewi*, and note that the gemination of
\*wj "was reversible": Proto-Northwest-Germanic \*awj yielded
Proto-West-Germanic \*[aww'] but reverted to pre-OE \*[auj]
[@RingeTaylor2014, p. 53]. Their derivation of the attested word runs
from the protected stem: \*hawją > PWGmc \*hawi (direct) beside
\*[haww'-] (protected); the protected stem develops through \*hauj- to
pre-OE \*hēaj-, whence with i-umlaut West Saxon *hīeġ* and Anglian
*hēġ*; the inherited direct form, carried as far as \*hǣwi, is marked as
analogically replaced at that stage [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 173, 246].
What \*hǣwi would have become had it been left alone is nowhere stated.

Campbell draws the same distinction from the other end. His account of
the sound change gives "WGmc \*hauuj" with loss of u: the "final result
ēg or īeg" [@Campbell1959, §120.2]. But in the morphology he is equally
explicit that "\*hewi, \*gliwi without gemination would be the
undisturbed developments", the attested nominative–accusative singulars
of both words standing "under influence of the other cases", and *glīg*
being a "re-formation" [@Campbell1959, §579(4), §411]. The long vowel of
his "final result", it should be noticed, belongs to the geminate route;
it is not an independent observation about the direct case.

Wright's analogy is narrower. For him the vocalism of *hīeġ* is regular,
and only the final consonant is borrowed: intervocalic \*j was lost after
a long vowel or diphthong, and "[f]orms like nom. hieg ... had the final
g from the inflected forms, as gen. hieges, dat. hiege" [@Wright1914,
§270 Note; cf. §357]. Two things deserve emphasis. First, Wright's
*hīeges* and *hīege* are paradigm illustrations, not citations; Dahl
could still write of his early Old English corpus that "[i]nflected
forms are thus not to be found in my material" [@Dahl1938, hay
section], and his genitive remains unattested at any date, but the
metrical Paris Psalter supplies three overt dative singulars *hege*
(Section 3.2), so the inflected paradigm is now attested — in the
eleventh century. Second,
the three early-apocope authorities do not invoke analogy for the same
thing. Ringe–Taylor and Campbell replace the entire inherited stem,
vocalism and all; Wright borrows only a final consonant into a stem
whose vocalism he takes to be regular. Any evaluation of "the"
analogical account must keep these scopes apart.

## 2.3 The late-apocope treatments: Dahl, Hogg, Hogg–Fulk, Boutkan

Dahl's discussion remains the fullest treatment of the word. On the
general chronology he holds that gemination preceded the loss of \*-ą,
so that *cynn* < \*kunją is regular, citing the Weser runic *kunni*;
Dahl (1938) reports that he rejects Dal's contrary argument from Old
High German *hewi* and *gewi* [@Dahl1938, pp. 82–84]. The original Dal
1934 article is not directly consulted here. But for 'hay' itself Dahl
proposes something that cuts across the whole debate: that the English
sequence never geminated at all. His premise is syllabic: \*-auj- was
parsed \*-au̯.ja-, with \*u̯ the second element of a genuine diphthong
rather than a consonant, so that gemination — a process affecting
consonant plus \*j — had nothing to operate on; and he finds "no
recorded w-forms" of Old English nouns of this shape. The
differentiation of \*au (his \*ǣo; elsewhere written ēa) "had already
set in" when \*-ą was lost; the stranded \*-j became final and
vocalized to \*-i; that \*-i umlauted the diphthong (West Saxon īe,
non-West-Saxon ē) and was then regularly lost after a long diphthong.
His derivation of the direct singular accordingly runs \*hauja̯ >
\*hǣoi(ə) > \*hǣoi > \*hīe, \*hē, the attested *hī(e)g* and *heg*
having their final -g "reintroduced from the inflected forms" (gen.
\*haujas > \*hǣoiæs > *hī(e)ges*, in which the intervocalic glide is
regularly preserved and strengthened — on his account the oblique -g-
is not a geminate product either). He "strongly doubts" that \*hewi
ever existed in English, Gothic *hawi* and Old High German *hewi*
notwithstanding, and he applies the same parse across the class:
\*gauja- in the place-name element -gē/-gie, \*awjō 'island' > īeg/ēg,
\*gliuja- in the *glīg* ~ *glēo* doublet, the verbs *cīegan*, *cēgan*,
*strēgan* [@Dahl1938, hay section and Excursus]. On Dahl's view, then,
'hay' is not a gemination diagnostic at all — and even his
non-analogical vocalism imports the final consonant from the paradigm.
Two features of the account should be marked at once: every vocalic
step is an ordinary Old English sound law granted the diphthongal
parse, and the parse itself is the entire point at issue, since
Ringe and Taylor's "reversible gemination" reaches the same pre-Old-
English \*auj from the consonantal analysis (Section 5).

Hogg's grammar derives the word from the geminated stem: \*hawwj- >
\*hauwj- > \*hēawj- > \*hīewj- > West Saxon *hīġ*, non-West-Saxon *hēġ*,
with \*j lost after the high diphthong and \*w after the low one, while
conceding that "the phonology of the change is obscure"
[@HoggGrammar1992, §3.18]. His earlier study of analogy supplies the
typological frame: the masculine ja-stems preserve traces of a
pre-analogical paradigm with ungeminated nominative beside geminated
accusative, levelled in *both* directions — *hyll* generalizing the
geminate, *hyse*, *mete*, *ile* the plain nominative — with "the balance
... a very slender one" [@Hogg1979Analogy, pp. 68–73; cf. @Adamczyk2018
on *mete* ~ *mette*]. Mixed paradigms of exactly the required kind are
thus independently attested in Old English; what is not thereby settled
is whether 'hay' ever had one.

Boutkan likewise orders gemination before low-vowel apocope, attributing
apparent ungeminated relics to later remodelling [@Boutkan1995,
pp. 209–213, 435–436]. On the other side, Stiles adduces comparative
ungeminated relics — Old English *mene*, Old Saxon *(hals-)meni* against
Old High German *menni* — as evidence for early loss [@Stiles2013,
pp. 15–16], with the standing qualification that relics argue inheritance
only if their transmission is in fact inherited.

## 2.4 The spelling-based discussions: Jones and Minkova

A separate strand of scholarship approaches the word through its late
Old English and Middle English spellings. Jones reads the Lindisfarne
alternations ⟨heig⟩ ~ ⟨heg⟩ (beside ⟨gefraign-⟩ ~ ⟨gefregn⟩, ⟨ðeign⟩ ~
⟨ðegn⟩) as showing a vowel space modified before the voiced palatal
fricative — a diphthongization of the early Middle English Breaking type
[@Jones1989, p. 21]; in his later discussion the alternants appear as
[hej]/[heij], and the development is generalized as trigger effacement,
[hej] → [heij] → [hei] [@Jones1989, pp. 149, 152]. Jones cites no verse,
but the tokens can be identified: the Lindisfarne gloss writes *heig* at
John 6:10 and *heg* at Matthew 6:30, each paired with *gærs*/*gers* in a
double gloss over Latin *faenum*, with a third token, first written
*hehg* and corrected to *heg* by the glossator, at
Matthew 14:19 (Section 3.1; @Cook1894, s.v. *heg*). The alternation is
thus genuine but distributed across verses, not a variation within one
passage. His quantity
notation, however, is not consistent: the same word is transcribed with
a short vowel in the Breaking discussion but long — ⟨hēg⟩ [heej̆], ⟨hīg⟩
[hiij̆] — in his tabulation of the West Saxon forms [@Jones1989, p. 150,
Table 3.17], and his short-vowel classification of ⟨heg⟩ elsewhere
glosses the item 'meadow' [@Jones1989, p. 55, Table 2.29] — a loose
rendering rather than a different lexeme: the glossed Latin *faenum* and
Cook's lexicographical identification secure the identity of the
Lindisfarne forms with the noun 'hay' and exclude the suspected
conflation with *hege* 'hedge, enclosure' [cf. @Minkova2014, p. 64].
Minkova's contribution is of a different kind:
discussing the hypothesis of contrastive diphthongal length, she observes
that "the etymological length in [ej] as in OE *hēġ* 'hay', also spelled
⟨heig⟩, and (presumably) [ej] as in OE *weġ* 'way' ... should not have
been preserved as /eːj/ in hay and /ej/ in way" — the two words merge in
Middle English [@Minkova2014, p. 178]. She assumes the conventional long
vowel of *hēġ*; what her argument establishes is that the Middle English
and modern reflexes are incapable of recovering the Old English
quantity, since any contrast in this position was neutralized.

Ritt offers a different account of the Lindisfarne accents. He proposes
that Aldred preferentially marked long vowels where their length was not
otherwise predictable from the spelling, and argues that this helps
explain the distribution without treating every acute as a direct
transcription of quantity [@Ritt2000, pp. 498–501, 505–508]. His Matthew
census records 426 accents: 318 in graphically closed syllables and 108
in graphically open syllables. In his table on p. 508, his alternative
analysis accounts for 328 of 376 non-ambiguous occurrences (87.3%),
leaving 48 exceptions. These totals describe the observed distribution
and Ritt's interpretation of it; they do not establish Farman's practice.
The subsequent 100-item CVC sample on p. 509 is arithmetically
inconsistent: from 79 short and 21 long items, after 15 long items six
items remain, but the prose says eight. Its reported 98% predictive
accuracy is therefore not reproduced here.

## 2.5 What is at issue

The scholarship thus divides over two separable questions. The first is
chronological: was the direct singular of \*hawją still \*hawją when
gemination operated? The second is lexical: by what route did the
attested Old English forms acquire their vocalism and their final -ġ?
Every published account except the plain geminate route borrows the
final consonant, the vocalism, or both from the inflected forms — forms
attested for this noun only in the dative, and only in the eleventh
century (Section 3.2). The following
sections assemble what is actually attested, and then ask what each
proposed history predicts.

# 3. The Old English evidence

## 3.1 The witnesses

The full concordance, with loci, editions, verification status and
exclusions, is maintained in `CONCORDANCE.tsv`; the principal witnesses
are the following. In the ninth-century Mercian gloss of the Vespasian
Psalter the word appears as *heg* throughout — at least eleven tokens
(Pss. 36.2, 71.16, 91.8, 101.5, 101.12, 102.15, 103.14, 104.35, 105.20,
128.6, 146.8, with a further token in the Vespasian Hymns), always
glossing Latin *faenum*, always without an overt ending [@Dahl1938, hay
section]. Two Kentish charters supply early compound forms: *heiweg*
(S 1196, conventionally dated 859) and *Hegforde* (S 328, 858). The
first has been read as ‘hay-way’, but its first element remains
disputed. The noun ‘hay’ is independently established by the *faenum*
glosses; those glosses do not identify the first element of this
compound. The first element of *Hegforde* is not securely identified as
fodder ‘hay’ rather than an enclosure term or another lexical item
[@Lowe2001, pp. 76–78, 85–86;
@Langlands2019, pp. 45–46, 109, 126; @Wager2017, pp. 169–174]. S 1196
is the earliest dated ⟨hei⟩ spelling, in the surviving Christ Church
single sheet BL Cotton Augustus ii.16; Sweet's *Oldest English Texts*
Ct. 43 prints *cyniges hei weg* (p. 450).[^dahl] The charter's text is
conventionally dated 859, but its transmission and dating require
qualification.[^s1196] Bryan had already printed *hei < *hauja-* and
recognized both long- and short-vowel histories behind ⟨ei⟩: he
contrasts *heiweg* with short-vowel *dei* ‘day’, and records
*meihanda* beside *meghond* [@Bryan1915, pp. 8, 23, 25]. His
reconstruction supports the ‘hay’ identification before Dahl, but is
not independent evidence for vowel quantity.[^bryan] The ninth-century
Kentish charter corpus also writes *dæġ* as *dei* (Sweet's Ct. 39 of
831 and Ct. 42 of 837), while the same-scribe corpus includes *dei* in
S 1195; ⟨ei⟩ and ⟨eg⟩ therefore cannot be assumed to mark distinct
quantities. The S 1196 witness-name *Deimund* is not a secure
same-scribe control because the witness list may represent a separate
writing stage. Cooper knew of S 1196 and deliberately excluded it from
his chronology of highway terminology because he suspected the document
was corrupt [@Cooper2003, p. 61 nn. 151, 155]. He notes its anomalous
dating, the transition from Latin into Old English, the apparent
correspondence between *cyniges heiweg* and *cyninges strete*, and the
unusual *communis via*. These are independent documentary difficulties.
The claim that this is an unusually early occurrence of ‘highway’,
however, depends on first identifying *heiweg* as ‘highway’; it cannot
then independently prove that identification. Cooper's bounded list of
fourteen charters and the spellings, witness dates, transaction dates,
and contexts available in their Sawyer Old Texts are recorded in
`COOPER_CHARTER_CORPUS.tsv`. S 508 has *higi wegge* and S 727 *heige
weye* in the electronic texts; neither is an independent, securely
dated demonstration of the etymology of S 1196. The table records the
accessible transcripts rather than a collation of every manuscript or
printed edition. The ⟨ei⟩ of *heiweg* carries no independent
information about vowel quantity. The Old English
Bede has
accusative *heg* in *on sumera heg ne māweþ* [@Miller1890, I p. 28].
The late West Saxon Lambeth Psalter writes *hyg*, *hig* [@Dahl1938;
Lindelöf's edition]. In Gregory's *Dialogues* the eleventh-century
Corpus manuscript (C) writes *heig* and *hig* where Cotton Otho (O) has
*heg* and Hatton 76 (H) substitutes *gærs* 'grass' [@Hecht1900, pp.
36–37]. Ælfric's Colloquy has *mid hig*, dative syntax with no overt
ending. The glossaries give *Foenum, heg* (Cleopatra A.iii), *hig oððe
gærs* (Julius A.ii), and — in the "Semi-Saxon Vocabulary" copied into
Worcester Cathedral F.174 by the Tremulous Hand of Worcester
(s. xiii; Wright–Wülcker's "c. 1150" is superseded by the
identification of the hand) — *Fenum, hei, uel græs*
[@WrightWuelcker1884, I.289.45, I.325.21, I.547].
In the West Saxon gospels at Mark 6:39 Corpus 140 has *hig*, with later
*haig* (Hatton 38) and *heig* (Royal); the Lindisfarne gloss there has
*gers*, while Rushworth has *hegge* alongside *grese*. The Rushworth
gloss is Owun's, not Farman's, whose contribution ends at Mark 2:15
[@Skeat1871, pp. 48–49].

The only direct edition readings ⟨hieg⟩ located in this audit are
Eadwine Psalter glosses at PPs 71.16 and 146.8, printed by Harsley
[@Harsley1889, pp. 72, 241]. The witness is Trinity College Cambridge,
MS R.17.1, dated c. 1150; its folios have not been independently
collated here, and the dialect of these glosses is not established.
These readings must be distinguished from normalized *hīeġ* and from
reconstructed or illustrative forms. The other inspected West Saxon
traditions supply *hig/hyg* (including the Lambeth Psalter and CCCC 140)
and, in later gospel copies, *haig/heig*; they do not provide a
manuscript ⟨ie⟩ spelling of this noun.

Independent support for a ‘hay-way’ compound comes from Langlands's
topographical reading of S 445 *higweges*, though its transaction is
dated 939 and its surviving witness is a fifteenth-century copy. He
leaves S 963 *hig wege* disputed between ‘high-way’ and ‘hay-way’, and
S 765 *hegweige* ambiguous; their surviving manuscripts are respectively
eleventh- and fourteenth-century copies [@Langlands2019, pp. 45–46,
109, 126]. These examples make ‘hay-way’ a credible compound, not a
settled reading of S 1196. The enclosure vocabulary is a further live
possibility: S 296 has *be norðan hege* beside a river meadow, and
Wager discusses *hege*, *haga*, and *(ge)hæg* terminology, including
the Reculver forms *ealden hege* and *Gata gehægge* in S 546
[@Wager2017, pp. 169–174]. The first element of *Hegforde* cannot be
assigned to fodder ‘hay’ by context alone; deriving it from *hege*
would require an account of the absent final vowel, whereas a
consonant-final *(ge)hæg* stem would pose a different morphological
analysis.

The Northumbrian and Mercian gospel glosses supply six further tokens,
verified against the printed pages of the Cambridge synoptic editions.
Aldred's Lindisfarne gloss renders *faenum* three times with
the hay-word, in each case as one member of a double gloss with
*gers/gærs* 'grass': nominative *gers ɫ heg* at Matthew 6:30
[@KembleHardwick1858, p. 55; @Skeat1887, p. 59], accusative *ofer heg ɫ
gers* at Matthew 14:19, where the gloss first wrote *hehg* and altered
it to *heg* — Kemble–Hardwick print the original spelling
[@KembleHardwick1858, p. 117], Skeat the correction, with the
alteration recorded in his apparatus, "hehg, *alt. to* heg"
[@Skeat1887, p. 123] — and nominative *gærs ɫ heig micil*
at John 6:10 [@Skeat1878, p. 55]; at Mark 6:39 and Luke 12:28 he uses
*gers/gærs* alone [@Skeat1871; @Skeat1874, pp. 130–131]. Cook's
glossary registers the three tokens under a single strong neuter lemma
glossing *fœnum* [@Cook1894, s.v. *heg*], following the corrected
Matthew 14:19 reading and omitting the double glosses. In the Rushworth
Gospels, Farman's Mercian
gloss — independent of Lindisfarne in Matthew — writes *londes hóeg*
(nominative) at Matthew 6:30 and *on hóeg* (accusative) at Matthew
14:19, with an acute accent printed by all three editions made from the
manuscript [@KembleHardwick1858, pp. 55, 117; @Skeat1887, pp. 59, 123;
@Tamoto2013, pp. 19, 47]; Owun's Northumbrian gloss has
*heg ɫ gers micel* at John 6:10, reversing Aldred's order and respelling
his *heig* [@Skeat1878, p. 55]. Owun's gloss is textually very close to
Lindisfarne in John, though Kotake has shown that direct copying from
that manuscript is a premise rather than an established fact — the
non-Lindisfarne readings in his portions may reflect a common source or
intervening copies [@Kotake2016, pp. 377–396]; on either view his
⟨heg⟩ is a respelling within one textual tradition, not an independent
lexical witness. These verifications rest on the printed editions and
their page images. The Rushworth manuscript itself could not be
consulted for the accents: the Bodleian's digitization of Auct. D. 2. 19
covers a selection of folios that excludes both relevant pages.

The metrical Paris Psalter — the Old English verse psalms of BnF lat.
8824, an eleventh-century manuscript whose metrical portion is a
Saxonized copy of an Anglian-coloured original [@Fulk1992Meter,
pp. 410–414; @Toswell1990, ch. 1] — supplies six occurrences, of which
five are tokens. Three are overt dative singulars *hege*, all in
withering similes rendering *faenum*: *Ic eom hege gelic, þam þe hraðe
weornað, ðonne hit byð amawyn mannes folmum* (101.4), *and ic hege
gelic, swa hit hraðe weornað* (101.9), and *beoð mannes dagas mawenum
hege æghwær anlice* (102.14) [@Krapp1932PPs, pp. 72–73, 75]. All three
were verified in images of the manuscript (fols. 116r, 116v, 118v):
⟨hege⟩ is plainly legible in each, without accent, facing the Latin
*sicut foenum* verses. The dative reading is secured grammatically as
well as lexically: *gelīc* and *anlīce* govern the dative, 101.4
resumes the noun with dative *þam þe*, and 102.14 carries the agreeing
dative participle *māwenum*. Two further Psalter tokens are direct
forms, *heg* at 71.16 and *hig* at 105.17 [@Krapp1932PPs, pp. 31, 84];
a sixth occurrence, *hig* at 146.9, is not a token at all — the
manuscript reads *hio*, and *hig* is Krapp's emendation, as his
apparatus records [@Krapp1932PPs, p. 146]. Outside the Psalter, verse
offers one further token: accusative *hig* coordinated with *gærs* in
Andreas 38, *hie hig ond gærs / for meteleaste meðe gedrehte*
[@Krapp1932Vercelli, p. 4]. The six Psalter loci belong to a single
textual tradition and count as one witness.

Three classes of false positive must be excluded: the plural pronoun
*hig*; the distinct noun *hege* 'hedge, enclosure', whose Middle English
continuation is also written *hay* [@Minkova2014, p. 64]; and glosses
where *gærs*/*gers* translates *faenum* with a different lexeme. Parallel
copies of one passage — the three Dialogues manuscripts, the West Saxon
gospel manuscripts — are single textual traditions, not independent
witnesses.

## 3.2 The paradigm

Classified by grammatical cell (full table in `PARADIGM.md`), the record
has a definite shape. Direct-case tokens (nominative or accusative,
formally indistinguishable) are plentiful: every psalter-gloss, prose,
glossary and gospel-gloss token above, the two direct Paris Psalter
forms, and the Andreas accusative. The Colloquy's *mid hig* is dative
syntax with no ending — unsurprising, since an endingless dative beside
inflected variants is commonplace in late Old English. The dative with
overt *-e* is attested three times, exclusively in the Paris Psalter
tradition: *hege* at 101.4, 101.9 and 102.14. No genitive and no
unambiguous plural is attested anywhere; Wright's *hīeges* remains a
paradigm expectation. Dahl's observation that inflected forms are
absent holds for his early Old English corpus, not for the language as
a whole.

What the dative tokens establish must be stated with the same care as
what they do not. They show that eleventh-century Old English inflected
the word as a regular neuter with stem *heg-* throughout — the uniform
paradigm that every account on the table predicts for that date. They
do not show that the oblique stem was available as an analogical donor
before i-umlaut, which is when the early-apocope accounts need it
(Section 5): a text composed late and transmitted in one tradition
cannot date a pre-umlaut process. The two historically central
reconstructed entities therefore retain different standings. The
protected oblique stem is now attested in its late reflex but
undocumented at the date the analogy requires; the inherited
ungeminated direct form \*hawi/\*hewi remains wholly unattested in Old
English. Nor is the surviving record's distribution surprising: the
noun is a mass noun used overwhelmingly to render Latin *faenum* in
direct-case contexts, and the dative similes of the Psalter (*gelīc* +
dative) are exactly the construction in which an oblique of 'hay' would
be expected to surface.

## 3.3 Dialect and date distribution

The spellings do not sort into a single, homogeneous dialect history.
Anglian and early Kentish sources write ⟨heg⟩/⟨hei-⟩ (VP, VH, Bede,
charters); the tenth-century Northumbrian gospel glosses write ⟨heg⟩,
⟨heig⟩ (with the corrected first attempt ⟨hehg⟩) and Farman's Mercian
⟨hóeg⟩; the eleventh-century Paris Psalter writes ⟨heg(e)⟩ and ⟨hig⟩
side by side. Inspected West Saxon witnesses include ⟨hig/hyg⟩, with
⟨heig/haig⟩ in later gospel copies and Dialogues C. The two Eadwine
Psalter ⟨hieg⟩ readings are direct edition forms, but their dialect is
unassigned and the manuscript images were not checked.

Hogg distinguishes Alfredian and Ælfrician West Saxon dialect clusters
[@Hogg2006, pp. 399–402]; he also stresses the linguistic differences
between the Vespasian Psalter and Rushworth¹ (pp. 402–404), and cautions
against treating differences between Aldred and Owun as straightforward
geographical evidence for Northumbrian variation (pp. 405–406).

For West Saxon ⟨ie⟩ more generally, Hogg lists three possible sources:
palatalization of earlier /eː/, i-umlaut of earlier diphthongs, and
hiatus resolution involving /i/ and /e/ [@Hogg2006, p. 400]. The first
development is conditioned by a preceding palatal consonant (Hogg–Fulk
2011, §§5.49, 5.53); the second is treated in their §§5.82–84; the
third is discussed in §§5.146–147, where examples such as *sċe* and
*hie* accompany an explicitly uncertain analysis. Campbell §234 also
documents West Saxon contraction of /i+e/ and disyllabic forms in other
dialects [@Campbell1959, §234]. These are attested developments, not
evidence that any one applies to ‘hay’. Thus an ⟨ie⟩ reading, even if
securely assigned to a West Saxon witness, would not by itself identify
the source or prove an earlier diphthongal stem. The Anglian ē-vocalism
is likewise not a proxy for a homogeneous West Saxon history; the
Psalter's mixed ⟨heg(e)⟩ ~ ⟨hig⟩ fits its Saxonized-Anglian character
[@Fulk1992Meter, pp. 410–414]. The late
⟨ei⟩/⟨ai⟩ spellings belong to the Middle English diphthongal
developments discussed by Jones, and the Lindisfarne ⟨heig⟩ ~ ⟨heg⟩
variation is his earliest instance of them. The single independent-noun
⟨hei⟩ (Worcester F.174, s. xiii) not only postdates the Old English
quantity system but is demonstrably discontinuous with it: the
vocabulary abridges an eleventh-century glossary whose parallel entry
reads *Foenum, hig, oðde gærs* [@WrightWuelcker1884, no. X], and the
same thirteenth-century copy writes *Uia, wei* and *weibreode* for
certified-short *weġ* — its ⟨hei⟩ is an Early Middle English
⟨ei⟩-respelling of exemplar ⟨hig⟩, not a transmitted Old English
spelling. Farman's ⟨oe⟩ is a fact about vowel quality and spelling
convention, not length: Kuhn's study of his orthography shows the
unrounding of /ø(ː)/ complete in his dialect, with retained or inverted
rounded spellings of the ⟨oele⟩ type for *ele* — a word with umlaut of
*short* o [@Kuhn1945, p. 646] — while the \*au+i class at large is
written with plain ⟨e⟩ throughout his gloss. His ⟨oe⟩ attaches both to
etymological œ̄ (boéc, foérde, gemoéted) and, as an inverted spelling,
to ē of other origins, most pertinently the *hēran* < \*hauzjan class
(gehóerde, gehóerað) — the exact structural parallel of *hēġ* <
\*hauj-; a rounded vowel confined to 'hay' has nothing to recommend it.
Nothing in the distribution requires —
though nothing excludes — an ungeminated relic in the Anglian/Kentish
⟨heg⟩/⟨hei-⟩ forms (Section 5).

# 4. Orthography, vowel quantity, and syllabification

The conventional citation forms *hīeġ* and *hēġ* assert a long stressed
nucleus. It is worth asking, with some care, where that length actually
comes from, because the answer is: from reconstruction, supported at one
point — Farman's acute — by a direct but probabilistic Old English
observation.

The spellings themselves cannot decide. Old English ⟨g⟩ after a front
vowel normally spells /j/ [@HoggGrammar1992, §2.76], and no Old English
spelling distinguishes /e/ from /eː/ before /j/; ⟨heig⟩ is compatible
with /ej/, /eij/, /eːj/ and a falling diphthong, and Jones's own
transcriptions of the word span three of these possibilities
[@Jones1989, pp. 21, 55, 149–152]. Manuscript accents are the one place
where the record has improved. The acutes of the dictionaries —
Wright–Wülcker's *Hég*, Bosworth–Toller's *hég*, *híg* — are editorial,
and the Lindisfarne, Rushworth² and Paris Psalter tokens carry no
accent (the Psalter forms were checked in the manuscript images). But
Farman writes *hóeg* with an acute at both Matthew 6:30 and 14:19, and
the accent is printed by all three editions made independently from the
manuscript [@KembleHardwick1858, pp. 55, 117; @Skeat1887, pp. 59, 123;
@Tamoto2013, pp. 19, 47], which effectively excludes editorial
invention even though the manuscript pages themselves are not among the
folios the Bodleian has digitized, so the manuscript reading remains
unverified directly. What the acute means is a question
about Farman's practice, not about accents in general: Mokrowiecki's
calibration of two late Old English manuscripts found acutes marking
etymologically long vowels in roughly nine cases in ten, but
manuscript-specifically [@Mokrowiecki2015], and Jones reads the
Lindisfarne acute of ⟨wég⟩ 'way' as marking mere duration [@Jones1989,
p. 55]. The corrected accent census of Farman's Matthew–Mark stint
(Mt 1:1–Mk 2:15; `FARMAN_ACCENTS.tsv`) contains 196 non-target types
representing 299 tokens, plus target *hóeg* (one type, two tokens): 197
types and 301 Old English tokens total. The older 327-token total is
withdrawn. This is a census of accented tokens, not a denominator for
unaccented stressed vowels. The bounded matched calibration in
`FARMAN_ACCENT_CALIBRATION.tsv` counts both accented and unaccented
occurrences and separates tokens, surface types (acute ignored), exact
spelling types, and lexical types. Short-vowel-plus-glide controls
(*weg*, *dæg*) have 1/53 accented tokens, 1/9 surface types, and 1/2
lexical types (ten exact spellings). Their closed monosyllables have
1/26 accented tokens; forms with a following vowel graph have 0/27.
The short-*e* panel includes the unaccented spelling *weig* at Mark 1:3
(fol. 52v), where the gloss identifies ‘way’ in “prepare the way of the
Lord”; this is counted as a closed monosyllable. The script separately
excludes verbal *stigað/stigende* homographs from long-vowel noun *stīg*.
The only long-vowel-plus-glide control, *stīg*, has 1/2 tokens, 1/2
surface types, and 1/1 lexical type; both forms have following vowel
graphs, so neither is a closed-monosyllable match. The accented
*stígas* occurs at Mt 3:3, fol. 4v, where Latin *semitas eius* supports
an accusative plural; the other token, *stige* on fol. 52v, is an
alternative beside *gongas* and is not securely classified for case or
number. No likelihood ratio is inferred from this single lexeme.

Potential homorganic-cluster lengthening is kept separate. The short-HL
panel has 5/133 accented tokens, 5/23 surface types, and 4/5 lexical
types; by structure it has 3/52 closed monosyllables, 1/51 forms with a
following vowel graph, and 1/30 multisyllabic forms. It comprises 32
clear verbal *gangan* forms plus *lond*, *word*, *hond*, and two *cild*
tokens. Eight ambiguous *gang*-family forms and one *dægne* token are
reported separately and excluded. Treating the short-HL panel as short
gives 6/186 accented tokens, 6/32 surface types, and 5/7 lexical types;
treating it as lengthened gives 6/135 tokens, 6/25 surface types, and
5/6 lexical types. These small, selected controls are descriptive, not
a corpus-wide rate; repeated tokens are not independent. The full
accent census separately classifies 162 long-vowel types, 14 short-HL
types, 13 short types, and 7 uncertain types.

Two further observations sharpen but do not settle the inference for
this word. First, no short-vowel control is written with ⟨oe⟩, whereas
*hóeg* combines the acute with the digraph Farman otherwise applies to
his ē/œ̄ class (Section 3.3). Second, both *hóeg* tokens are accented,
but two tokens from one scribe are not independent lexical evidence.
Farman's acute is probabilistic evidence about his own late tenth-century
practice, not a precise rate or a ninth-century quantity fact. Ritt's
completed analysis of Aldred's Lindisfarne accents is relevant
methodologically but does not establish Farman's practice
[@Ritt2000, pp. 498–510].

The Middle English and modern reflexes are, perhaps surprisingly,
uninformative, and Minkova has explained why: the expected /eːj/ of
*hēġ* 'hay' and /ej/ of *weġ* 'way' "should not have been preserved" as
distinct — any length contrast before /j/ was neutralized, and the two
words duly merge [@Minkova2014, p. 178]. Modern *hay* therefore cannot
testify to Old English quantity. Verse, which cycle-1 collation had
not reached, turns out to supply tokens but no test. The monosyllabic
direct forms (*heg*, *hig*) stand in lifts, where a closed monosyllable
is metrically heavy whether its vowel is long or short; the disyllabic
datives could in principle engage resolution, but they occur only in
the metrical Paris Psalter — and there the two simile verses (*ic eom
hege gelic*, 101.4; *and ic hege gelic*, 101.9) scan as type B whether
*hege* is a resolved short lift or a long lift with *-ge* in the dip —
whose verse Fulk has shown to be too late,
too formulaic and too irregular to sustain quantitative inference —
its metrical deviations are products of transmission and incompetence
rather than phonology [@Fulk1992Meter, pp. 410–414; cf. @Toswell1990,
ch. 6] — and resolution is in any case the metrical equivalence
Minkova singles out as least directly grounded in the ambient language
[@Minkova2003, pp. 23–25]. The metre is not silent; it is
demonstrably unusable here. And syllabification is equally
underdetermined: nothing in the record distinguishes /hej/ from
disyllabic /he.i/ for the early non-West-Saxon spellings.

What remains is reconstruction, but its evidential premise must be
explicit. The normalized West Saxon *hīeġ* is not itself a manuscript
reading; the direct-edition ⟨hieg⟩ forms are in the Eadwine Psalter,
whose dialect has not been established here. If those forms instantiate
West Saxon ⟨ie⟩, the three documented historical sources of that
diphthong still require separate evaluation before they can constrain
the stem. The quantity of the direct singular is therefore not
established by a securely localized West Saxon spelling. Farman's
calibrated acute is a distinct, defeasible observation about his own
practice and does not decide among the historical accounts.

# 5. The competing derivations

Five derivations of the attested direct singular deserve explicit
statement. They are laid out stage by stage in the research files
(`DERIVATIONS.md`, `DAHL_REASSESSMENT.md`); here we give their logical
structure and where each succeeds or fails. Throughout, the two frozen
CAPR models serve as explicitness checks on the handbook derivations —
a model output is a prediction of the formalized account, not
independent evidence about history.

**A0: early apocope, no further assumptions.** If \*-ą was lost before
gemination [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–47], the direct singular \*hawją >
\*hawi developed ungeminated: \*hawi > \*hæwi > (i-umlaut) \*hewi —
Campbell's "undisturbed" development [@Campbell1959, §579(4)] — and,
with regular loss of \*w before unstressed \*i, something like \*hei.
The outcome is a short-vowelled form with no final consonant, spelled
⟨hei⟩ or ⟨heg⟩ at best. Two points about this output must be kept
apart. First, it would be **orthographically invisible**: ninth-century
Kentish ⟨ei⟩ ~ ⟨eg⟩ interchange over short /e/ + /j/ (*dei* ~ *deg* for
*dæġ*, Section 3.1), and no Old English spelling marks quantity, so the
Anglian/Kentish ⟨heg⟩/⟨hei-⟩ tokens can neither confirm nor exclude an
ungeminated relic — the earliest ⟨hei⟩, in compound-internal
*heiweg* of S 1196 (conventionally dated 859), is
literally the string A0 predicts, but every control shows the same
digraph over certified short V+j, and the one later independent-noun
⟨hei⟩ (F.174, s. xiii) is demonstrably a respelling of exemplar ⟨hig⟩
(Section 3.3), i.e. continuous with the levelled/geminated form, not
with A0. Second, for the traditional normalized West Saxon *hīeġ*:
the derivation cannot produce ⟨ie⟩. That inference is conditional,
however: the direct ⟨hieg⟩ readings identified here are in the Eadwine
Psalter, whose dialect is unassigned, while the inspected West Saxon
manuscripts write ⟨hig/hyg⟩. The absence of ⟨ie⟩ from those witnesses
does not establish a West Saxon e-vocalism, and the normalization is
not a manuscript control. A0's failure as an account of a specifically
West Saxon ⟨ie⟩ form therefore depends on a dialectal and textual
identification not yet secured; its status behind Anglian/Kentish forms
remains undetermined.

**A1: early apocope plus independently motivated later changes.** Two
candidates were examined. Hogg's hiatus resolution [@HoggGrammar1992,
§§5.129–134] applies to post-w-loss \*he.i: the stressed vowel in open
final position counts long, and resolution of long vowel plus front
vowel is loss of the unstressed vowel, yielding *hē* — the right
process, independently supported, but the wrong output: non-West-Saxon
vocalism only, and the final segment destroyed rather than explained.
Resyllabification of /e.i/ to /ej/ is phonetically trivial and brings
A0's output into line with the non-West-Saxon ⟨heg⟩/⟨heig⟩ spellings,
but no independent Old English change then supplies either length or
West Saxon *īe*. The negative finding is worth stating precisely: **no
independently supported chain of regular changes carries the
early-apocope consonantal-w direct form to the normalized West Saxon
form — neither to its vocalism nor to its final consonant — if that
normalization represents the language of the relevant witness.** A
lengthening rule invented for this word alone would be ad hoc and is
rejected.

**A2: early apocope plus paradigmatic levelling.** This is Ringe and
Taylor's actual account: the inherited direct form, carried as far as
\*hǣwi, was replaced by the protected stem \*hēaj- before i-umlaut,
whence West Saxon *hīeġ*, Anglian *hēġ* [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 173,
246]. After the replacement the derivation is identical with the
geminate route; the accounts differ only in history, not in any Old
English prediction. The levelling is typologically unobjectionable —
Hogg's study of the masculine ja-stems shows mixed paradigms levelled
in both directions [@Hogg1979Analogy, pp. 68–73], and Old High German
*houwi* beside *hewi* may show the very same act on the continent — but
it is an extra, word-specific assumption, and its donor paradigm is
attested in Old English only late: the Paris Psalter datives of
Section 3.2 supply an inflected stem in the eleventh century, but
nothing in the record dates the levelling itself to the pre-umlaut
window the account requires. The continental cognates strengthen the
typological case considerably: Middle Dutch shows **hoye** < \*hauj-
(the oblique stem) beside **houwe** < \*hawi (the old nominative) as
doublet reflexes of the very same paradigm split [@Boutkan1995,
pp. 212–213], so 'hay' demonstrably levelled — in both directions —
elsewhere in West Germanic. If the normalized West Saxon *hīeġ*
reflects an actual West Saxon ⟨ie⟩ development, it would require an
appropriate historical source. The available word-attestations do not
establish that the source was specifically a protected stem, nor that
levelling occurred; both remain inferences requiring adjudication.

**B2: late apocope.** If \*-ą survived gemination [@Dahl1938,
pp. 81–86; @HoggFulk2011, §2.23; @Boutkan1995, pp. 209–213], the direct
singular geminated with its obliques: \*hawwją > (resolution of the
geminate glide) \*hauj > \*ēaj-type diphthong > i-umlaut > the
normalized West Saxon *hīeġ*, Anglian smoothed *hēġ*. Every step is
regular within the analysis; no analogy is needed for this word in any
cell. Its
cost is carried elsewhere: the ungeminated direct forms of Gothic and
(on one reading) Old High German must be explained, and the chronology
must survive the relic evidence (Section 6).

**Dahl: no gemination.** Dahl's derivation (Section 2.3) shares B2's
output and A2's chronological innocence. Its vocalism is regular granted
the diphthongal parse of \*-awj-; its final consonant is analogical by
his own statement. The decisive observation, for our purposes, is a
convergence: Ringe and Taylor themselves note that the West Germanic
gemination of \*wj was "reversible" — \*awj > [aww'] > [auj]
[@RingeTaylor2014, p. 53] — so that the geminate route, the reversed-
geminate route and Dahl's never-geminated route all pass through the
same pre-Old-English \*auj. **From that stage onward the accounts
converge, but the currently identified ⟨hieg⟩ readings are not securely
West Saxon and the normalized form is not an independent manuscript
datum.** Hogg's several sources for West Saxon ⟨ie⟩ mean that even a
securely West Saxon reading would require an additional
environment-specific argument before it established an ēa-type stem
in this word.

# 6. Analogy and the comparative evidence

Since every account except plain B2 invokes analogy somewhere — Ringe–
Taylor for the whole stem, Wright and Dahl for the final consonant — the
comparative standing of the analogical operations matters.

The donor side is secure in type and now attested in token, though only
late (the Paris Psalter datives, Section 3.2). Mixed
ja-stem paradigms with geminate and non-geminate alternants existed and
levelled in both directions: *hyll* generalized the geminate, *hyse*,
*mete*, *ile* the plain form, with "the balance ... a very slender one"
[@Hogg1979Analogy, pp. 68–73; cf. @Adamczyk2018 on *mete* ~ *mette*].
Old High German *houwi* beside *hewi* is routinely read as the geminate
stem levelled into the direct cell — but that direction of levelling is
itself a consequence of the early-apocope chronology that creates the
ungeminated cell in the first place; on the opposite chronology *houwi*
is regular and *hewi* the innovation, and Dahl's Excursus duly derives
the Old High German forms without gemination [@Dahl1938, pp. 107–109].
The continental doublet therefore cannot fix the direction of levelling
independently; what Gothic *hawi* and Old Norse *hey* secure is an
ungeminated direct cell for Proto-Germanic, not a consonantal \*hewi
stage for pre-Old-English — precisely the stage Dahl denies, with his
observation, which has survived both research cycles' collations, that
no w-form of any Old English \*-awj- noun is on record.

Within Old English the glide class itself is internally
non-discriminating. The 'island' word and the verbs *cīegan*, *strēgan*
reach their attested shapes on every account; the *glīg* ~ *glēo*
doublet, examined closely (`COMPARATIVE_CONTROLS.md`), is consistent
both with Campbell's verdict of re-formation [@Campbell1959, §411,
§579(4)] and with Dahl's regular double development, and its chief
contribution is indirect: the West-Saxon-only distribution of the
g-forms, always ⟨i⟩ before ⟨g⟩, confirms that final -g restoration from
oblique cases is a real and repeated process in exactly this class —
which lowers the cost of the one analogical step that every surviving
account of 'hay' except B2 requires. The only glide-class word that
leans one way is 'hue': attested *hīew/hīw* with no †*hīeg* is what the
geminate or levelled routes predict, and Dahl must explain the missing
g-form by homonymic clash with 'hay' — the one plainly ad hoc move in
his chapter.

The words that genuinely discriminate the chronologies lie outside the
glide class: the light neuter C+j nouns, whose attested geminate direct
cells (*nett*, *bedd*, *cynn*) are regular only under late apocope and
require levelling under early apocope, against the ungeminated relics
*mene*, Old Saxon *(hals-)meni* beside Old High German *menni*, which
favour early apocope if their transmission is inherited [@Stiles2013,
pp. 15–16; @Boutkan1995, pp. 435–436]. 'Hay' adds nothing to either pan
of that balance.

# 7. Implications for the West Germanic chronology

The result of Sections 4–6 is narrower than earlier versions of this
argument claimed. The corpus establishes the noun and its inflected
dative, but no securely localized manuscript ⟨ie⟩ form that by itself
requires an ēa-type direct stem. The Eadwine Psalter's ⟨hieg⟩ readings
are direct edition forms of c. 1150, but their dialect and manuscript
readings remain unverified. If a West Saxon *hīeġ* analysis is adopted,
gemination, pre-umlaut levelling, and Dahl's diphthongal parse remain
possible routes; the surviving spellings do not choose among them. The
final -ġ is regular under the geminate analysis and analogical under
others, while the inherited consonantal-w form remains unattested in
Old English. The Paris Psalter datives establish a late inflected stem,
not the earlier chronology of any levelling.

This negative has a positive use. 'Hay' has figured in the literature
as part of the \*-awj- evidence for one chronology or another — Dal's
early-loss argument rested in part on Old High German *hewi* and *gewi*
as reported by Dahl [@Dahl1938, pp. 82–84], and the handbooks' geminate
derivations of *hīeġ* implicitly enlist the word for the split
chronology. Both enlistments overdraw the evidence. The chronological
question must be decided where the classes actually diverge — the light
C+j neuters against the *mene*-type relics — and the \*-awj- nouns
should be struck from both sides' witness lists. Within the frozen CAPR
models the same moral holds in formal dress: the two adopted
chronologies differ for this word only in the direct cell of Model A,
whose mismatch is a visible, deliberately uncertified residue, and the
corpus-wide scores (387/396 against 388/396) share their inputs and
cannot arbitrate history.

# 8. Conclusion (provisional)

The Old English word for 'hay' is abundantly attested in the direct
case — including three verified Lindisfarne tokens (one first written
⟨hehg⟩ and corrected by the glossator), two accented Mercian tokens in
Rushworth¹, and one dependent token in Rushworth² — and attested with an
overt inflection only in the dative, in the three manuscript-verified
*hege* of the metrical Paris Psalter. Its conventional long vowel is a
reconstruction on which all viable derivations agree, supported but not
proved by Farman's acute, which calibration shows to be probabilistic
evidence about his own practice. The direct ⟨hieg⟩ edition readings in
the Eadwine Psalter are late and not securely assigned to West Saxon;
the normalized *hīeġ* therefore cannot be treated as a manuscript fact.
Hogg's three possible sources of West Saxon ⟨ie⟩ are real developments,
but their applicability to this word remains to be demonstrated. The
Paris Psalter forms supply an overtly inflected stem centuries too late
to date the levelling proposed by the early-apocope account.

The source record does not yet locate the early-apocope direct form
among the proposed dialectal outcomes. The Kentish ⟨hei-⟩ is
compound-internal and its lexical identification remains disputed; the
thirteenth-century independent-noun ⟨hei⟩ in F.174 is a later
respelling of ⟨hig⟩. The direct Eadwine Psalter ⟨hieg⟩ readings are
potentially relevant to the West Saxon argument, but the witness's
dialect and manuscript readings require verification. The next
adjudication should assess the applicability of Hogg's ⟨ie⟩
developments, the regular development of the inherited direct stem, and
the necessity, timing, and scope of levelling. The source-preparation
cycle has supplied the bounded evidence for that decision without
settling it.
