---
title: "Old English ‘hay’ and the scope of paradigmatic levelling"
abstract: |
  Whether West Germanic gemination preceded nasal apocope determines
  whether light neuter ja-stems geminated in the direct singular.
  Potentially inherited continental singleton forms of ‘bed’ favour
  considering early loss, but ‘hay’ appears to require an additional
  analogy on that order. Late loss gives a protected-type stem
  regularly; under early loss, Ringe and Taylor (2014) obtain it by
  substituting the protected stem before i-umlaut, accounting for
  both Anglian and West Saxon. Without substitution, regular
  development reaches \emph{*he.i} at hiatus. The ninth-century
  Kentish compound *heiweg*, absent from their principal discussions
  of ‘hay’, is preferably read as ‘hay-road’. Its first member matches
  that prediction in writing, not necessarily in pronunciation or
  ancestry. Phonological parallels motivate desyllabification, which
  would preserve both inherited components, or contraction with
  secondary palatal supply, which would preserve only the source of
  the vowel. Neither continuation is established for ‘hay’. West
  Saxon high vocalism favours a protected source under early loss,
  but the earliest Anglian evidence does not require one. The
  possibility of entirely regular survival remains open, and the
  apparent additional objection to early apocope is diminished.

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The chronological test

<!-- P-orders -->

Did West Germanic gemination precede or follow the loss of final
nasal \emph{*-ą}? Ringe and Taylor place both that loss and the
loss of the oral vowel in endings such as masculine \emph{*-az}
before gemination. Hogg and Fulk retain \emph{*-ą} through
gemination, though the oral vowel has already been lost
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–53, 173; @HoggFulk2011, pp. 20–21 §2.23].
Boutkan instead places gemination before both losses
[@Boutkan1995, pp. 209–213]. The comparison here concerns the
nasal ending.

<!-- P-tradeoff -->

This difference changes the relation between the stems of light
neuter ja-nouns. If the nominative–accusative singular lost its
final vowel early, its \emph{*j} ceased to be prevocalic and the
stem escaped gemination. Forms such as the genitive and dative
retained a following vowel. Continental *peti*, *beti* and
*bedi* ‘bed’ give positive comparative grounds for early loss
if they continue the inherited ungeminated direct stem.
On that chronology, Old English direct *bedd* can be explained
by extension from the independently attested geminated stem
of *bedde*, *beddes* and *beddum*. Those forms make the transfer
possible; they do not establish that it took place. The late
order gives direct *bedd* regularly but needs a separate
explanation for the continental singleton forms. Boutkan’s
account makes the latter secondary rather than inherited
without gemination (Section 6)
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–47; @HoggFulk2011, §2.23;
@Boutkan1995, pp. 211–213, 435–436; @Stiles2013, pp. 15–16;
@Krapp1932PPs, Ps 40:3; *Beowulf*, lines 1240, 1791].

<!-- P-hay-predictions -->

‘Hay’ makes that distinction especially useful because the two
orders differ in the source of the vowel and the final palatal
rather than in a surviving geminate. Under early loss, the
inherited direct singular \emph{*hawją} develops through
\emph{*hawi}, \emph{*hæwi} and \emph{*hewi} to \emph{*hei},
initially with hiatus. Under late loss the direct singular
develops like the protected cells, yielding the long nucleus
and glide conventionally represented by West Saxon *hīeġ*.
Ringe and Taylor adopt early loss but reach the protected-type
outcome by replacing direct \emph{*hæwi} with the stem
\emph{*hēaj-} before i-umlaut. From this substituted stem they
derive both the high West Saxon and the mid Anglian reflexes
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 173, 246].
If that substitution is necessary, ‘hay’ adds a historical
requirement under early apocope that the late order does
not have. The question is whether that apparent disadvantage
can be removed or reduced.

<!-- P-motivation -->

Those treatments do not discuss the ninth-century Kentish
*heiweg* [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 173, 246; @Sweet1885, p. 450,
Ct. 43]. On the preferred interpretation ‘hay-road’, the
first element has exactly the letters ⟨hei⟩, corresponding
to the sequence predicted if the direct stem is allowed to
develop without substitution under their ordering. The
compound does not establish how the sequence was pronounced
or which cell supplied it. The early Anglian simplex
spellings ⟨heg⟩ and ⟨heig⟩ give a separate test: the noun
occurs in direct syntax, and the mid vowel can be compared
with the outcome of the unremodelled stem
[@Stevenson1843VP; @Skeat1878, p. 55].

<!-- P-thesis -->

The first question, then, is whether the inherited direct
singular could have survived by regular sound change in early
Anglian. If it did not, a second question is whether the vowel
could still be inherited: contraction could preserve its source
even if a palatal had to be supplied from another form. The
high West Saxon vowels favour the protected stem under early
loss, but the Anglian mid vowel need not have the same source.
Either Anglian history would diminish the apparent disadvantage
of early apocope without deciding the chronology.

## 1.2 The two regular derivations

<!-- P-trace-diverge -->

Table 1 starts both derivations from the consonantal
\emph{*wj} of \emph{*hawją}. The first difference is whether
\emph{*j} is still followed by a vowel when the labial could
geminate. Early loss of \emph{*-ą} strands singleton \emph{*j}
after the labial of a light stem. It vocalizes to \emph{*i},
leaving a single intervocalic \emph{*w}. If \emph{*-ą}
instead remains through gemination, prevocalic \emph{*j}
triggers gemination before the ending is lost. Vocalization
of a stranded singleton cannot then apply: the geminated
labial supplies the second member of a diphthong and
\emph{*j} remains consonantal. The ordering thus determines
both the material available for the nucleus and the segment
that conditions umlaut.

|:-------------------------|:----------------------|:-----------------------|
| Inherited direct singular | \emph{*hawją} | \emph{*hawją} |
| Gemination before prevocalic \emph{*j}, if the ending remains | Not yet applied | \emph{*hawją} > \emph{*hawwją} |
| Nasal apocope | \emph{*hawją} > \emph{*hawj} | \emph{*hawwją} > \emph{*hawwj} |
| Vocalization of a stranded singleton glide | \emph{*hawj} > \emph{*hawi} | Blocked by the geminate |
| Gemination in the early order | No prevocalic \emph{*j} | Already applied |
| Resolution of the geminated labial sequence | No geminate | \emph{*hawwj} > \emph{*hauj} |
| Diphthong fronting and development | No diphthong | \emph{*hauj} > \emph{*aeuj} > \emph{*ēaj} |
| Fronting of short \emph{*a} | \emph{*hawi} > \emph{*hæwi} | No short \emph{*a} |

<!-- P-trace-end -->

In the early order, fronting and i-umlaut give \emph{*hewi};
subsequent loss of \emph{*w} before unstressed \emph{*i}
leaves \emph{*hei}, with short stressed /e/ followed by
syllabic /i/. This is a regular stage feeding any later
resolution of hiatus, not a claim that two syllables
persisted into written Old English. In the late order the
diphthong develops to \emph{*ēa} before being mutated by
consonantal \emph{*j}. In the conventional West Saxon
representation \emph{*hīeġ}, the macron marks a long
nucleus and the dot marks /j/. It is not a transcription
of the regular outcome in every dialect.[^trace-notation]

These are regular predictions of the two orderings with the
stated treatment of the labials, not complete analyses attributed
to either grammar. Campbell and Hogg retain the labial glide
longer in their treatments of the geminated sequence. Ringe and
Taylor derive \emph{*auj} from a reversible gemination rather than
beginning with a diphthong. Dahl’s analysis starts with a
diphthongal representation and gives a separate account of final
*ġ* (Section 6) [@Campbell1959, pp. 45–46 §120.2;
@HoggGrammar1992, §3.18; @RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 173;
@Dahl1938, pp. 81–86 and Excursus].

## 1.3 Ringe and Taylor’s protected-stem analysis

<!-- P-substitution -->

Even under early loss, the protected cells have a following
vowel at gemination. In Ringe and Taylor’s analysis, their
stem develops along the line \emph{*[hawʲwʲ-]} >
\emph{*hauj-} > \emph{*hēaj-}. In Old High German they
identify *hewi* as the inherited direct singular and
*houwi* as an extension of the geminated stem. For English
they set out the substitution in their treatment of
umlaut [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 173, 246]:

**Substitution before i-umlaut:** direct \emph{*hæwi} is replaced by
protected \emph{*hēaj-}, as endingless \emph{*hēaj}.

This replacement abandons the continuation of \emph{*hæwi}
through \emph{*hewi} to hiatus: both the short stem vowel
and the final syllabic \emph{*i} are replaced. After the
substitution, i-umlaut conditioned by \emph{*j} and regular
dialectal development give West Saxon *hīeġ* and Mercian
and Northumbrian *hēġ*. Ringe and Taylor do not identify
the English donor cell or continue the unremodelled English
line beyond \emph{*hæwi} in these passages. Their account
shows how the protected stem can yield the attested dialectal
contrast; it does not show that the contrast requires
replacement in Anglian. To determine what the direct stem
could have supplied, the recorded forms must be examined
for what they establish about the vowel and the palatal.

[^trace-notation]: Initial *h* is conventional throughout. Both derivations front the initial fricative from [x] to [ç] before umlaut. The notation \emph{*aeu} is schematic for a fronted diphthong stage, not three syllabic vowels.

# 2. The Old English attestations

## 2.1 *Cyniges heiweg*

<!-- P-char-reading -->

The sequence ⟨hei⟩ occurs in *heiweg* in the bounds of the
Kentish charter S 1196. Ringe and Taylor do not discuss the
compound in their principal treatments of ‘hay’ (Section 1.1).
On the surviving mid-ninth-century
sheet (BL Cotton Augustus II 16), Sweet reads *cyniges hei
weg*, followed by *stret to scufelingforde*; the bounds mention
the burg-dwellers’ meadows and later *an cyninges strete*
[@Sweet1885, p. 450, Ct. 43].[^s1196] The reading and word
division are Sweet’s, not the result of a fresh collation.
If the first element is ‘hay’, the compound gives an early
written counterpart to the \emph{*hei} reached by the direct
stem under early apocope.

<!-- P-char-positive -->

The meadows give a reason independent of the derivation to take
the first element as ‘hay’: a road serving meadow land could
be named for the crop. Langlands’s comparison with S 445 makes
that function concrete: *higweges* occurs beside a meadow, and
a much later map calls a route there Mower’s Lane
[@Langlands2019, pp. 45–46, 109, 126]. S 445 survives in a
fifteenth-century copy, and neither its bounds nor the map
identifies the function of the S 1196 road.

The spelling also fits ‘hay’. Sweet lists the first element
under that word; Bryan gives the historical comparison with
Germanic \emph{*hauja-} and adduces Kentish *dei* ‘day’
[@Bryan1915, pp. 8, 23, 25].[^bryan] *Dei* establishes
⟨ei⟩ as a spelling for a vowel followed by a palatal glide,
not for disyllabic /e.i/; the two letters do not mark the
quantity distinction Bryan reconstructs. Dahl adopts ‘hay-road’
and includes it alongside *Hegforde*, without adding an
independent argument from the bounds [@Dahl1938, ‘hay’
discussion].[^dahl] Other potentially relevant *hig* or *heg*
road names are later, survive in later copies, or are disputed.
Setting and spelling thus support ‘hay-road’ together, but
a road by meadows could also be the king’s highway.

<!-- P-char-rival -->

If the first element is the adjective ‘high’, the compound
has no bearing on the development of ‘hay’. Bosworth–Toller
lists it under *hēah-weg*, with *hēah-strǣt* as a semantic
parallel; Clark Hall prints ‘highway?’, and Cooper adopts
that interpretation [@BosworthToller1898, s.v. *hēah-weg*;
@ClarkHall1960, p. 152; @Cooper2003, p. 61 nn. 151, 155].
If *cyniges hei weg* and *cyninges strete* refer to the same
route, the latter expression supports the identification
of a significant road. It does not show that the first
expression named it for its status rather than its use.

<!-- P-char-phon -->

The semantic parallel leaves a difference in form unexplained.
For ‘hay’, the early-apocope derivation gives the sequence
\emph{*he.i}, which could develop a palatal glide; a protected
stem already supplies a source for a palatal (see below).
Kentish *dei* illustrates how a vowel and glide could be
written ⟨ei⟩. The ‘highway’ interpretation must additionally
explain what became of the velar fricative of *hēah-* before
the *w* of *weg* and how that development yielded a form
written ⟨hei-⟩.
Neither dictionary supplies such a local correspondence, and
the charter comparison found no securely comparable
ninth-century Kentish example. This does not make the required
development impossible, but it does give ‘hay’ the better
phonological comparison. The spelling alone does not determine
the vowel’s quality or quantity.[^char-minor]

[^char-minor]: An enclosure term is semantically possible but likewise has no demonstrated local ⟨hei-⟩ reflex [@Wager2017, pp. 169–174].

<!-- P-char-document -->

Cooper excludes S 1196 from his survey of highway terminology
[@Cooper2003, p. 61 nn. 151, 155]. The uncertain date of
the transaction, the abrupt transition from Latin to English,
and the position of the *communis via* clause are problems
of date and transmission, whichever etymology is adopted.
The Latin phrase is not a gloss on *heiweg*; nor does it
settle how the two mentions of a king’s road are related.
A different problem arises only if *heiweg* means ‘highway’:
it would be an isolated ninth-century instance of that term
in the material under comparison. On the ‘hay-road’ reading
there is no such early attestation to explain. That removes
the chronological difficulty for ‘highway’ terminology, not
the irregularities of the document, and is not an independent
reason to prefer ‘hay-road’.

<!-- P-char-verdict -->

‘Hay-road’ is preferable because it accounts both for the
setting and for the written form. The meadows and the S 445
comparison give a reason for naming the road after hay; the
Kentish spelling convention and the predicted sequence
\emph{*he.i} make ⟨hei⟩ intelligible for that word.
‘Highway’ can account for the road’s significance, but still
lacks a local parallel for the required reflex of *hēah-*.
The edition therefore gives good reason to recognize ⟨hei⟩ in
a ninth-century form of ‘hay’. Establishing the more likely
lexeme, however, does not identify which historical stem
supplied the first element.

<!-- P-char-compound -->

The hay-house compounds show that a stem shaped like the
direct singular could be used in composition. Glossarial
*highus* and *heghus* render ‘hay store’; their first elements
match simplex *hig* and *heg*, with no visible connector.
Other ja-stems show the same pattern, but *bedtīd*, for
example, already has the geminate of simplex *bedd*.[^compound-forms]
Agreement between the compound and the direct form thus tells
us which stem shape was available for composition, not whether
that shape antedates levelling. An old compound could preserve
an earlier direct-shaped stem after the simplex was remodelled;
a later formation could use the stem then current.

Nor need an inherited compound have had a direct-shaped first
member. Campbell’s examples of connecting vowels, including
*rīcedōm*, show why the word-final development cannot simply be
assumed in composition [@Campbell1959, §359]. If a vowel
followed \emph{*j}, it could preserve the environment for
gemination. That history would require evidence of the connector,
the age of the compound and the subsequent loss of the vowel.
Neither *heiweg* nor the hay-house forms preserves such a connector.

A protected stem could also give ⟨hei⟩. Kentish *ċēgan* ‘call’
independently illustrates a mid-vowel outcome of
i-umlaut of \emph{*ēa}. For a stem of that type with a final palatal,
*dei* supplies a plausible spelling convention
[@RingeTaylor2014, p. 246; @Bryan1915, pp. 23, 25]. No
independently long local control establishes ⟨ei⟩ for /eːj/,
but neither is the spelling known to be restricted to a
short vowel. A secure contrast between a Kentish compound
and its contemporary simplex, or evidence for a connector,
could distinguish histories that the letters alone leave open.

[^compound-forms]: *Highus* glosses *faenile*, *heghus* glosses *fenile* [@WrightWuelcker1884, I, cols. 186.13, 237.36]. For direct-shaped *hrycgmearh* and *cynlīc*, see Campbell [-@Campbell1959, §§348–349 n. 3]; for further connecting vowels (*endeleas*, *lǣcecræft*) and their absence or later loss in other compounds, see §359.

<!-- P-char-close -->

On the preferred reading of S 1196, the regular early-apocope
sequence has an exact written counterpart in a probable
ninth-century form of ‘hay’. In the sources examined, no
other early example of exact ⟨hei⟩ was found: ⟨heig⟩
is a different spelling.[^early-hei] The thirteenth-century
Worcester glossary F.174 has *Fenum, hei, uel græs* beside
*hig* in the older Julius parallel; Worcester also writes
*wei* ‘way’ [@WrightWuelcker1884, I, cols. 325.21, 547].
Worcester’s ⟨hei⟩ therefore need not preserve hiatus or
continue the Kentish form. Neither Worcester nor the later Middle
English variants supplies another Old English attestation of ⟨hei⟩
[@MiddleEnglishDictionaryHei, s.v. *hei*].

The early correspondence calls for explanation, but it does
not establish short quantity, retained hiatus or descent
from the inherited direct singular. A compound can preserve
a stem shape without revealing its grammatical source.
To investigate the survival of the inherited direct singular
more directly, we turn to simplex attestations in which the
grammatical function of the noun can be established.

[^early-hei]: The comparison covered the *híg/hég* and road-name entries, the principal glossary loci, and the Kentish road comparanda [@BosworthToller1898, s.v. *hēah-weg*; @Toller1921, s.v. *híg*; @WrightWuelcker1884, I, cols. 289.45, 325.21, 402.36, 547; @Cooper2003, p. 61].

## 2.2 Simplex forms

<!-- P-simplex-anchor -->

What form did ‘hay’ have when used as a singular subject or direct
object in its own right? The earliest secure direct objects occur
in the ninth-century Mercian gloss of the Vespasian Psalter. It has
eleven instances of endingless *heg* in the Psalms and one in the
canticles. Four Psalm occurrences are secure direct objects
(103:14, 104:35, 105:20, 146:8). In *forð gelædes heg neatum*
‘you bring forth hay for cattle’ (103:14), for example, *heg* is
the thing brought forth [@Stevenson1843VP; @Kuhn1965VP]. The
Old English Bede gives independent accusative evidence: in
*on sumera heg ne māweþ* ‘does not mow hay in summer’, *heg*
is the object of ‘mow’. This is the reading of Miller’s composite
edition, not a reading independently checked across the surviving
tenth-century copies [@Miller1890, I, p. 28].

The late tenth-century Gospel glosses add evidence from both
functions. At Mt 6:30 Aldred’s *gers ɫ heg* and Farman’s
*londes hóeg* belong to the object of ‘clothe’. At Jn 6:10
Aldred’s *gærs ɫ heig micil* is the subject of the existential
clause; Owun has *heg ɫ gers micel* in the corresponding
gloss [@Skeat1887, p. 59; @Tamoto2013, p. 19; @Skeat1878, p. 55].
These syntactic contexts identify the direct function, not the
ancestry of the stem or the sounds represented by its letters.
Other contexts do not permit an equally secure case assignment.[^simplex-case]
The dates are those of the surviving English writing: the Latin
manuscripts may be older than their glosses, while a copy may
postdate the English translation or gloss it transmits. The
date of copying does not by itself establish the age of the
exemplar.

[^simplex-case]: Neither Aldred’s *ofer heg* nor Farman’s *on hóeg* at Mt 14:19 has distinctive case marking [@Skeat1887, p. 123; @Tamoto2013, p. 47]. A positional reading of *gesittan on hóeg* favours the dative; taking the hay as the goal of sitting down permits an accusative. Position does not require the dative after *ofer*: CCCC 140, Mk 6:39, has accusative agreement in *sæte ofer þæt grene hig* despite the seated posture [@Skeat1871, pp. 48–49]. In Vespasian Hymn 7:4, snow falls *ofer heg* in a comparison with rain falling *ofer græs*: motion favours the accusative, but the English does not determine it. Nor does *swa swa* assign case: in withering similes such as *spe spe heg eorðan* (Ps 71:16), an elliptical comparison with the subject is plausible, but there is no separate subject agreement with the noun [@Stevenson1843VP; @Kuhn1965VP]. The corresponding Latin cases do not resolve these English ambiguities.

<!-- P-simplex-distribution -->

The early Anglian witnesses give mid-vowel spellings: Vespasian
*heg* and the Northumbrian *heg* and *heig* of Aldred and Owun.
Farman’s *hóeg* belongs to his own Mercian Matthew gloss; its
acute is evidence to be assessed for that witness. The ordinary
unmarked spellings do not independently establish quantity.
The Lindisfarne *hehg* and corrected *heg* at Mt 14:19 are
known from the printed
editions and apparatus, not from inspection of the manuscript
alteration, and are readings of a single occurrence.[^simplex-editions]
Owun’s *heg* is separately written, but his John gloss and
Aldred’s transmit closely related material; copying directly
from the surviving Lindisfarne manuscript is not established
[@Kotake2016, pp. 377–396].

The West Saxon evidence instead includes high-vowel spellings:
*hig* in the West Saxon Gospels (Mk 6:39) and *hyg* in the
Lambeth gloss (e.g. Ps 101:5) [@Skeat1871, pp. 48–49;
@Lindeloef1909, p. 158]. These spellings pose a different
problem of vowel quality, not a contrast in measured length.

the Psalter glosses by locus.

Later witnesses do not sort into two uniform groups: the
metrical Paris Psalter has both mid-
and high-vowel spellings, and manuscripts of the *Dialogues*
vary in spelling and sometimes in the word used. Neither the
provenance of a manuscript nor the origin of its scribe by itself
assigns a dialect to the transmitted reading. Nor does recurrence
in related copies or glosses count as independent evidence of the
same development: the Junius Psalter gloss, for instance, is
textually related to Vespasian’s.

The printed pages were checked; usable manuscript images of the target Gospel loci were not examined. <!-- P-simplex-eadwine -->

The conventional West Saxon citation form *hīeġ* is not a
transcription of the observed high-vowel spellings ⟨hig⟩ and
⟨hyg⟩. Its ⟨ie⟩, macron and dotted *ġ* should not be taken as
evidence that an early West Saxon manuscript records that
spelling, quantity and consonant value. The Eadwine Psalter
does record ⟨hieg⟩ twice, at Pss 71:16 and 146:8 in the
*Romanum* column. Both readings are visible in the manuscript
and printed by Harsley [@Harsley1889, pp. 124, 241].[^simplex-eadwine]
What is uncertain is not the reading but its place in the
transmission. The gloss in this twelfth-century Canterbury
manuscript was largely copied from older material and
selectively modernized [@Faulkner2017]. Neither token can be
assigned securely to an early West Saxon textual layer or
taken as a record of local twelfth-century Kentish pronunciation.

The early Anglian mid-vowel spellings and the West Saxon
high-vowel spellings thus pose distinct historical problems.
Neither the manuscript spellings nor their normalization
settles how the inherited direct stem developed. Under early
apocope the next question is what regular developments were
available to the direct stem, particularly once loss of
\emph{*w} brought its vowels into hiatus.

# 3. Historical phonology

## 3.1 Development to hiatus

<!-- P-loss -->

Can the inherited direct singular supply the vowel and final
segment suggested by the Anglian spellings? On the consonantal
analysis, early apocope gives the following regular development:

\emph{*hawją} > \emph{*hawi} > \emph{*hæwi} > \emph{*hewi} > \emph{*he.i}.

Loss of \emph{*-ą} leaves \emph{*j} syllabic. The resulting
\emph{*wi} permits Anglo-Frisian fronting and then i-umlaut,
as in the reconstructed histories of ‘ewe’ and ‘itch’
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 148, 237; @Campbell1959, §579(4)].
Noninitial \emph{*w} was regularly lost before fully unstressed
high front \emph{*i}, including long \emph{*ī}. The contrast
between West Saxon *gierwan* ‘prepare’ and *giereþ* ‘prepares’
supports syllabic \emph{*i} as the trigger: earlier syncope
had removed it before \emph{*j} in the infinitive, but it
remained in the finite form. In the preform of *gierede*
‘prepared’, loss of \emph{*w} lightened the first syllable
and thereby prevented general syncope. This fixes the loss
before that syncope [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 258, 260;
@Campbell1959, §§237.1b, 406].

Ringe and Taylor derive *glī* ‘merrymaking’ from \emph{*gliwi}
and *sǣ* ‘sea’ from \emph{*sāwi}. These final sequences raise
a second question, since the attested forms have lost both
the consonant and the separate syllable. By contrast,
\emph{*pylwī} > *pyle* ‘pillow’ supports the consonant loss
after another consonant, where it did not bring two vowels
together.[^loss-controls] Ringe and Taylor place the loss
after i-umlaut: on their analysis, the mid vowel of *eowu*
‘ewe’ presupposes umlaut, but its transfer to the ō-stems
then removed the environment for loss. They acknowledge
*cleweþa* ‘itch’ as exceptional and suggest analogy with
*clāwan* ‘scratch’ or competition between suffixes with
different vowels [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 259–261]. The
application to ‘hay’ requires no special consonant loss:
given \emph{*hewi}, it yields \emph{*he.i}. What became of
the hiatus is a separate question.

[^loss-controls]: Compare also \emph{*āwi} > *ǣ* ‘law’ and \emph{*niwi-} > *nī-* in compounds such as *nī-cenned* ‘newborn’. Anglian *ēde* ‘flock of sheep’ preserves evidence of the loss, whereas Ringe and Taylor explain West Saxon *eowede* by remodelling on *eowu*. The weak verbs often restore *w* by levelling; independent evidence for its loss includes *gierede* ‘prepared’, Northumbrian *strēdun* ‘they strewed’ and Vespasian *gecēd* ‘called’ [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 258–260; @Campbell1959, pp. 100, 166–167, 327–328]. The early glossary spellings *streidæ/streide* are less secure: they may belong to *stregdan*, as discussed below.

## 3.2 Contraction and glide formation

<!-- P-hiatus-test -->

Can ordinary developments of final \emph{*e.i} account for
Anglian ⟨heg⟩ and ⟨heig⟩? If the hiatus survived, the
written forms would have to represent two syllables.
If final \emph{*i} lost syllabicity, both inherited
components could survive in one syllable. If the vowels
contracted, the result could retain the inherited source
of the nucleus, but any final glide would have to come
from elsewhere. Ringe and Taylor’s substitution has a
different starting point: it supplies both components from
the protected stem without passing through inherited
\emph{*he.i}.

The spelling gives a reason to take the glide seriously.
Old English commonly writes /j/ with ⟨g⟩
[@HoggGrammar1992, §2.76]. In *deg/dei* ‘day’ and Farman’s
*weig* ‘way’, the final glide has an independently
reconstructible consonantal source [@Bryan1915, pp. 23, 25;
@Tamoto2013, Mk 1:3]. This makes a vowel followed by /j/
an ordinary reading of the Anglian spellings of ‘hay’;
it does not tell us whether that glide came from a
syllabic vowel.

<!-- P-hiatus-retain -->

Hiatus need not have disappeared as soon as it arose.
The early glossaries write ⟨streidæ⟩ (Erfurt 899) and
⟨streide⟩ (Corpus 1910), without the length marks often
supplied in discussion. If these are forms of ‘strew’,
their two vowel letters are consistent with nonfinal hiatus
after \emph{*w}-loss. The scansion of *stred* at *Beowulf*
2436 independently calls for two syllables in that verse,
though Hogg allows a pronunciation favoured by slow speech
rather than a surviving archaism [@Pheifer1974, §59.2 n. 1
and no. 899; @Sweet1885, Corpus no. 1910; @Campbell1959,
§237.1b; @HoggGrammar1992, §5.133]. Within the Vespasian
tradition Hogg discusses ⟨hie⟩ and ⟨sie⟩ and takes the
analogical formation ⟨siem⟩ at Ps 26:4 as particularly strong
evidence for disyllabicity [@Sweet1885, Vespasian Ps 26:4].
That comparison involves the
reverse vowel order, /iː.e/, rather than /e.i/
[@HoggGrammar1992, §5.146]. Together these examples
allow persistence in some environments without dating the
loss of final /e.i/ in ‘hay’.

If ⟨heg⟩ represents retained hiatus, there should be some
independent reason to take ⟨g⟩ as a syllabic vowel in that
environment. Lindisfarne *heista* and *neista* can reflect
hiatus after loss of \emph{*h}, but the one apparent
parallel with ⟨ig⟩, *heigsta*, is nonfinal and Brunner
regards it as probably a scribal error.[^hiatus-spelling]
The early Mercian, Northumbrian and Kentish spellings
examined here do not establish ⟨eg⟩ or ⟨ig⟩ for final
/e.i/. Persistence is possible; it does not by itself
account for the letters in the Anglian simplex.

[^hiatus-spelling]: Pheifer and Hogg allow that the glossary *streide* may instead be a weak preterite of *stregdan* [@Pheifer1974, §59.2 n. 1; @HoggGrammar1992, §5.133 n. 2]. For Lindisfarne *heista/neista* beside *hesta/nesta* and Brunner’s isolated *hēiȝsta*, see Brunner [-@SieversBrunner1965, p. 112 §129 n. 5]. Campbell treats *streide* and Kentish *gecēide* alongside spellings associated with palatal vocalization: ⟨ei⟩ does not identify the direction of change. Vespasian *hefie* beside forms in ⟨-ige⟩ is evidence for vocalization, not for formation of a consonant from an inherited syllabic vowel [@Campbell1959, §§237.1b, 266–267].

<!-- P-hiatus-glide -->

The second vowel could instead have become a glide. Hogg
reconstructs \emph{*kla.wu} > \emph{*kla.u} > \emph{*klau}
in the history of Old English *clēa* ‘claw’: after loss
of \emph{*w}, the formerly unstressed vowel joined the
preceding nucleus rather than disappearing. The plurals
*cnēo* ‘knees’ and *trēo* ‘trees’ provide related comparisons
in his account [@HoggGrammar1992, §3.19]. Those outcomes
continue established back-vowel diphthong types. The proposed
front-vowel development need not extend the diphthong
inventory: /ej/ consists of a stressed mid vowel and a
consonantal palatal glide, a structure Old English already
had in words such as *weġ* ‘way’.[^hiatus-controls]

The comparison motivates \emph{*he.i} > \emph{*hej}:
fully unstressed final /i/ after stressed short /e/
could lose syllabicity after \emph{*w}-loss, provided
contraction had not already eliminated the sequence.
The structural precedent and the ordinary reading of ⟨heg⟩
give this proposal independent motivation and an attested
target, respectively. Neither settles whether it operated
with these front vowels in final position, whether it
preceded contraction, or in which varieties it occurred.

[^hiatus-controls]: Campbell’s loanword examples concern gliding before another vowel; his metrical synizesis examples need not reflect ordinary lexical syllabification [@Campbell1959, §§512, 553–557]. Vespasian ⟨siem⟩ supports persistence, not the proposed desyllabification: its second vowel is not the high vowel posited for ‘hay’.

Contraction is a genuine competitor to glide formation.

and contraction yield the same result. Vespasian *gecēd*
is especially instructive, since it places contraction

*strēdun* does not depend on identifying the early glossary

identical and unlike vowels may have contracted at

contraction of final \emph{*e.i} in ‘hay’

If final contraction took place before \emph{*i} could
become a glide, \emph{*he.i} > \emph{*hē} would preserve
the inherited source of the nucleus but eliminate the
inherited source of the palatal. To explain /heːj/ on
this account requires both contraction in the relevant
environment and a historically available source that
actually supplied /j/ without replacing the vowel. A
protected alternant could supply it; no such transfer is
directly established. Campbell’s re-formation of *glīg*
invokes a wider pattern of alternations, not a transfer
from a particular inflected cell of ‘glee’. Brunner’s
alternative retains an inherited palatal. The comparison
motivates investigating how the two components could
have acquired different histories, not assuming that
they did [@Campbell1959, §§120.2, 411;
@SieversBrunner1965, §130 n. 3]. Sections 4.2–4.3 take
up the scope and direction of levelling and the ‘glee’
comparison in more detail.

<!-- P-hiatus-result -->

The early-apocope derivation supplies an inherited mid
vowel at hiatus. Its subsequent fate is less certain.
Desyllabification before contraction could preserve both
inherited components as short /ej/. Contraction with
secondary restoration could preserve the source of the
nucleus in long /eːj/ while giving the consonant a
different history. Protected-stem substitution also
yields Anglian /eːj/, but requires the actual replacement
of the direct stem, not merely the existence of a
protected alternant. Failure to show an inherited source
for the glide would not establish that the vowel was
replaced. Conversely, retaining more inherited material
is no substitute for evidence that final contraction and
secondary supply of the glide both took place.

These accounts differ in what would rule them out. An
independent demonstration of length in a particular early
witness would exclude unlengthened /ej/ as a direct
explanation of that witness. Can the spellings and
metrical evidence establish such a distinction?

## 3.3 Orthography and vowel quantity

<!-- P-quantity-test -->

An independently long vowel in an early Anglian witness would
rule out unlengthened /hej/ there, but would not distinguish
contraction with glide restoration from protected-stem vocalism.
Conversely, independent evidence for short /ej/ would count
against both long-vowel histories as formulated. The macron in
conventional *hēġ* is not such evidence: insofar as the length
comes from deriving the form from the protected stem (Section 1.3),
using the macron to establish that derivation would be circular.

Vespasian ⟨heg⟩ and Northumbrian ⟨heg⟩ and ⟨heig⟩ accommodate
a mid vowel plus /j/ without distinguishing /e/ from /eː/.
The added ⟨i⟩ in *heig* does not independently indicate an
extra mora or syllable. Jones uses both short and long
transcriptions in discussing ‘hay’; these are interpretations,
not manuscript quantity marks. His short-vowel entry ⟨heg⟩
glossed ‘meadow’ is not securely identifiable as ‘hay’
[@Jones1989, pp. 21, 55, 149–152]. Kentish *dei* ‘day’ shows
that ⟨ei⟩ is compatible with a short vowel and a glide. The
lack of a matched long-vowel spelling does not establish that
*hei-* was short (Section 2.1). Thus the ordinary spellings
do not establish either quantity.

Even a scribe who used the acute for length need not have
marked every long vowel. Ritt proposes that in Aldred’s
Lindisfarne gloss the acute preferentially marked length
where it was not otherwise predictable from the spelling.
On that account an unmarked vowel can still be long
[@Ritt2000, pp. 505–510]. This explains why the absence of
a mark cannot be interpreted apart from the scribe’s practice.
Farman’s marks must be assessed in his own gloss.

<!-- P-quantity-farman -->

At Mt 6:30 and 14:19, all three editions print Farman’s
*hóeg* with an acute [@KembleHardwick1858, pp. 55, 117;
@Skeat1887, pp. 59, 123; @Tamoto2013, pp. 19, 47]. This is
agreement among editions: neither folio was available in the
digitized selection consulted. If Farman seldom used the acute
on short vowels in comparable forms, its recurrence in ‘hay’
would favour length.

Neither short-vowel control has ⟨oe⟩. Kuhn’s *oele* ‘oil’,
however, shows that the digraph could represent a short rounded
vowel. Farman also writes it in forms of *hēran* ‘hear’, such
as *gehóerde*, where the long vowel belongs to the class
derived by umlaut of earlier *ēa* from Germanic *au*

or identifying the source of the vowel. Digraph and accent
belong to the same spellings and are not independent trials.

Taken together, Farman’s spellings favour length in his own
gloss. This is positive local evidence against unlengthened
/hej/, not proof of length even there, and it cannot be projected
back onto Vespasian or across Anglian. Nor does it tell us
whether Farman’s nucleus was inherited through contraction
or supplied by a protected stem. It therefore does not decide
the order of apocope and gemination.

<!-- P-quantity-quality -->

The West Saxon evidence raises a question of quality rather than
quantity. Whereas the Anglian spellings point to a mid vowel,
West Saxon *hig* and *hyg* point to high vocalism. Neither
desyllabification nor contraction with a secondary glide raises
the inherited mid vowel in the histories proposed in Section 3.2.
Unlike the conventional macron in *hēġ*, the contrast between
the written vowels is positive evidence that these histories
do not account for the West Saxon forms as they stand.

The sources of West Saxon ⟨ie⟩ are not equally relevant here.
Palatal diphthongization of earlier /eː/ requires a preceding
palatal consonant; a following /j/ does not meet that condition.
The resolution of /i.e/ likewise gives no precedent for raising
in the reverse sequence /e.i/. Umlaut of an earlier diphthong,
by contrast, has an independently motivated input in the
protected stem, though not in either direct mid-vowel history
[@Hogg2006, p. 400; @HoggGrammar1992, §5.163 and §§5.49, 5.53, 5.82–84, 5.146–147].
Under early apocope, protected vocalism therefore has a better
historical basis for West Saxon than a raising process for
which there is no comparable evidence. The two Eadwine spellings
do not localize an early ⟨ie⟩ stage (Section 2.2), and *hig* and
*hyg* do not establish length, prove a particular intermediate
diphthong or identify the operation by which the vowel entered
the direct singular. The case for protected vocalism in West
Saxon does not show that the Anglian vowel was borrowed too.

<!-- P-quantity-close -->

Metre does not settle the Anglian quantity. If the direct forms
are monosyllables closed by /j/, the coda makes the syllable heavy
even with a short vowel. A heavy lift therefore does not require
a long nucleus. An inflected form might give a better test if
the intervocalic consonant begins the second syllable, leaving
the first open. Consider Paris Psalter Ps 101:4 *ic eom hege
gelic* ‘I am like hay’ and Ps 101:9 *and ic hege gelic* ‘and I
[am] like hay’ [@Krapp1932PPs, pp. 72–73]. In both verses, if the
initial words are unstressed and *-lic* takes the second lift,
the pattern is type B: dip, lift, dip, lift. With a single /j/
as onset of the second syllable (/he.je/), short *he-* and
unstressed *-ge* can together fill the first lift by resolution;
only the prefix *ge-* of *gelic* occupies the medial dip. With
long /heː.je/, *hē-* alone fills the first lift, and *-ge* and
the prefix fill a two-syllable dip. The short parse depends on
the open first syllable; it would not be a case of resolution
if ⟨g⟩ closed that syllable. On the stated assumptions, neither
verse requires one vowel quantity rather than the other.

That ambiguity is distinct from the limits of the witness.
Fulk finds irregularities in the distribution of unstressed
syllables and in alliteration in the metrical Psalms, and argues
that a reviser Saxonized an Anglian text, sometimes retaining
forms needed for the metre. This makes fine quantitative
inferences from a transmitted spelling especially insecure
[@Fulk1992Meter, pp. 410–414; @Toswell1990, ch. 6]. Middle
English offers no independent check either: once short and
long vowels before /j/ merged, ‘hay’ and ‘way’ could agree
even if their Old English vowels differed in length. The
agreement does not show that they were already alike
[@Minkova2014, p. 178].

The earliest Anglian spellings thus leave both short and long
mid vowels open, and neither metre nor the later merger decides
between them. Desyllabification remains a motivated account of
a short inherited nucleus. Farman’s spellings favour length in
his own gloss, not in Vespasian or Anglian generally. Contraction
can account for an inherited long mid vowel if a secondary
palatal was supplied; transfer from a protected stem can
account for the same Anglian outcome and has positive support
from the high West Saxon vowel under early apocope. Since the
available quantity evidence cannot distinguish the two
long-vowel histories, the next question is what independent
grounds the noun’s morphology provides for transferring a
stem or supplying the palatal alone.

# 4. Historical morphology

## 4.1 Competing directions of levelling

<!-- P-morph-question -->

On Ringe and Taylor’s early-apocope account, the protected stem
\emph{*hēaj-} replaced direct singular \emph{*hæwi} before
i-umlaut (Section 1.3). The substitution supplied both a
different vowel and a palatal consonant
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 173, 246]. There are independent reasons
to consider such a transfer. The protected stem belonged to
several cells, and a noun need not be used often in the plural
for its genitive or dative singular to furnish a model.
Uniform stems in other *ja*-paradigms also offered a model for
levelling. In Hogg’s analysis of masculine *hyll* ‘hill’, the
geminate stem spread to the nominative singular, with the
uniform neuter *cynn* ‘race’ as a possible model. Yet in *hyse*
‘warrior’ he infers extension of the nominative form to the
accusative, while the plural retained its geminate
[@Hogg1979Analogy, pp. 68–73]. That is not the alternation
reconstructed for ‘hay’, but it shows why pressure for uniformity
does not, by itself, pick a donor.

<!-- P-morph-yellow -->

Ball and Stiles find more specific grounds for the direction
of levelling in ‘yellow’. Early glossaries write the direct
singular *gelu* (also *gelo*); the inflected stem shows *geolw-*,
as in *geolwes*. In their reconstruction, inflected forms,
including the weak adjective, had \emph{*lw}, which broke short
/e/ to *eo*. Direct \emph{*gelu} escaped because it lacked the
cluster. In Anglian, later back-mutation could give *geolu*
from the unbroken direct form without transfer. In West Saxon,
by contrast, they expect palatal diphthongization to yield
\emph{*gielu}, on the model of *giefu* ‘gift’. They explain
West Saxon *geolu* instead by extension of the broken vowel
from the protected stem, probably before i-mutation
[@BallStiles1983, pp. 15–18, §§4.2–4.3]. The expected West
Saxon alternation and its levelling are inferences, not
observed stages. What motivates their direction is that the
inflected stem provides a regular source for the vowel that
the direct West Saxon history does not predict.

<!-- P-morph-meal -->

‘Meal, flour’ presents the opposite possibility. The written
record has both direct *melu* and *meolu* and genitive *melwes*
and *meolwes*. On Ball and Stiles’s account, direct
\emph{*melu} lacked the \emph{*lw} cluster that caused breaking
in the protected stem, \emph{*melw- > *meolw-}. They propose
that after breaking, but before back-mutation, the frequent
direct singular of this uncountable noun supplied its
monophthong to the inflected forms, creating new
\emph{*melw-} variants alongside the broken stem. When regular
back-mutation then produced *meolu*, the monophthongal
inflected variants in turn helped maintain direct *melu*.
Thus each set of forms could support the other at a different
stage. Luick and Sievers–Brunner instead explain direct *melu*
from inflected forms that escaped back-mutation. The spelling
distribution alone does not choose between these explanations:
Ball and Stiles’s initial outward transfer depends on their
reconstruction of breaking in the inflected forms before
back-mutation [@BallStiles1983, p. 18, §4.3;
@SieversBrunner1965, §110.1].

They apply the same reasoning to ‘fat’. In addition to
inflected *smer(u)w-* in the medical texts, they cite the
early Épinal–Érfurt dative *smeruui* (gloss 944), with
monophthongal vocalism already in an inflected form. On their
account this is evidence for outward transfer before
back-mutation. ‘Tar’, however, has early *teru/tero* but
no inflected *terw-* in their material. The reported genitive
plural *teorwena* may point to uses other than as a mass noun;
the surviving distribution could also be an accident of
transmission [@BallStiles1983, pp. 18–19, §4.3 and n. 18].
Meaning alone does not determine the direction of levelling.

If direct singular ‘hay’ was likewise frequent relative to
its inflected forms, retaining its vocalism or extending it
to other cells is historically motivated, not merely the
absence of protected-to-direct transfer. But Ball and Stiles
infer relative frequency; they do not measure it. Their
*wa*-stems had a different conditioning environment and
attested inflected variants with contrasting vowels. For the
*ja*-stem ‘hay’, the recorded datives do not give that test.
The comparison supplies a reason to consider the other
direction, not evidence that ‘hay’ took it.

## 4.3 The scope of levelling and the comparison with ‘glee’

<!-- P-morph-scope -->

Survival of the inherited direct stem need not entail its
extension. If its vowel did spread to the inflected stem,
the dative could still retain *-e*. Conversely, an endingless
dative such as *mid hig* could arise by loss of the ending
without borrowing a direct stem. Paris *hege* therefore
attests inflection, but written ⟨e⟩ does not distinguish an
inherited direct mid vowel from a mid-vowel reflex of the
protected stem. Neither dative establishes the donor or the
direction of levelling.

Transfer into the direct singular can itself have different
scopes. Ringe and Taylor’s pre-umlaut substitution brings
the protected vowel and palatal in together. If the inherited
direct vowel first contracted, by contrast, only the palatal
would need to be supplied. Such an account needs a source of
/j/ accessible after contraction in the variety concerned,
and a reason for the consonant to spread without the vowel.
Reconstructing a protected stem with /j/ is not sufficient.
Wright proposes that a secondary glide formed between vowels
in inflected forms and then spread to the direct singular in
‘hay’ and ‘island’. This is a model for a limited transfer,
not independent evidence that it happened in ‘hay’ or that
a glide formed regularly from final /e.i/
[@Wright1914, §270 and note, §357].

The comparisons give reasons to consider either direction
of levelling, but the reconstructed and attested cells do
not tell us which way ‘hay’ was remodelled, if at all.
The structurally comparable ‘glee’ provides a further test of how
far a comparison can support consonantal re-formation
without replacement of the nucleus.

<!-- P-morph-glee -->

‘Glee’ is a close comparison because it is another light
neuter in which the early direct and protected stems could
differ at gemination. On Ringe and Taylor’s analysis, the
direct development is \emph{*gliwi > *gli.i} > *glī*, with
loss of \emph{*w} as in ‘hay’. Contraction of identical
vowels does not establish what happened to final /e.i/.
For morphology the important contrast is with the protected
genitive: their geminated reconstruction is reflected in
*glīowes*, an inflected form retaining a labial
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 250, 259, 318].

Campbell takes *glīg* and *glīges* to be re-formations beside
*glīw/glīow*. He compares words in which final *w* was lost
after *i*, but labial forms arose by restoration from
inflected forms. The resulting alternations include *Tīg*
beside *Tīwes-* (the god’s name), with *Tīges* showing that
the *g*-form could itself spread to an inflected cell.
The model for ‘glee’ is that cross-lexical alternation, not
transfer of a *g* from the *w*-bearing genitive *glīowes*.
On this analysis re-formation could alter the end of the
word without importing a different nucleus
[@Campbell1959, §§120.2, 411, 579(4)].[^glee-variants]

Brunner instead derives the palatal from inherited *j*:
singleton *w* combined with preceding *i* as *iu*, which
underwent i-umlaut to West Saxon *ie* or *ī*, while *j*
survived. In protected forms, *j* could geminate the
labial; *w* then persisted after diphthong formation and
the palatal was lost. This accounts for a palatal without
secondary supply. It cannot be carried over unchanged to
the early history of ‘hay’, where stranded \emph{*j} is
already syllabic in \emph{*hawi}. Such an account of ‘hay’
would need independent grounds for a different input or
sequence [@SieversBrunner1965, §130 n. 3, §173 n. 2,
§247 n. 1].

[^glee-variants]: Brunner compares palatal forms of ‘new’ in compounds such as *nīġcumene* ‘newly come’, beside *nīcumen* and simplex *nīwe*. For ‘glee’ he contrasts *glīg* and forms such as *glīgmann* ‘minstrel’ with *glīw*, *glēo* and compounds such as *glēodrēam*. He also records *glīwes* beside the less frequent *glīowes* [@SieversBrunner1965, §130 n. 3, §247 n. 1].

Campbell’s account gives a reason to consider limited
re-formation, not proof of the two steps that ‘hay’ would
require. Final /e.i/ must first contract in the relevant
variety. A historically accessible source must then actually
supply /j/ without replacing the vowel. Neither ‘glee’
analysis establishes that source or its availability at the
required time. Replacing less material does not by itself
make the account preferable.

The morphology thus supplies more than a reconstructible
protected stem. Other paradigms show why an inflected stem
might spread into the direct singular, and the mass-noun
comparisons show why frequent direct forms might instead
resist replacement or extend their vocalism. Neither
motivation measures usage in prehistoric ‘hay’; its attested
datives establish inflection, not a direction of levelling.
The qualified precedent for limited re-formation makes it
worth distinguishing from whole-stem substitution when
weighing these histories against the spelling and phonology.

# 5. The interpretation of the Old English forms

## 5.1 Earliest Anglian and Kentish evidence

<!-- P-compare-forms -->

The earliest Anglian forms require a mid vowel and, on the
ordinary reading of ⟨heg⟩ and ⟨heig⟩, a palatal glide.
Their nominative or accusative use identifies the direct
singular, but neither its written vowel nor the attested
datives determine which stem supplied that vowel or how long
it was. Kentish *heiweg*, if ‘hay-road’ as argued in Section
2.1, supplies an exact written counterpart to the hiatus
stage \emph{*he.i} reached under early apocope. It does not
establish hiatus in pronunciation or identify a grammatical
cell. The Anglian simplex remains evidence for the direct
singular even if the charter compound is interpreted otherwise.

Early apocope offers two ways to keep the inherited vowel.
Desyllabification of final /i/ would yield short /hej/ and
preserve both components without analogy. The comparison
with *clēa* motivates the structural change, not its
application to final /e.i/ (Section 3.2). This is a stronger
regular-survival reading of ⟨heg⟩ than retained hiatus,
which needs a use of ⟨g⟩ not independently established there.
Alternatively, contraction to \emph{*hē} followed by
secondary supply of /j/ would give long /eːj/ without
replacing the nucleus. Neither step has been established
for this form.

The same long vowel and glide can have a protected-type
history. Ringe and Taylor replace direct \emph{*hæwi}
with protected \emph{*hēaj-} before i-umlaut (Section 1.3);
they do not transfer the already developed Anglian vowel
and glide at a later stage. A reconstructible donor and
independently motivated cases of levelling make this a
serious alternative. The datives do not establish that it
occurred in Anglian. Late apocope gives the same protected-type
outcome directly, without English stem transfer; the
ungeminated continental forms raise a separate question
(Section 6). And a single order of apocope and gemination
need not entail identical subsequent histories in Anglian
and West Saxon.

<!-- P-compare-failure -->

If desyllabification did not operate in early Anglian final
/e.i/, the analogy-free account loses its means of supplying
the glide. This would not establish that the vowel was
borrowed. The nonfinal contraction parallels give independent
grounds for considering an inherited long vowel, but
contraction must occur in the relevant final environment,
and a source accessible at that time must actually supply
/j/ without replacing the vowel. Campbell’s ‘glee’ comparison
motivates a re-formation of that scope; it establishes
neither event in ‘hay’. Neither a bare \emph{*hē} ‘hay’
nor a contemporaneous, diagnostic alternation has been found.

Paradigmatic uniformity provides independent grounds for
whole-stem levelling, but the Anglian evidence does not
require transfer of the donor’s vowel. This distinction
rests on at least one inherited-vowel history remaining
defensible. If desyllabification were excluded and either
final contraction or secondary palatal supply failed, the
positive case against obligatory replacement would be lost.
The written evidence, the different regular predictions of
the two orders and the limits of the quantity evidence
would still stand.

## 5.2 Farman and West Saxon

<!-- P-compare-regions -->

The regional evidence weighs differently on these histories.
In the earliest Anglian spellings the vowel is mid and its
length remains open. Farman’s repeated use of the acute and
⟨oe⟩ favours length in his gloss (Section 3.3). If independently
confirmed, length would rule out unchanged short /hej/ for
Farman, but not distinguish contraction with palatal supply,
protected-stem substitution, or regular late apocope. His
spellings date neither contraction nor levelling elsewhere.

West Saxon ⟨hig⟩ and ⟨hyg⟩, by contrast, point to a high vowel.
Neither direct continuation through \emph{*he.i} supplies that
quality as proposed. Under early apocope, the protected stem
has an advantage: umlaut of its diphthong provides an
independently motivated source of high vocalism (Section 3.3).
That does not require the same transfer in Anglian. Regional
reflexes of the same protected material can account for the
contrast; so can different degrees of remodelling, with
inherited direct vocalism retained or differently developed
in some Anglian forms. The record establishes neither the
boundary nor the date of any such transfer. It gives no
reason to posit different regional orders of the original
West Germanic changes.

# 6. Implications for West Germanic chronology

<!-- P-compare-continental -->

The Old High German doublet *hewi/houwi* makes stem ancestry
crucial to the chronological comparison. Ringe and Taylor
identify *hewi* as the inherited direct singular and *houwi*
as the result of extending the geminated oblique stem.
With late nasal apocope, the direct stem would geminate
regularly, so it is *hewi* that needs another explanation.
Boutkan proposes a southern development that simplifies
geminated material; he treats the High German doublets on
that model, with the geminate in the competing form supplied
by analogy. He assigns Middle Dutch *hoye* to oblique
\emph{*hauj-} and *houwe* to \emph{*hawi}, the old nominative
stem in his reconstruction. Nominative ancestry does not
mean that the stem escaped gemination: he places gemination
before apocope and takes Dutch to follow the southern pattern
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 173; @Boutkan1995, pp. 211–213].
The doublets establish that two shapes coexisted, not whether
the ungeminated member was inherited without gemination or
was secondary. Nor do they establish which way English
levelled. Gothic *hawi* supports the comparative input and
attests an ungeminated form outside West Germanic; it cannot
show that a West Germanic direct form escaped gemination.

Dahl changes the input rather than merely the ordering.
He treats the material before \emph{*j} as a diphthong and
does not posit gemination in this English class. In his
account the diphthong differentiates before \emph{*j}
becomes final. He allows the inflected forms to preserve or
restore the final palatal; his derivation of the direct
singular shows loss after umlaut and subsequent restoration.
His account of the vowel is not a development through \emph{*he.i}, and
his account of the whole form is not analogy-free
[@Dahl1938, pp. 81–86, 97]. A similar \emph{*auj} shape in
Ringe and Taylor results from resolution of a geminated
sequence, not from an inherited diphthong
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 173]. Convergence on that shape
does not decide between the reconstructions. The comparison
of the two orders here remains conditional on consonantal
\emph{*wj}.

<!-- P-compare-bed -->

The original chronological question concerns whether the
direct singular still had prevocalic \emph{*j} when gemination
applied. In *bedd* ‘bed’, *nett* ‘net’ and *cynn* ‘kin’,
the attested geminate is the very feature the early order
cannot supply in that cell. Ringe and Taylor’s extension
from protected forms explains it; on the late order it is
the regular direct outcome [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–47;
@HoggFulk2011, §2.23]. The converse test needs ungeminated
forms whose ancestry can be established independently.
Stiles treats Old English *mene* and Old Saxon *(hals-)meni*
‘necklace’, beside Old High German *menni*, as inherited
ungeminated direct forms. Ringe and Taylor likewise treat
Old High German *beti* ‘bed’ as a relic, while Boutkan’s
account permits a secondary ungeminated stem. These comparisons
weigh against the late order only to the extent that the
ungeminated forms are inherited without gemination. The
Latin contexts of the isolated ‘bed’ glosses do not by
themselves secure the vernacular case, and the evidence
does not date nasal apocope in every West Germanic variety
[@Stiles2013, pp. 15–16; @RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–47;
@Boutkan1995, pp. 211–212, 435–436].[^bed-glosses]

[^bed-glosses]: The [*Althochdeutsches Wörterbuch*, I, cols. 935–937, s.v. *betti*](https://awb.saw-leipzig.de/?sigle=AWB&lemid=B00752) indexes *peti* with Latin *stratum* (Steinmeyer–Sievers, *Die althochdeutschen Glossen*, II, p. 221, l. 60; Munich, Clm 18550a, fol. 154v, l. 22), *beti* with *area* (IV, p. 228, l. 15), and *bedi* with *culcitram* (Paulus, *Historia Langobardorum*, 1878 edition, p. 144, l. 4, apparatus p). These are edition-based readings: *peti*, in particular, is the editorial rendering of *p&i*, not a diplomatic transcription.

In ‘hay’, resolution of the labial-palatal sequence removes
the corresponding surface test: the Anglian vowel is not
itself a surviving geminate, and early direct development
supplies a mid vowel independently. Even if the final
palatal was supplied anew, the operation would not have to
replace the vowel. That difference in scope is not a reason
to prefer the account simply because it replaces less;
each operation needs its own historical motivation.
Rather, the independently motivated Anglian alternatives
weaken an apparent objection to the same early order that
can explain the continental singleton forms of ‘bed’ as
inherited. The Kentish written match gives some corroboration
to the early prediction, though a protected stem could also
have supplied the first element of the compound (Section 2.1).
The comparison turns on the ancestry of particular forms and
the evidence for each development, not a tally of analogies.

# 7. Conclusion

<!-- P-conclusion -->

Under early nasal apocope, regular development gives the
inherited direct singular a mid vowel at the hiatus stage
\emph{*he.i}. On the preferable reading ‘hay-road’, Kentish
*heiweg* gives an exact written counterpart, not a record
of its pronunciation or stem ancestry. The Anglian simplex
forms independently admit an inherited source for their
vowel. Desyllabification could preserve the whole stem;
contraction followed by secondary palatal supply could
preserve the source of the vowel alone. Both continuations
have independent motivation, but neither is established.
The West Saxon high vowel gives particular grounds for
protected vocalism under early apocope without making
whole-stem replacement the only defensible Anglian history.

A securely dated direct form with independent quantity
evidence and a distinguishable protected alternant would
help separate these histories. An independently dated
development in the precise final hiatus environment would
also change their relative standing. The potentially inherited
continental singleton forms of ‘bed’ give independent grounds
for considering early nasal apocope. The apparent necessity
of a further substitution in ‘hay’ has weighed in the other
direction. In Anglian, an entirely regular continuation remains
possible; even with secondary palatal supply, the vowel could
be inherited. These alternatives diminish that objection to
early apocope without settling the relative chronology.
