# 1. Introduction (provisional)

The Old English word for 'hay' survives in the manuscripts as *heg*,
*heig*, *hig*, *hyg*, and, in late copies, *haig*; the grammars normalize
it as West Saxon *hīeġ*, non-West-Saxon *hēġ*. Behind these spellings lies
a Proto-Germanic neuter ja-stem \*hawją (cf. Gothic *hawi*, Old High
German *hewi* beside *houwi*, Old Norse *hey*), a derivative of the verb
'to hew': hay is 'that which is cut'. The word is short, concrete, well
attested across Germanic, and etymologically transparent. It is also, as
this article will show, a remarkably sensitive diagnostic of one of the
most contested orderings in early West Germanic phonology: the relative
chronology of the loss of final short low vowels and West Germanic
consonant gemination before \*j.

If the inherited endings \*-az and \*-ą were lost before gemination, as
Ringe and Taylor maintain [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–47], the
nominative–accusative singular \*hawją lost its ending in time to escape
gemination altogether, and an ungeminated direct-case form developed —
the form that surfaces in Old High German as *hewi*. If instead the nasal
ending survived gemination, as Dahl, Hogg and Fulk hold [@Dahl1938,
pp. 81–86; @HoggFulk2011, §2.23], the direct singular geminated like its
oblique cases, and West Saxon *hīeġ* is its regular outcome. The first
account must then explain the attested Old English forms by analogical
replacement from the protected cases; the second needs no analogy for
this word, but must explain away the ungeminated forms of the continental
languages.

The problem has usually been treated as settled in one direction or the
other by general arguments about the noun classes concerned. This article
re-examines it from the word itself: a source-critical collection of the
actual Old English attestations and their grammatical cells; an
assessment of what the spellings do and do not establish about vowel
quantity and syllabification; an explicit, stage-by-stage comparison of
the competing derivations, including the question whether any
independently motivated sequence of regular changes could carry the
ungeminated direct form to an attested Old English shape without
analogical interference; and an evaluation of the analogical account
against the comparative evidence. The aim is not to vindicate a
chronology but to determine what the evidence of this word can, and
cannot, decide.

# 2. Previous scholarship

## 2.1 The chronological problem

West Germanic gemination doubled a consonant before \*j (\*satjan >
OE *settan*), but the geminating \*j was itself subject to earlier loss in
certain endings. In the masculine ja-stems the nominative singular ended
in \*-jaz and the accusative in \*-ją; in the neuters, nominative and
accusative alike ended in \*-ją. Whether these endings were still present
when gemination operated determines whether the direct singular of a
light-stemmed ja-stem noun geminated at all.

Hogg and Fulk set out the alternatives with exemplary clarity. The
expected development, with nominative \*-az lost early and accusative
\*-ą lost late, would give a pre-Old-English masculine paradigm with
ungeminated nominative \*sagi beside geminated accusative \*saggi; "it is
noteworthy, however, that **\*sege**, the expected reflex of \*sagi, never
occurs in OE, the nom.sg. always being of the type *secg* ... We must
therefore assume, with Dahl (1938: 81–6), that only \*-az was lost prior
to gemination and that acc.sg. *secg*, neut.sg. cynn are regular forms",
the masculine nominative having been re-formed by the syncretism of
nominative and accusative; "[a]lternatively, one might suggest, following
Campbell (1977: §576), see also Dal (1934), that both inflexions were
lost prior to gemination, in which event all geminated forms in the
nom.acc.sg. would be re-formations from the oblique forms or the plural"
[@HoggFulk2011, pp. 20–21, §2.23]. The two alternatives named in that
paragraph — Dahl's split chronology and Dal's early loss — remain the
live options; Ringe and Taylor's ordering is a modern restatement of the
second [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 44–47], and Boutkan's of the first
[@Boutkan1995, pp. 209–213]. For the neuter 'hay' the difference is
stark: under the split chronology the direct singular kept its nasal
ending through gemination and *hīeġ* is regular; under early loss the
direct singular was \*hawi, ungeminated, and the attested forms require
explanation.

## 2.2 The early-apocope treatments: Ringe–Taylor, Campbell, Wright

Ringe and Taylor identify \*hawi explicitly as "the inherited nom.-acc.
sg.", comparing Old High German *hewi*, and note that the gemination of
\*wj "was reversible": Proto-Northwest-Germanic \*awj yielded
Proto-West-Germanic \*[aww'] but reverted to pre-OE \*[auj]
[@RingeTaylor2014, p. 53]. Their derivation of the attested word runs
from the protected stem: \*hawją > PWGmc \*hawi (direct) beside
\*[haww'-] (protected); the protected stem develops through \*hauj- to
pre-OE \*hēaj-, whence with i-umlaut West Saxon *hīeġ* and Anglian
*hēġ*; the inherited direct form, carried as far as \*hǣwi, is marked as
analogically replaced at that stage [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 173, 246].
What \*hǣwi would have become had it been left alone is nowhere stated.

Campbell draws the same distinction from the other end. His account of
the sound change gives "WGmc \*hauuj" with loss of u: the "final result
ēg or īeg" [@Campbell1959, §120.2]. But in the morphology he is equally
explicit that "\*hewi, \*gliwi without gemination would be the
undisturbed developments", the attested nominative–accusative singulars
of both words standing "under influence of the other cases", and *glīg*
being a "re-formation" [@Campbell1959, §579(4), §411]. The long vowel of
his "final result", it should be noticed, belongs to the geminate route;
it is not an independent observation about the direct case.

Wright's analogy is narrower. For him the vocalism of *hīeġ* is regular,
and only the final consonant is borrowed: intervocalic \*j was lost after
a long vowel or diphthong, and "[f]orms like nom. hieg ... had the final
g from the inflected forms, as gen. hieges, dat. hiege" [@Wright1914,
§270 Note; cf. §357]. Two things deserve emphasis. First, Wright's
*hīeges* and *hīege* are paradigm illustrations: as Section 3 shows, no
overtly inflected form of this noun is identified in the sources examined,
a fact already registered by Dahl — "Inflected forms are
thus not to be found in my material" [@Dahl1938, hay section]. Second,
the three early-apocope authorities do not invoke analogy for the same
thing. Ringe–Taylor and Campbell replace the entire inherited stem,
vocalism and all; Wright borrows only a final consonant into a stem
whose vocalism he takes to be regular. Any evaluation of "the"
analogical account must keep these scopes apart.

## 2.3 The late-apocope treatments: Dahl, Hogg, Hogg–Fulk, Boutkan

Dahl's discussion remains the fullest treatment of the word. On the
general chronology he holds that gemination preceded the loss of \*-ą,
so that *cynn* < \*kunją is regular, citing the Weser runic *kunni*; he
explicitly rejects Dal's contrary argument from Old High German *hewi*
and *gewi* [@Dahl1938, pp. 82–84; @Dal1934]. But for 'hay' itself he
proposes something that cuts across the whole debate: that English
\*-awj- never geminated at all, there being "no recorded w-forms" of Old
English nouns of this shape; diphthongal differentiation of \*aw was
already under way when \*-j became final, and final \*-i was regularly
lost after long vowels and diphthongs. His derivation of the direct
singular runs \*hauja̯ > \*hǣoi(ə) > \*hǣoi > \*hīe, \*hē, the attested
*hī(e)g* and *heg* having their final -g "reintroduced from the inflected
forms" (gen. \*haujas > \*hī(e)ges); and he "strongly doubts" that
\*hewi ever existed in English, Gothic *hawi* and Old High German *hewi*
notwithstanding [@Dahl1938, hay section]. On Dahl's view, then, 'hay' is
not a gemination diagnostic at all — and even his non-analogical
vocalism imports the final consonant from the paradigm.

Hogg's grammar derives the word from the geminated stem: \*hawwj- >
\*hauwj- > \*hēawj- > \*hīewj- > West Saxon *hīġ*, non-West-Saxon *hēġ*,
with \*j lost after the high diphthong and \*w after the low one, while
conceding that "the phonology of the change is obscure"
[@HoggGrammar1992, §3.18]. His earlier study of analogy supplies the
typological frame: the masculine ja-stems preserve traces of a
pre-analogical paradigm with ungeminated nominative beside geminated
accusative, levelled in *both* directions — *hyll* generalizing the
geminate, *hyse*, *mete*, *ile* the plain nominative — with "the balance
... a very slender one" [@Hogg1979Analogy, pp. 68–73; cf. @Adamczyk2018
on *mete* ~ *mette*]. Mixed paradigms of exactly the required kind are
thus independently attested in Old English; what is not thereby settled
is whether 'hay' ever had one.

Boutkan likewise orders gemination before low-vowel apocope, attributing
apparent ungeminated relics to later remodelling [@Boutkan1995,
pp. 209–213, 435–436]. On the other side, Stiles adduces comparative
ungeminated relics — Old English *mene*, Old Saxon *(hals-)meni* against
Old High German *menni* — as evidence for early loss [@Stiles2013,
pp. 15–16], with the standing qualification that relics argue inheritance
only if their transmission is in fact inherited.

## 2.4 The spelling-based discussions: Jones and Minkova

A separate strand of scholarship approaches the word through its late
Old English and Middle English spellings. Jones reads the Lindisfarne
alternations ⟨heig⟩ ~ ⟨heg⟩ (beside ⟨gefraign-⟩ ~ ⟨gefregn⟩, ⟨ðeign⟩ ~
⟨ðegn⟩) as showing a vowel space modified before the voiced palatal
fricative — a diphthongization of the early Middle English Breaking type
[@Jones1989, p. 21]; in his later discussion the alternants appear as
[hej]/[heij], and the development is generalized as trigger effacement,
[hej] → [heij] → [hei] [@Jones1989, pp. 149, 152]. His quantity
notation, however, is not consistent: the same word is transcribed with
a short vowel in the Breaking discussion but long — ⟨hēg⟩ [heej̆], ⟨hīg⟩
[hiij̆] — in his tabulation of the West Saxon forms [@Jones1989, p. 150,
Table 3.17], and his short-vowel classification of ⟨heg⟩ elsewhere
glosses the item 'meadow', raising the suspicion of conflation with the
distinct noun *hege* 'hedge, enclosure' [@Jones1989, p. 55, Table 2.29;
cf. @Minkova2014, p. 64]. Minkova's contribution is of a different kind:
discussing the hypothesis of contrastive diphthongal length, she observes
that "the etymological length in [ej] as in OE *hēġ* 'hay', also spelled
⟨heig⟩, and (presumably) [ej] as in OE *weġ* 'way' ... should not have
been preserved as /eːj/ in hay and /ej/ in way" — the two words merge in
Middle English [@Minkova2014, p. 178]. She assumes the conventional long
vowel of *hēġ*; what her argument establishes is that the Middle English
and modern reflexes are incapable of recovering the Old English
quantity, since any contrast in this position was neutralized.

## 2.5 What is at issue

The scholarship thus divides over two separable questions. The first is
chronological: was the direct singular of \*hawją still \*hawją when
gemination operated? The second is lexical: by what route did the
attested Old English forms acquire their vocalism and their final -ġ?
Every published account except the plain geminate route borrows the
final consonant, the vocalism, or both from the inflected forms — forms
which, for this noun, are nowhere attested in Old English. The following
sections assemble what is actually attested, and then ask what each
proposed history predicts.

# 3. The Old English evidence

## 3.1 The witnesses

The full concordance, with loci, editions, verification status and
exclusions, is maintained in `CONCORDANCE.tsv`; the principal witnesses
are the following. In the ninth-century Mercian gloss of the Vespasian
Psalter the word appears as *heg* throughout — at least eleven tokens
(Pss. 36.2, 71.16, 91.8, 101.5, 101.12, 102.15, 103.14, 104.35, 105.20,
128.6, 146.8, with a further token in the Vespasian Hymns), always
glossing Latin *faenum*, always without an overt ending [@Dahl1938, hay
section]. Two Kentish charters supply the earliest dated tokens, both as
compound first elements: *heiweg* 'hay-road' (A.D. 839) — the earliest
⟨hei⟩ spelling — and *Hegforde* (A.D. 858). The Old English Bede has
accusative *heg* in *on sumera heg ne māweþ* [@Miller1890, I p. 28].
The late West Saxon Lambeth Psalter writes *hyg*, *hig* [@Dahl1938;
Lindelöf's edition]. In Gregory's *Dialogues* the eleventh-century
Corpus manuscript (C) writes *heig* and *hig* where Cotton Otho (O) has
*heg* and Hatton 76 (H) substitutes *gærs* 'grass' [@Hecht1900, pp.
36–37]. Ælfric's Colloquy has *mid hig*, dative syntax with no overt
ending. The glossaries give *Foenum, heg* (Cleopatra A.iii), *hig oððe
gærs* (Julius A.ii), and — in a fragmentary vocabulary of about 1150 —
*Fenum, hei, uel græs* [@WrightWuelcker1884, I.289.45, I.325.21, I.547].
In the West Saxon gospels at Mark 6.39 Corpus 140 has *hig*, with later
*haig* (Hatton 38) and *heig* (Royal); the Lindisfarne gloss there has
*gers* and Rushworth *heggetgrese* [@Skeat1871, Mark 6:39].

Three classes of false positive must be excluded: the plural pronoun
*hig*; the distinct noun *hege* 'hedge, enclosure', whose Middle English
continuation is also written *hay* [@Minkova2014, p. 64]; and glosses
where *gærs*/*gers* translates *faenum* with a different lexeme. Parallel
copies of one passage — the three Dialogues manuscripts, the West Saxon
gospel manuscripts — are single textual traditions, not independent
witnesses.

## 3.2 The paradigm

Classified by grammatical cell (full table in `PARADIGM.md`), the record
has a striking shape. Direct-case tokens (nominative or accusative,
formally indistinguishable) are plentiful: every psalter, prose and
glossary token above. Syntactically dative *mid hig* shows no ending.
No overtly inflected token is identified in the collated sources: no genitive,
no dative in -e, no unambiguous plural — Dahl's observation of 1938
stands. Wright's *hīeges* and *hīege* are paradigm expectations, as is
the *mid hīeġe* of normalized grammars. Both of the historically central
reconstructed entities — the protected oblique stem that serves as the
analogical donor in the early-apocope accounts, and the inherited
ungeminated direct form \*hawi/\*hewi — are equally unattested in Old
English. The analogical explanation and the regular-development
explanation are, within the sources examined, evidentially symmetrical:
each rests on a reconstructed form.

## 3.3 Dialect and date distribution

The spellings sort cleanly. Anglian and early Kentish sources write
⟨heg⟩/⟨hei-⟩ (VP, VH, Bede, charters); late West Saxon and later copies
write ⟨hig⟩, ⟨hyg⟩, ⟨hieg⟩, with ⟨heig⟩/⟨haig⟩ in the latest gospel
manuscripts and Dialogues C. The West Saxon i/y/ie-vocalism is the
regular i-umlaut of \*ēa — a stem shape that only the geminate route
produces; the Anglian ē-vocalism is its regular smoothing. The late
⟨ei⟩/⟨ai⟩ spellings belong to the Middle English diphthongal
developments discussed by Jones. The single ⟨hei⟩ of c. 1150 postdates
the Old English quantity system. Nothing in the distribution requires —
though nothing excludes — an ungeminated relic in the Anglian/Kentish
⟨heg⟩/⟨hei-⟩ forms (Section 5).
