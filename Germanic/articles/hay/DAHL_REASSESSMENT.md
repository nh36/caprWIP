# Dahl's no-gemination analysis of 'hay': reassessment (research cycle 2)

Source basis: the held original of Dahl 1938 (local extract
`docs/references/local/sc047-early-apocope/nominal-levelling/dahl1938.txt`,
with the PDF beside it for image checks; OCR conventions: `@` = ǣ, `8/3`
= g/ȝ). The hay section, the -awj- discussion, the island/glee/hue/new
parallels, the chronology (pp. 81–86) and the Excursus on the continental
forms (pp. 107–109) were all read in the original. Dal (1934) is known
here only through Dahl's and Hogg–Fulk's reports (ILL pending).

## 1. Dahl's argument, reconstructed stage by stage

### 1.1 His own notation, preserved

Direct singular:

> \*hauja̯ > \*hǣoi(ə) > \*hǣoi > \*hīe, \*hē → *hī(e)g*, *heg*

Genitive singular:

> \*haujas > \*hǣoiæs > *hī(e)ges*, *heges*

(\*hauja̯ is Dahl's notation for the form with weakened/nasalized final
vowel; \*hǣo is his notation for the differentiated diphthong that
elsewhere appears as ǣa/ēa; the parenthesized (ə) is his residue of the
final vowel before its loss.)

### 1.2 The eight required determinations

1. **Inherited representation.** Dahl starts from \*hauja̯ — i.e. he
   treats the medial sequence as a *genuine diphthong* \*au followed by
   \*j, syllabified \*-au̯.ja-, citing Chadwick for the syllabification.
   The glide is not the second consonant of a \*-wj- cluster but the
   second element of the nucleus. This representational decision carries
   the whole analysis.

2. **Is gemination categorically excluded?** For this class, in English,
   yes: "As there are no recorded w-forms of OE nouns containing PrG
   -aui-, it is an obvious conclusion that gemination did not take place
   within this group in English." The exclusion is conditioned by the
   diphthongal syllabification (gemination affected consonant + j, and
   \*u̯ here is not a consonant), and it is categorical for the whole
   -auj- group — in his Excursus (pp. 107–109) he extends the denial to
   all of West Germanic, explaining OHG *houwi*, *gouwi*, *gistrouwi* by
   gradual \*i > \*u assimilation after the unmutated diphthong and OHG
   *hewi* via \*haui with later modification, with OFris *hā*, OS *gō*
   from -aui- directly. The continental extension is the most exposed
   part of the account; the English claim is the operative one here.

3. **Mechanism of the differentiation.** The "English differentiation"
   of \*au (his \*ǣo — the ordinary change otherwise written \*au > ēa)
   had already set in when \*-ą was lost; once the diphthong had
   differentiated, "a solution of the diphthong would hardly be
   possible", i.e. the \*u̯ could no longer be reinterpreted as
   consonantal \*w. This is not a special sound change: it is the
   regular Old English development of \*au, applied inside this word
   because of the diphthongal syllabification.

4. **Chronological order.** (i) differentiation of \*au under way;
   (ii) loss of final \*-ą(ə), stranding \*-j, which becomes final and
   vocalizes to \*-i; (iii) i-umlaut of the \*ǣo diphthong by that
   final \*-i (WS īe, non-WS ē); (iv) loss of final \*-i after long
   vowels and diphthongs (the law he states as parallel to final \*-u
   loss). Each step is a change he applies elsewhere; none is invented
   for this word.

5. **Does i-umlaut contribute to WS īe?** Yes, essentially as in the
   geminate account: umlaut of the ēa-type diphthong yields WS īe,
   Anglian/Kentish ē. The difference from the geminate route is not the
   umlaut but what created the diphthong.

6. **Restoration of the final consonant.** After final \*-i loss the
   direct form is \*hīe/\*hē; the attested -g is "reintroduced from the
   inflected forms", i.e. from gen. \*haujas > *hī(e)ges*, where the
   intervocalic \*-i- (his -i- = [j]) is *regularly* preserved after the
   palatal diphthong and strengthened to -g-. Note that on his account
   the oblique -g- is **not** a geminate product: the obliques too are
   ungeminated, and their -g- is the regular intervocalic reflex. The
   analogical step is therefore minimal — a single final consonant from
   a paradigm whose stem vowel already matches.

7. **Independent support for the vocalism.** Dahl adduces the same
   development in \*gauja- 'district' > *-gē/-gie* (the place-name
   element in Eastry, Surrey, Ely), \*awjō 'island' > \*ǣoiu > *īeg/ēg*
   (with -g "regularly disappearing but might be retained or
   reintroduced from other cases"; Northumbrian *ēu* via early -i- loss
   plus contraction), and \*gliuja- > \*glīe > *glī(e)g* beside
   unumlauted *glio(w)*. The verbs *cīegan*, *cēgan*, *strēgan* he takes
   as regular on the same basis, and ætīewan's -w- "cannot be due to
   gemination". The class behaves uniformly on his reading.

8. **Why no English \*hewi.** Because the consonantal-w parse never
   existed in English: by the time \*-j was stranded, the diphthong had
   differentiated and could not be resolved into vowel + \*w. Gothic
   *hawi* and OHG *hewi* reflect languages where (on his view) the
   resolution went differently or the diphthong modified later; he
   "strongly doubts" that \*hewi ever existed in English, and observes
   that no w-forms of any OE -auj- noun are recorded.

### 1.3 Translation into modern terms

In modern notation Dahl's path is: PGmc \*haują with \*-au̯.j-
syllabification; regular \*au > ǣa/ēa already in progress at the loss of
\*-ą; stranded \*-j > \*-i; i-umlaut ēa > īe (WS) / ē (Angl.) by the
final \*-i; apocope of \*-i after a long diphthong; analogical final -ġ
from the regular ungeminated obliques \*haujas > hī(e)ġes. The crucial
divergence from both CAPR models is at the very first step: both frozen
models parse \*-wj- as consonant + j (Model A vocalizes the stranded
glide late; B2 geminates it), whereas Dahl parses \*-u̯j- as
diphthong + j from the start.

## 2. Vocalism and final consonant assessed separately

**Vocalism: conditionally defensible and essentially regular.** Dahl's
stressed nucleus is produced by the ordinary \*au > ēa change plus
ordinary i-umlaut. Granted his syllabification premise, every vocalic
step is an independently established Old English sound law; nothing is
invented for 'hay'. The premise itself (diphthongal \*-au̯.j-) is the
real locus of dispute: Ringe and Taylor's "reversible gemination"
(\*awj > [aww'] > [auj], p. 53) reaches the same pre-OE \*auj by the
opposite route, and Hogg §3.18's geminate derivation likewise passes
through \*hauwj-. **For the stressed vowel, Dahl's route and the
geminate route converge on the same pre-OE diphthong and are thereafter
observationally equivalent in English.**

**Final consonant: analogical, by his own statement.** The attested
*hī(e)g/heg* has -g "reintroduced from the inflected forms". Dahl's
account is therefore *not* a wholly non-analogical derivation of the
attested word — only of its vocalism. What distinguishes his analogy
from Ringe–Taylor's is its scope (one segment, as in Wright, not the
whole stem) and the fact that his donor obliques have the same nucleus
as the direct form, so the levelling is phonologically invisible except
for the -g.

Two entries carry the adversarial weight. *Hue* is the one word where
Dahl's class prediction fails on the surface (no †hīeg 'hue'), and his
homonymy explanation (avoidance of clash with 'hay', *hīew/hīw* a
"compromise" between \*hī(e)g and unmutated *hīow*) is the one plainly
ad hoc move in the chapter. *Glee* is his best positive card: the
*glīg* ~ *glēo* doublet is exactly what a stranded-glide account with
variable umlaut feeding predicts, and the strictly West Saxon
distribution of *glīg* (always ⟨i⟩ before ⟨g⟩, never ⟨io/eo⟩) that he
documents is a genuine observation (see COMPARATIVE_CONTROLS.md).

His negative universal — "no recorded w-forms of OE nouns containing
PrG -aui-" — survives the cycle-1 and cycle-2 collations: no †hæw-,
†heaw- noun token of 'hay', no †glēow-type for the direct cell of
'island' or 'district', has turned up in the sources examined. The claim
remains a bounded corpus observation (Dahl's corpus plus ours), not a
proof.

## 4. Chronological compatibility

Dahl advances the hay analysis *within* a book that argues gemination
preceded \*-ą loss (pp. 81–86: Weser runic *kunni*; hyse/mete/ile/dyne;
nasalization counteracting apocope; Dal 1934 reported as "vindicating
the traditional view" on grounds Dahl rejects). But the hay derivation
itself does not use that chronology: it removes the word from
gemination's input class altogether. Check against both orderings:

- **Under late apocope (Dahl's own, = B2's ordering):** \*-ą is present
  during gemination, but gemination is inapplicable (no consonant + j);
  \*-ą then weakens/drops, stranding \*-j. Consistent.
- **Under early apocope (Dal/R&T's ordering):** \*-ą is lost before
  gemination; \*-j is stranded earlier; the only requirement is that the
  differentiation of \*au has begun before the stranded glide could be
  reparsed as \*w. Dahl's own formulation ("differentiation had already
  set in when -ą was lost") is actually easier the *later* the apocope,
  but nothing excludes the early ordering if differentiation is early.

**Consequence: Dahl's route is compatible with either general
chronology, at the cost (under early apocope) of one additional timing
assumption about differentiation. 'Hay' ceases to be a diagnostic of
the gemination–apocope ordering on his account.** This is the sense in
which Dahl "changes the nature of the problem" (assignment outcome D).

## 5. Verdict

**Classification: conditionally defensible for the vocalism;
observationally near-equivalent to the geminate route; explicitly
analogical for the final consonant.**

- The vocalism is *regular* granted one contested representational
  premise (diphthongal syllabification of \*-awj-). It is not ad hoc:
  the same premise is applied across the whole -auj- class with
  largely consistent results, *hue* being the exception he must patch.
- From pre-OE \*auj onwards, Dahl's derivation and the geminate
  derivation (B2; also R&T's reversible gemination) are segment-for-
  segment identical in English. No Old English datum in the collated
  record distinguishes "never geminated, diphthong from the start" from
  "geminated, then resolved back to a diphthong". The difference is
  recoverable only from comparative evidence (whether OHG *hewi* etc.
  require a consonantal-w stage) and from theory-internal economy.
- The final -ġ is analogical on his own statement, so Dahl does not
  supply what outcome A of the thesis assessment would need (a fully
  regular derivation of the complete attested form). What he supplies
  is a second route to the attested *vocalism* that bypasses
  gemination.

**Effect on cycle 1's Claim C ("West Saxon *hīeġ* requires
gemination"):** the claim must be weakened. What the West Saxon
⟨ie/i/y⟩ vocalism requires is an **ēa-type diphthongal stem nucleus
feeding i-umlaut**. That nucleus is reachable by (i) gemination with
\*wwj-resolution (B2, regular), (ii) analogical stem replacement before
umlaut (A2 = Ringe–Taylor), or (iii) Dahl's never-resolved inherited
diphthong. It is *not* reachable from the consonantal-w forms \*hæwi/
\*hewi by any independently supported change (cycle 1's A0/A1 findings
stand). Claim C is therefore confirmed only in the narrower form: *the
WS vocalism excludes the ungeminated consonantal-w direct form as its
source; it does not by itself prove historical gemination.* See
ADVERSARIAL_REVIEW.md.
