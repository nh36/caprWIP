## Eadwine Psalter: direct readings and textual provenance

The two located manuscript readings of ⟨hieg⟩ were checked in Cambridge,
Trinity College MS R.17.1, produced at Canterbury around 1150. Both are in
the Romanum column, aligned with the Latin line below; the manuscript does
not number the gloss lines.

The Latin *fenum* is nominative singular; *terrae* depends on it. | ‘Like hay of the earth’; the gloss has no overt case ending. | No visible accent, erasure, or correction. These are direct manuscript observations, not normalized *hīeġ* or
editorial reconstructions. The related Vespasian Psalter has ⟨heg⟩ at
both loci; the Paris Psalter has ⟨heg⟩ at 71.16. This supports a wider
Psalter-gloss tradition for the lexeme, but does not establish direct
dependence or explain Eadwine's ⟨i⟩.

Faulkner's 2017 author preprint (28 PDF sheets; the published chapter is
pp. 72–107, with no established sheet-to-page mapping) describes the
manuscript as a Canterbury production of the 1150s (preprint sheet 1).
He summarizes Berghaus's classification of the gloss as an A-type text,
ultimately related to Vespasian but most immediately to a witness related
to Cambridge UL Ff. 1. 23; he also notes nearly 2,500 independent glosses
and leaves the exemplar's age and provenance open (sheets 2–3). Archaic
features may point to an exemplar substantially earlier than the
late-tenth- to mid-eleventh-century intermediate in Berghaus's stemma.
The copy is layered: OE1 and a close collaborator largely preserve the
exemplar; OE2 modernizes increasingly; OE3 contributes at greater length;
and OE4 corrects part of the text against a D-type gloss (sheet 3 and
following discussion). The overall A-type affiliation does not establish
the stemmatic status of these two forms. Inherited gloss spelling, a
contemporary scribe's spelling, and a later scribal revision remain
separate possibilities. The fol. 258r attribution identifies a hand, not
the dialect of that hand or the language of its exemplar; the fol. 125r
hand is unassigned. Neither reading can presently be classified as
Kentish or West Saxon.

The two readings therefore establish that manuscript ⟨hieg⟩ exists in
Eadwine; they do not establish its date within the transmission, its
dialect, or which historical source of West Saxon ⟨ie⟩ (if any) applies.

## West Saxon ⟨ie⟩: sources and dialect qualifications

Hogg 2006: 400 identifies three proposed sources of West Saxon ⟨ie⟩:
palatalization of earlier /eː/, i-umlaut of earlier diphthongs, and
hiatus resolution involving /i/ and /e/. The page's table contrasts the
spellings and conditioned distributions of Alfredian and Ælfrician
West Saxon; *hieran* ‘hear’ and *wiersa* ‘worse’ are illustrative
outputs, not evidence that any one source applies to ‘hay’. Hogg–Fulk
2011 §5.163 restates the categories as palatal diphthongization of
*/e(p)/, i-umlaut of specified diphthongs, and possible resolution in
forms such as *sċe* ‘be’ subjunctive. The mechanisms have distinct
conditions:

- Hogg–Fulk §§5.49–54 discusses palatal diphthongization of original
  front vowels conditioned by a preceding palatal, with examples such as
  *giefu* ‘gift’ and *sċeatt* ‘treasure’. These illustrate the broader
  palatal-conditioned development; they are not attestations of ‘hay’.
  The representation and outcome vary by dialect, and Hogg–Fulk
  explicitly distinguishes West Saxon from Northumbrian outcomes.
- Hogg–Fulk §§5.82–84 treats i-umlaut of diphthongs before a following
  /i/ or /j/. Examples include West Saxon *hyran* ‘hear’ from a
  diphthongal source and *wyrsa* ‘worse’ among the forms involving
  breaking and i-umlaut. The treatment of some earlier diphthongs,
  especially /eo/, is explicitly uncertain (§5.84).
- Hogg–Fulk §§5.131–47 treats hiatus resolution. In §5.146 the special
  West Saxon sequence *i + e* is commonly resolved as ⟨ie⟩, as in
  *sīe* and *hīe*; §5.147 states that the analysis is not certain and
  contrasts West Saxon with Anglian outcomes. Campbell §§39 and 234–35
  likewise distinguishes inherited diphthong/long-vowel spellings such
  as *hieran*, *liehtan*, and *ciese* from West Saxon ⟨ie⟩ produced by
  development of short vowels or by contraction; Campbell notes
  dialectal hiatus in forms corresponding to West Saxon *sīe*.

These independently attested mechanisms show why ⟨ie⟩ alone does not
identify the history of the Eadwine forms. The normalized dictionary
form *hīeġ* remains distinct from the manuscript's two ⟨hieg⟩ readings;
the readings' dialect and relevant conditioning environment have not
been established.

Hogg 2006: 399–402 distinguishes Alfredian and Ælfrician clusters rather
than treating all West Saxon evidence as one homogeneous chronological
series. His Mercian discussion (pp. 402–404) stresses substantial
linguistic differences between the Vespasian Psalter and Rushworth¹.
His Northumbrian discussion (pp. 405–406) cautions against assigning
geographical significance automatically to differences between Aldred
and Owun. These qualifications matter when using spellings in different
manuscript traditions to reconstruct the chronology of one dialect.

## Lindisfarne accents and Ritt's proposal

Ritt 2000 challenges Jones's interpretation of Aldred's accentuation.
The observed distribution in Ritt's Matthew census is 426 accents, 318
in graphically closed syllables and 108 in graphically open syllables
(pp. 505–508). Ritt proposes that acutes preferentially mark long vowels
where length would otherwise be unpredictable from the spelling. In the
p. 508 table he excludes 50 ambiguous cases (35 with parallel closed
forms and 15 instances of *gé*), leaving 376; his analysis accounts for
318 closed-syllable and 10 antepenultimate items, 328/376 (87.3%),
with 48 exceptions. This is Ritt's explanatory account, not an observed
fact that every acute directly marks quantity.

The p. 509 CVC test should not be cited as a verified 98% result. Ritt
reports a 100-item sample with 79 short and 21 long vowels; after 15 of
the long-vowel items have accented variants, six items remain
arithmetically, but the prose then counts six reduced pronouns plus two
exceptions. Those statements total 102 items, not 100. The discrepancy
is retained as a caveat; no corrected accuracy is inferred. Neither
Ritt's proposal nor his Lindisfarne counts are evidence that Farman used
accents in the same way.

## Final bounded Farman control search

The corrected accented census remains 197 surface types and 301 tokens
(196 non-target types/299 tokens plus *hóeg*, one type/two tokens). The
bounded calibration includes unaccented tokens and separates graphical
structure, grammatical form, token, surface-type, and lexical-type
counts:

- Short-vowel-plus-glide controls (*weg*, *dæg*): 1/53 accented tokens;
  1/9 surface types and 1/2 lexical types with an accent. Among bare
  closed monosyllables the count is 1/26; among forms with a following
  vowel graph it is 0/27. Spellings include *weg*, *wege*, *wegæ*,
  *weig*, *wæg*, *wæge*, and *wég*.
- Long-vowel-plus-glide panel: 1/14 tokens, 1/10 surface types, and
  1/2 related lexical types with an accent. Noun *stīg* has *stígas*
  (accusative plural; Mt 3:3, fol. 4v, Latin *semitas eius*) and
  *stige* (beside *gongas*; case/number unresolved; Mark 1:3, fol. 52v);
  both have following vowel graphs. Verb *stīgan* supplies twelve
  tokens and eight surface types, all unaccented. *Astig* at Mt 27:40,
  fol. 48v, has a closed stressed root but is a prefixed disyllabic
  imperative; the other eleven forms are multisyllabic. Thus no
  monosyllabic long-vowel-plus-glide word-form control was found, and
  noun and verb counts are not independent lexical evidence.
- The *stīg* forms and selected verbal contexts were checked against
  Tamoto 2013, pp. 8, 96, and 104. Infinitive and finite *stīgan* forms
  with ⟨g⟩ before a back-vowel ending (five tokens) are listed
  separately, not treated as palatal-glide controls.
- Potential short vowels before homorganic clusters remain separate.
  The short-HL matched panel is 5/133 accented tokens (5/23 surface
  types; 4/5 lexical types). The sensitivity analysis records 6/186 if
  these vowels are counted as short, and 6/147 if counted as lengthened;
  repeated tokens do not constitute independent evidence. Eight
  uncertain *gang*-family tokens and one *dægne* are separately listed
  and excluded.

The refreshed script and output are
`calibrate_farman_accents.py` and `FARMAN_ACCENT_CALIBRATION.tsv`.
This deliberately bounded search found no additional suitable
monosyllabic long-vowel-plus-palatal-glide control. The selected sample
does not support a precise probability or likelihood ratio. At most, it
describes Farman's own late tenth-century accent practice; it does not
settle the quantity of *hóeg* or project Aldred's practice onto Farman.

## Status, limitations, and next adjudication

Superseded conclusions are marked in `QUANTITY_EVIDENCE.md`,
`UNRESOLVED.md`, `ADVERSARIAL_REVIEW.md`, and the earlier cycle memos.
The former categorical exclusion of Model A on West Saxon vocalism is
conditional, not current; neither is it established that every viable
historical derivation predicts a long direct vowel. Continental doublets
are attestations; identification of their grammatical stems and
directions of levelling remain historical inferences dependent on
chronology. The article and concordances distinguish direct manuscript
verification from edition-based evidence.

The British Library S 1196 preview was too faint to verify the reading
directly. Lindisfarne target images could not be retrieved reliably at
usable resolution; S 328 was not checked against an adequate facsimile.
Those entries remain edition-based. The Eadwine readings above are the
only located manuscript ⟨hieg⟩ forms, and their textual and linguistic
provenance remains open. Private sources are checkout-specific; use the
registered `holdings.tsv` and `page_map.tsv`, verify local paths and
checksums after moving worktrees, and do not redistribute restricted
files.

The next cycle's unresolved questions are the regular development of
the inherited direct stem under early nasal apocope; the applicability
and relative chronology of hiatus resolution; the interpretation of
West Saxon vocalism; and the necessity, timing, direction, and dialectal
scope of paradigmatic levelling, including the comparative implications
of ‘hay’, ‘bed’, and other ja-stem nouns. No such substantive question is
adjudicated here.
