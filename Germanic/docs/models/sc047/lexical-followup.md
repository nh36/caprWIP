# SC047 lexical and comparative follow-up

## Fen: a regional comparison, not a selected English cell

Hofmann (1970, pp. 95–96) reconstructs neuter *fanja-* ‘marsh’
from Gothic *fani*, Old Norse *fen*, Old English *fen(n)* and
Old High German *fenni/fenne*. He treats Old English masculine
*fennas* as secondary. He disputes a reported Old High German
feminine *fenna*: the Florentine gloss edition reads *fenne*
(Steinmeyer–Sievers III, p. 648, l. 46). The Latin gloss
*palude* does not settle the vernacular case; the biblical
context at Daniel 8:3 has *ante paludem*. Blok and De Tollenaere's
alternative feminine *fanjō* must be distinguished from the
inherited neuter, not inferred merely from a later feminine reflex.

**Old Saxon.** Wadstein (1899, *Kleinere altsächsische
Sprachdenkmäler*, p. 59, ll. 32–33) records *feni* in the Essen
Gospel glosses to John 7, against Latin *palus*; his index
classifies it as nominative singular. The singleton and final
vowel are the relevant readings. This is a tenth-century gloss,
not a free vernacular clause, so the Latin lemma supports but
does not prove the Old Saxon case. The edition text was checked;
the surrounding glyphs in the OCR were not used as readings.
Hofmann (pp. 97–98) contrasts northern *feni* with southern
Continental *fenni*, including the OHG reflex just discussed.
*Hadunueni* and the open-syllable vowel reflexes in northern
Dutch and western Low German independently point to a northern
singleton stem, but do not identify an inflectional cell.
The distribution has overlaps and is not a clean branch boundary.

**Old Frisian.** Hofmann (pp. 104–105) cites both Brokmer
manuscripts (B1 late thirteenth century, B2 dated 1345):
Buma (1949), B1 p. 101, ll. 11–12 and 16–17; B2 p. 102,
l. 6 and p. 104, l. 7 and rubric. The Buma–Ebel (1965)
edition independently confirms masculine subject *thi fene*
([§163](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/texte/etcs/germ/afries/brokmrx/brokm178.htm)),
doubled *fenne* after prepositions
([§51](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/texte/etcs/germ/afries/brokmrx/brokm067.htm),
[§52](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/texte/etcs/germ/afries/brokmrx/brokm068.htm)),
and doubled plural after *inna*
([§160](https://titus.uni-frankfurt.de/texte/etcs/germ/afries/brokmrx/brokm175.htm)).
The latter reads *fennem*, parallel to *medum*, where Hofmann
reports *fennen* and *meden*. The editions agree on stem
doubling, not on the exact plural spelling; the manuscript
readings in Buma's 1949 apparatus were not independently verified.
Hofmann additionally notes B1 *fenna* once (Buma p. 182);
its status remains uncertain. The Frisian word here means
‘pasture’, with demonstrative *thi*, not ‘marsh’ with
independently attested neuter gender.

**What the comparison tests.** Hofmann explains the alternation
as inherited singleton direct *fani* beside protected geminated
inflections, followed by gender and semantic reclassification.
The Essen gloss and regional vowel evidence make inheritance
more plausible than a Frisian spelling accident alone. An early,
case-secure neuter singleton plus a protected double in the same
variety would strengthen this case; evidence that the singleton
arose only after reclassification, dialect mixing or secondary
shortening would weaken it. Hofmann (pp. 105–108) also discusses
extension of doubled stems and later geographical redistribution.
The evidence supports the possibility of a regional inherited
singleton, not a uniform Continental pattern or an independent
date for English nasal loss. English [*fen(n)*](https://bosworthtoller.com/010296)
and dative *on fenne* are the relevant English comparanda;
simplex *fen/fenn* alone does not resolve earlier nasal quantity.

**English probes.** A first removes the nasal ending from *fánją*
and vocalizes the stranded glide (*fanj → fani*); English
umlaut and unstressed lowering give *fene*. B2 first doubles
*n* in *fannją*, then gives *fenni → fenn → fen*, with high-vowel
loss and final simplification. Both give protected dative
*fánjai → fenne* and reconstructed genitive *fánjas → fennes*.
Both give *fen* from reconstructed direct plural *fánjō*;
this is a phonological probe, not a verified English plural.
No Continental form served as an English target. **Defer**
catalogue admission pending securely parsed English cells and
better control of gender and spelling; retain the traces.

## Wicg: separate quantity from palatalization

Ringe and Taylor (2014, p. 213) reconstruct a light neuter
*wigja-* from the English and comparative forms, including
Old Saxon genitive plural *wiggeo*, Old Frisian *widze* and
Old Norse *vigg*. Their direct *wigi* retains a singleton;
the protected inflections have a doubled palatal stem. The
neuter direct input is *wígją*; *wígjaz* is only a separate
oral-ending negative, not a rival attested nominative.
The contrast to test is whether a double exists **before**
velar palatalization, not whether an orthographic *cg*
can be made to match. A securely inherited singleton direct
form or evidence of separate direct-stem replacement would
favour the A history; an independently demonstrated direct
geminate not supplied by an inflection would favour B2.

*Elene* 1196 has direct accusative *þæt wicg* after the
poem has mentioned a king's *mearh* with bit and rings:
the horse, not *bridels*, is the object of *byrð*. The
neuter demonstrative supports the English gender without
settling the inherited stem quantity. *Beowulf* 234 and 286
have *wicge* as a riding instrumental and after *on*,
respectively; 1045 has genitive plural
*wicga* with the numeral for eight. Those witnesses and
Old Saxon *wiggeo* support the protected stem independently.
Oblique-to-direct transfer is possible under A and does not
require a special sound law; the existence of a donor
does not itself demonstrate transfer.

**English probes.** A loses *ą* first, giving *wigj → wigi*.
The singleton *g* palatalizes to a fricative, which merges with
the glide; the result is *wiġe*. B2 first gives *wiggją*, then
*wiggi*; velar palatalization acts on an existing double,
giving doubled palatal affricate notation before high-vowel
apocope and surface *wiġġ*. This normalized output compares
with written *wicg*, not a second attestation. In both models
protected *wígjai, wígjas, wígjô* geminate before palatalization
and give *wiġġe, wiġġes, wiġġa*. The genitive singular is a
prediction only. The oral negative *wígjaz* gives *wiġe* in
both; it loses oral *a* before gemination. Retain *wicg* as
a **permanent unscored control**: it tests both routes through
palatalization, but the evidence for a protected donor makes
the attested direct form insufficient to settle the chronology.

## Hay: no secure Model A selection

Hecht's *Dialogues* p. 36, ll. 2 and 13, gives direct
*heig*/*hig* with *heg* in the apparatus; *mid hig* attests
oblique use but not the overt ending of the protected dative.
The bounded follow-up checked the *hī/hīg* dictionary entries,
Toller's supplement and variant queries for *heg*, *heig*,
*hige* and possible *-es* forms. Compounds with *hēg/hīg*
and *hege* ‘hedge’ are not witnesses for an inflected simplex
‘hay’. Paradigm tables predict *hīeġe* and *hīeġes*;
they do not attest either spelling. Thus A's inherited
*hæwi* → experimental *hei* and the independently modelled
protected stem remain research traces. No protected target
is selected. B2's direct *hīeġ* is a normalized comparison
to the direct witnesses, not their diplomatic spelling.

## Pledge and the shared plural ending

*Pledge* permanently replicates the light dental environment without
a catalogue row. Both cases of direct *wádją* yield A *wede* and
B2 *wedd* (Ælfric, Thorpe II p. 272, ll. 6–8, subject *wedd*,
object *wed*). The protected forms give *wedde* (Genesis 17:13)
and *weddes* (Genesis 9:12). These are independent lexical
witnesses, but no independent proof of which stem the direct form
inherits.

Both models derive *-em* from ō-stem *-ōmaz/-ōmiz*, against the
usual Old English dative plural *-um*. Analogical extension of
*-um* from other declensions is plausible, not demonstrated.
Ringe and Taylor (2014, pp. 57–58, 115) discuss early *i*-loss
and the dative–instrumental merger. In contrast, the separate
*bádjamiz* probe does yield attested *beddum*: the shorter vowel
undergoes raising before *m*. The probe uses the model's
inherited *-amiz* representation, not a reconstruction abstracted
from *Beowulf* alone. No special *-em → -um* change is warranted.
