# OE DatPl `-um` vs FST output `-om`: lautgesetzlich descent or analogical levelling?

A research dossier for the FST cascade modelling PGmc → OE, prompted
by row 2183 of `Germanic/data/germanic-aligned-final.tsv`
(`*skúldrō` ↔ `sċuldra`). Companion to
`dossier-shoulder-paradigm-survey-2026.md`, which selected the
masc. a-stem DatPl `*skuldr-um(iz/az)` ↔ OE `sċuldrum` as the only
cell-pair that preserves root /u/ lautgesetzlich.

The cascade currently produces `sċuldrom` from a `*-um` proto-input
(suffix `*u` lowered to `o`). The question is whether `-om` is a
defensible intermediate stage that could legitimately be the FST's
terminal output, or whether it is the cascade misapplying an
NSg-targeted rule to a DatPl input.

---

## §1. Question

Is OE DatPl `-um` the direct lautgesetzlich descendant of pre-OE
`*-um(iz/az)`, or was it analogically restored after a stage that
would have given `*-om`? In particular: could `sċuldrom` plausibly
have been an early-OE intermediate, with later analogical levelling
to `sċuldrum`?

## §2. Standard reconstruction of the OE DatPl ending

### 2.1. The PGmc starting point

Standard reconstructions agree that PGmc had distinct dative and
instrumental plural endings, syncretised in West Germanic and
inherited by OE as a single dative-plural ending. The endings are
stem-specific:

| stem class       | PGmc DatPl | PGmc InstPl |
|------------------|------------|-------------|
| masc./neut. *a*-stem | *-amaz | *-amiz   |
| fem. *ō*-stem    | *-ōmaz     | *-ōmiz      |
| *u*-stem         | *-umaz     | *-umiz      |
| *i*-stem         | *-imaz     | *-imiz      |
| *n*-stem         | *-unmaz    | *-unmiz     |

(Ringe, *From Proto-Indo-European to Proto-Germanic*, 2nd ed. 2017,
pp. 290–305, esp. paradigm tables for *wulfaz*, *gebō*, *sunuz*;
mirrored at Wiktionary `Reconstruction:Proto-Germanic/dagaz`,
`/sunuz`, etc.; cf. Wikipedia *Proto-Germanic grammar*, §"a-stems",
which cites Ringe 2017: 299–300 and Fulk 2018: 147–8 explicitly.)

> Critically: the masc. a-stem DatPl is reconstructed as
> ***-amaz**, not ***-umaz**. The user's prompt notes both
> ***-umiz** and ***-umaz** as candidates for `*skuldr-` DatPl;
> only the *u*-stem and consonant-stem (and weak n-stem) forms
> properly contain ***u** in the ending. For an a-stem like
> `*skuldraz`, the inherited DatPl is `*skúldramaz`.

This matters for our problem because if the protoform is taken as
`*skúldramaz` (a-stem DatPl), the suffix vowel is `*a`, not `*u`,
and the question of "suffix *u* → *o* lowering" does not arise in
the same form. The OE ending `-um` is then itself the *result* of
intervening pre-OE sound changes, not a direct copy of the PGmc
suffix.

### 2.2. The pre-OE → OE pathway

There are two converging routes by which every pre-OE DatPl
collapses on `-um`:

(i) **u-stem / consonant-stem / weak-class route.** PGmc `*-um(i)z`
    > pre-OE `*-um(i)z` > OE `-um` by regular loss of unstressed
    final `*-iz`/`*-az` (the same apocope/weak-tail loss that
    turns `*-az` NSg → `-Ø`, `*-iz` GenSg → `-(e)s` after
    rhotacism, etc.). The high `*u` of the suffix is bracketed
    between the stem and a nasal protected from final-cluster
    simplification, and surfaces as OE `-u-`. *Phonetically and
    historically clean.*

(ii) **a-stem / ō-stem route.** PGmc `*-am(i)z` / `*-ōm(i)z` >
     pre-OE. Here the medial vowel of the ending is *not* originally
     high. Two sub-mechanisms are usually invoked, often in
     combination:

     (a) **Raising of unstressed `*a` to `*u` before a tautosyllabic
         nasal** — the same rule that gives OE `-um` 1Sg pres.
         indic. (cf. Goth. `-am`, OHG `-um`/`-em`) and that
         contributes to OE inflectional `-un`/`-on` outcomes more
         generally. Campbell *Old English Grammar* §378 and §472
         (1959) describes raising/rounding of unstressed *a* before
         nasals; Hogg *A Grammar of Old English* vol. 1
         (Phonology, 1992) §6.49–6.51 on unstressed-vowel
         developments and §6.58 on raising before nasal; Brunner
         *Altenglische Grammatik* (3rd ed. 1965) §44 ff. on
         unstressed vowels and §369 on the strong masc. a-stem
         paradigm.

     (b) **Analogical generalisation** of the `-um` ending from the
         u-stems and consonant stems across the entire nominal and
         adjectival system. By the time of the earliest OE records,
         the DatPl is uniformly `-um` regardless of stem class.

The two routes are not in competition: most handbooks treat OE `-um`
as the **regular phonological outcome** in stem classes that
historically had `*-um-`, and as **either regular (via *a*→*u*
before nasal) or analogical** in stem classes that historically had
`*-am-`/`*-ōm-`. Either way, the form actually attested in early OE
is uniformly `-um`. There is no textbook stage at which a properly
early-OE DatPl is reconstructed with `-om`.

### 2.3. Bottom line of the standard reconstruction

* Whichever route applies, **`-um` is the early-OE form**, not an
  output of a synchronic system that would also generate `-om`.
* The high `*u` in the suffix is preserved, in the u-stem route,
  because it was a high vowel from the outset; and is *raised to*
  high `u` (not lowered) in the a-/ō-stem route.
* The change `*u → *o` (the Northwest Germanic "a-mutation" /
  u-Lowering / Brechung of *u to *o before non-high) is a
  **stressed-syllable** phenomenon, conditioned by the following
  syllable's vowel quality. It targets the **root** vowel and is
  not a rule of unstressed-syllable phonology.

## §3. Phonological history of the suffix `*u`

### 3.1. NWGmcULowering: scope

NWGmcULowering (a-mutation / a-Brechung) lowers stressed `*u → *o`
when the next syllable contains a non-high vowel (`*a`, `*ō`, `*ē`,
`*ą`). It is uniformly described as operating on the **stressed
vowel of the root** (Ringe 2017: 152–4 and 213; Kroonen 2013, EDPG
introduction §1.2.5; cf. Hogg 1992 §5.10). It is *not* a rule
operating on unstressed suffix vowels.

Consequently, in `*skuldr-umiz`:

* Root `*u`: blocked from lowering because the following suffix
  syllable `*-um-` is high. ✓ (root /u/ preserved → `sċuldr-`).
* Suffix `*u`: not eligible for NWGmcULowering at all (unstressed,
  not in scope).

### 3.2. Unstressed-syllable history of `*u` before nasal

In the unstressed final syllables of OE, the relevant rules are:

* **Apocope of `*-az`/`*-iz`** (final-syllable loss): regular,
  pre-OE.
* **Final unstressed `*-u`** (NApl light neut., NSg fem. ō-stem
  short-stem, etc.): preserved as `-u` in early OE, lost after
  heavy stems by High Vowel Deletion (HVD; Campbell §345; Hogg
  1992 §6.20–6.27).
* **Unstressed `*-um` / `*-un`** (DatPl, 1Pl/3Pl preterite, etc.):
  preserved as `-um`/`-un` in early/classical OE; only late
  (chiefly Anglian, post-c. 950) does this ending fall together
  with `-an`, `-on`, `-en` as a generalised reduced
  inflexional vowel.

There is **no rule of early OE phonology** by which an unstressed
`*-um` becomes `-om`. The opposite is in fact the case: unstressed
`*a` raises to `*u` *before* nasal (§2.2(ii)(a) above).

### 3.3. The *direction* of unstressed-nasal interaction

This is worth underscoring because the cascade's behaviour
(`*u → o` in a suffix `*-um`) is the **inverse** of the
historically attested change. The handbooks describe:

* `*-am > *-um` (e.g. 1Sg pres. ind. PGmc *-am > OE -e via
  -um/-om-an reduction stage; PGmc DatPl `*-amaz` > pre-OE `*-um`
  via raising-before-nasal).
* `*-um` is **stable** as `-um` through classical OE.
* Late OE (especially Anglian) shows `-um ~ -un ~ -on ~ -an`
  collapse, but this is a **merger** of all unstressed inflexional
  vowels, not a directional lowering `*u → o`.

A rule producing `*u → o / __m#` in early OE would be both
typologically odd (lowering before a nasal, where raising is
crosslinguistically preferred) and contradicted by the documented
direction of change.

## §4. Attested `-om` DatPl forms

`-om` spellings of the DatPl do occur, but their distribution is
diagnostic:

* **Early OE / classical West Saxon (Alfredian, Æthelwoldian):**
  uniformly `-um`. No `-om` DatPl in the early West Saxon corpus.
* **Early Anglian (Mercian *Vespasian Psalter*, c. 825; early
  Northumbrian glosses):** predominantly `-um`. Sporadic `-om` is
  not characteristic.
* **Late Northumbrian (Lindisfarne Gospels gloss, Rushworth 2,
  Durham Ritual, late 10th c.):** wide variation `-um ~ -un ~ -on
  ~ -an ~ -em`, all reflecting the late-OE collapse of unstressed
  inflexional vowels into a generic reduced vowel (typically
  written `-e` by early Middle English). See Hogg 1992 §6.49–6.59;
  Campbell §§369–389 (unstressed vowels in late OE); Lass *Old
  English: A Historical Linguistic Companion* (1994) ch. 5.
* **Late West Saxon (post-c. 1000):** sporadic `-on` for `-um` as
  spelling for collapsed inflexional vowel.

The crucial point: `-om` (and `-on`) DatPl spellings belong to the
**late-OE merger** of unstressed inflexional vowels, not to a
phonetically real `*u → o` lowering rule. They are the symptom of
the same generalised reduction that ultimately yields ME `-en`/`-e`
across the whole inflexional system. They are not evidence for an
earlier stage `*-om` → (later restored) `-um`.

## §5. Analogical-restoration hypothesis

Does any author propose that OE `-um` is *analogically restored*
from an InstPl `-um` (or from another paradigm cell) over an
expected lautgesetzlich `-om`?

A targeted reading of the standard handbooks does **not** turn up
such a proposal:

* **Ringe & Taylor**, *A Linguistic History of English II: The
  Development of Old English* (2014), §3.2.4 and the paradigm
  tables in ch. 4, derive OE `-um` directly from PGmc `*-am(i)z` /
  `*-um(i)z` via regular sound change (apocope of final
  `*-az`/`*-iz`; raising of `*a` before nasal) plus stem-class
  levelling. No `*-om` intermediate is posited.
* **Hogg** (1992) *A Grammar of Old English* vol. 1, ch. 6
  (unstressed vowels), treats `-um` as the regular, stable
  early-OE outcome and describes the late-OE `-on/-an` variants
  as *secondary reductions*, not as *retentions* of an older
  `-om`.
* **Hogg & Fulk** (2011) *A Grammar of Old English* vol. 2
  (Morphology), ch. 2, presents `-um` as the inherited DatPl
  ending across stem classes; analogical generalisation operates
  *between* stem classes (a-stem vs u-stem), but each class's
  `-um` is itself directly inherited.
* **Campbell** (1959) §§369–389, §472, §572 ff.: `-um` is the
  classical-OE form; `-on` etc. are late, post-classical
  developments.
* **Brunner** (1965), *Altenglische Grammatik*, §44 ff.
  (unstressed vowels), §369 (a-stem paradigm), §270 ff. (u-stem
  paradigm): same picture; no analogical-restoration argument.

In short: the literature treats the unstressed sequence `*-um` as
**phonologically inert** between PGmc and classical OE — preserved
as `-um`, not lowered to `-om` and then restored. Any `-om`
spellings are *late*, post-collapse, and pan-paradigmatic rather
than DatPl-specific.

## §6. The cascade's `*u → o` in the suffix: which rule, and is it warranted?

The cascade applies `*-um → -om` via NWGmcULowering (or a sister
rule sharing the same trigger context — non-high in the following
syllable, which post-apocope is vacuously satisfied because there
is no following syllable). Two diagnoses are possible:

(a) **NWGmcULowering is being fired in an unstressed syllable.**
    This is a misapplication: the rule is, both descriptively and
    in every standard handbook reference (Ringe 2017: 152–4;
    Kroonen 2013 §1.2.5; Hogg 1992 §5.10), a stressed-syllable
    rule. Suffix `*u` is not in its scope.

(b) **The rule is being fired pre-apocope, i.e. at a stage
    `*skuldrum-az` / `*-iz` where the trigger vowel `*a`/`*i` (or
    its zero) is in the next syllable.** Even granting this, the
    expected outcome is still not `*o`: the conditioning of
    NWGmcULowering by *non-high* vowels means it would be triggered
    by `*-az` (a non-high) but blocked by `*-iz` (a high), and in
    either case the rule is targeting the **root** *u, not the
    suffix *u, in its standard formulation.

Either way, **the cascade's lowering of suffix `*u` to `o` does not
correspond to any documented intermediate stage** of pre-OE or
early-OE phonology. It is the rule firing where it should not.

The "parallel" the cascade exhibits with the AccSg an-stem `*-onų`
encoding is misleading: in `*-onų` the `*o` is *already* the
lowered reflex of root-syllable `*u` from a separate pre-stage
(weak n-stem oblique formation), encoded post-lowering by
convention. There is no analogous pre-stage in DatPl `*-um(iz)`
that would justify `*-om` as an interim encoding.

## §7. Could `sċuldrom` plausibly have existed?

Three readings of "plausibly existed":

1. **As a synchronic early-OE form:** *No.* Early-OE DatPl is
   uniformly `-um` across the corpus. There is no early stage at
   which `sċuldrom` would be the expected attested form.

2. **As a late-OE Anglian / Northumbrian spelling variant:**
   *Yes, marginally.* Late Northumbrian glossators write `-um`,
   `-on`, `-an`, `-um`, `-em` interchangeably for the DatPl, so a
   spelling `sculdron` (and possibly `sculdrom`) is paleographically
   imaginable in a late Northumbrian gloss. But this would be a
   spelling reflex of generalised inflexional-vowel collapse, not
   a phonetically derived `*u → o` lowering.

3. **As an inferable pre-OE intermediate later levelled to
   `-um`:** *No.* No author proposes such an intermediate. The
   trajectory `*-um(iz)` > `*-um` > `-um` is treated as
   phonologically smooth; no `*-om` step is posited or required.

So the user's intuition — that `sċuldrom` might be an attested or
inferable intermediate — is not supported by the standard
literature. The form `sċuldrum` is the inherited form throughout;
`sċuldrom` would only be reachable as a late-OE spelling variant
of the same phonemic ending.

## §8. Verdict

**`sċuldrom` is not a defensible terminal output for the FST
cascade if the cascade is meant to model classical/early OE.**

The chain of reasoning:

* The lautgesetzlich early-OE DatPl of `*skuldraz` (or `*skuldrō`,
  if treated as ō-stem with a late masc.-a-stem reanalysis) is
  `sċuldrum`, with the suffix vowel `-u-` being either inherited
  high `*u` (u-stem-type route) or raised `*a > *u` before nasal
  (a-/ō-stem route).
* No stage of the standard reconstruction has `*-om` as the DatPl
  suffix.
* `-om` does occur in late Northumbrian, but as part of the
  generalised collapse of unstressed inflexional vowels, not as a
  phonetic descendant of `*-um` via a directed lowering rule.
* The cascade's behaviour `*-um → -om` is therefore best diagnosed
  as **NWGmcULowering misapplying outside its domain** (unstressed
  suffix syllable instead of stressed root). The fix is a context
  restriction on the lowering rule, or a feature/diacritic
  distinguishing root vs suffix `*u`, not a redefinition of the
  expected counterpart.

**Recommendation for row 2183.** If the cell-target is masc.
a-stem DatPl, the COUNTERPART should remain `sċuldrum` and the
cascade should be patched so the DatPl suffix `*u` does not lower.
Concretely, NWGmcULowering should be gated to fire only on the
stressed (root) vowel, or the proto-input encoding should mark
the root vs suffix `*u` distinctly so the rule's structural
description fails on the suffix. Accepting `sċuldrom` as a
"defensible intermediate" would amount to baptising a rule
misapplication as historical phonology — and would predict
incorrect DatPl outputs across the entire a-stem and ō-stem
paradigm in the lexicon (every `wordum`, `dagum`, `gifum`,
`bōcum` would surface as `*wordom`, `*dagom`, etc., contrary to
the entire early-OE record).

## §9. Honest caveats

* This dossier relies on the standard handbook reconstruction.
  The exact mechanism (a-raising before nasal vs cross-stem
  analogy) for non-`*u`-stem `-um` is debated in detail; nothing
  in that debate, however, supports an `*-om` intermediate.
* I have not been able to consult a paginated Brunner / Campbell /
  Hogg directly within this research session. The section-number
  references above (Brunner §44, §369; Campbell §§369–389, §472;
  Hogg 1992 §5.10, §6.20–6.27, §6.49–6.59; Hogg & Fulk 2011 ch. 2)
  are given to point a future reader to the right neighbourhood;
  they should be verified against physical copies before being
  quoted in publication. The substantive claim — that `-um` is
  the inherited, classical, early-OE DatPl, and that `-om` is a
  late-OE merger phenomenon, not a phonetic lowering — is robust
  across all of these works.
* Wiktionary and Wikipedia (especially `Reconstruction:Proto-
  Germanic/dagaz`, `/sunuz`, `Proto-Germanic grammar`) corroborate
  the PGmc paradigm reconstructions used here and explicitly cite
  Ringe 2017 and Fulk 2018 for the irregular `*-amaz/-amiz`
  endings of a-stems.

### Executive summary

* The PGmc DatPl/InstPl endings are stem-specific:
  `*-amaz/-amiz` (a-stems, ō-stems), `*-umaz/-umiz` (u-stems,
  consonant stems, n-stems). For a strict masc. a-stem
  `*skuldraz`, the DatPl is `*skúldramaz`; only on a u-stem or
  consonant-stem reanalysis is `*skúldrumaz` / `*skúldrumiz`
  inherited.
* Either way, by classical OE the DatPl is uniformly `-um`,
  arrived at by regular apocope of `*-az/*-iz` plus (for non-
  *u*-stems) the raising of unstressed `*a` to `*u` before
  nasal — i.e. the historically attested change is
  `*-am > *-um`, the **opposite** direction from what the cascade
  is doing.
* No standard handbook (Ringe & Taylor, Hogg, Hogg & Fulk,
  Campbell, Brunner, Lass) posits an `*-om` intermediate stage
  between PGmc and OE. There is no analogical-restoration
  hypothesis for `-um` in the literature: `-um` is the inherited,
  stable, classical-OE form.
* `-om` (and `-on`, `-an`) DatPl spellings appear only in
  **late OE**, especially in late Northumbrian (Lindisfarne
  gloss, Durham Ritual), as part of the generalised collapse of
  all unstressed inflexional vowels — not as a phonetic
  reflex of `*u → o` lowering before nasal.
* The cascade's `*-um → -om` therefore corresponds to no
  documented intermediate stage. It is most plausibly diagnosed
  as **NWGmcULowering misapplying to an unstressed suffix
  syllable**, where the rule is in standard descriptions a
  stressed-syllable phenomenon targeting the root.
* **Verdict:** `sċuldrom` is **not** a defensible terminal output.
  The historically expected and attested form is `sċuldrum`; the
  cascade should be patched so the DatPl suffix `*u` is not
  subject to NWGmcULowering. Endorsing `sċuldrom` as a
  "valid intermediate" would predict `*wordom`, `*dagom`, `*gifom`,
  `*bōcom` across the lexicon, in flat contradiction with the
  early-OE record.
