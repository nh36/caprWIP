### Reconstruction and comparative evidence

Kroonen cites the noun under stem-level `*burdi-` and gives Old English `(ge-)byrd` among the reflexes [@Kroonen2013]. The selected input `*búrdiz` is the nominative-style form that stands behind that stem label.

### Old English evidence

Clark Hall and Bosworth-Toller both attest simplex `byrd` as an Old English noun meaning 'birth' [@ClarkHall1960; @BosworthToller1898]. The prefixed form `gebyrd` is also well established in the tradition: Kroonen lists `(ge-)byrd`, Bosworth-Toller has a separate `ge-byrd` entry, and Campbell cites `gebyrd` and `gebyrdu` in his grammatical discussion [@Kroonen2013; @BosworthToller1898; @Campbell1959].

### Form note

The relevant comparator here is the simplex noun `byrd`. The prefixed forms remain related attested material within the same lexical family, and Hogg's discussion of deverbal feminines provides the broader derivational setting [@Hogg1992].

### Development to Old English

From `*búrdiz`, loss of final `z` gives `*búrdi`. I-umlaut fronts `u` to `y`, and high-vowel apocope then yields `byrd`. The result is the ordinary simplex Old English noun.
