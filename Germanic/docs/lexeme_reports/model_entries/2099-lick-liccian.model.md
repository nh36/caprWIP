# lick — OE liccian

PROTO: *líkkōjaną
PROTOFORM: *líkkōjaną
COUNTERPART: liccian
DERIVATION_CLASS: regular

### Comparative stem and selected infinitive

Old English [`liccian`]{.iv lang=oe sort=liccian role=target_form} 'lick' is a weak verb with a medial geminate in the normalized citation form. Old Saxon *likkon* and Old High German *lecchon* support the consonantal comparison. Ringe and Taylor reconstruct a West Germanic class-II stem; Orel gives the longer citation formation [likkōjanan]{.recon} ‘lick’. CAPR's selected infinitive explicitly represents that longer suffix, not the finite ending [@RingeTaylor2014, p. 50; @Orel2003, p. 285; @BosworthToller1898, p. 614].

### The Vespasian finite comparison

The Vespasian Psalter has [`liciað`]{.iv lang=oe sort=liciath role=evidence_form} 'lick' glossing Latin *lingent*, with ‘his enemies’ as plural subject. The token is third-person plural, with one written *c*; it is neither the selected infinitive nor the separately modelled third singular *liccaþ* ‘licks’. The spelling is evidence for *i* in the weak suffix, not proof that the consonant was historically a singleton or that the whole finite token should be emended to *licciað* [@Sweet1885, p. 286 (Ps. 71:9); @RingeTaylor2014, p. 228].

All three orders derive \emph{*líkkōjaną} > *liccian*. The model retains the geminate through SC083, which removes the first vowel of its weak *e.i* representation. The finite third singular instead has *-aþ* and does not go through that sequence [@Campbell1959, §356.4; @RingeTaylor2014, pp. 80, 228].
