### Reconstruction and comparative evidence

The reconstruction of the Germanic word is not uniform in the handbooks. Orel gives **`*skuldr(j)ō`**, a feminine ō-/jō-stem, and explicitly notes that Old English `sculdor` is masculine beside OFrisian `skulder`, Middle Low German `schulder`, and Old High German `scultra, scultirra` [@Orel2003, p. 345]. Kroonen instead reconstructs **`*skuldra-`**, a masculine a-stem, and treats the Old High German feminine forms as secondary developments from **`*skuldrjōn-`** [@Kroonen2013, p. 478]. Ringe and Taylor then cite **PWGmc `*skuldru`** for the Old English branch [@RingeTaylor2014, p. 142].

The disagreement is not merely graphic. Orel's feminine ō-/jō-stem, Kroonen's masculine a-stem, and Ringe and Taylor's shortened West Germanic branch form imply different paradigms and different expectations for the Old English inflection. The problem is therefore not only what the lexical headword was, but which inflectional cell best explains the Old English evidence.

For the present derivation, the crucial form is neither Orel's citation form nor Kroonen's masculine lemma nor Ringe and Taylor's shortened branch form in isolation, but a dative/instrumental plural input **`*skúldramiz`**. Such an input is morphologically compatible with the inherited plural ending that later yields Old English `-um`, and it is the only form in the comparison below that can be matched directly with an attested Old English paradigm form.

### Old English evidence

The ordinary Old English headword is **`sculdor`**. Bosworth-Toller and Clark Hall both lemmatize `sculdor` as the normal dictionary form, and the Bosworth-Toller material also preserves plural and oblique forms such as **`sculdru`**, **`sculdra`**, and **`sculdrum`** [@BosworthToller1898; @ClarkHall1960]. The form relevant for the present derivation is the dative plural **`sculdrum`**, which is directly attested in the dictionary material [@BosworthToller1898].

The singular evidence does not point in only one direction. Bosworth-Toller's Supplement records a weak-feminine **`sculdra, an`**, so `sculdra` belongs to the Old English lexical record, but it stands beside rather than instead of the stronger masculine paradigm headed by `sculdor` [@BosworthToller1898]. Brunner and Luick also record later spellings such as **`sceoldor`** and the i-mutated dative plural **`scyldrum`**, which show secondary phonological and analogical reshaping within Old English rather than the inherited baseline alone [@SieversBrunner1965, §92.2.a; @Luick1914, p. 230].

The comparison form needed here is therefore not the ordinary singular lemma by itself, nor the later weak-feminine doublet by itself, but the attested dative plural **`sċuldrum`**. The spelling with `sċ-` is a normalized representation of the same Old English initial cluster.

### Development to Old English

The selected input **`*skúldramiz`** is interpreted as a Proto-Germanic dative/instrumental plural form. In this environment the post-tonic `a` before `m` is raised to `u`, giving a form of the **`*skúldrumiz`** type. The crucial point is that unstressed `u` is regularly preserved before `m`, especially in the dative plural ending `-um`: Campbell states this explicitly, and Hogg formulates the same condition for the dative plural inflexion [@Campbell1959, §373; @Hogg1992, §3.3.1.3]. Brunner's wording points in the same direction by excluding `m` from the environments in which medial `o` became general in West Saxon [@SieversBrunner1965, §44 Anm. 7].

Subsequent reduction of the ending removes the final `*i` and `*z`, so that the inflectional ending appears in Old English as **`-um`**. The initial cluster is written here as **`sċ-`**, and the full development is thus `*skúldramiz > *skúldrumiz > *skúldrum > sċuldrum`. The transducer confirms that sequence by deriving `sċuldrum` from `*skúldramiz`.

### Conclusion

The Germanic word for ‘shoulder’ is reconstructed in different ways by the handbooks, and the Old English evidence itself preserves both a strong masculine paradigm headed by `sculdor` and a secondary weak-feminine doublet `sculdra`. The singular headword therefore does not by itself settle the inherited development. The dative plural **`sċuldrum`**, however, is explained directly by a Proto-Germanic datative/instrumental plural input **`*skúldramiz`**. For that reason the present entry treats `sċuldrum` as a regular paradigm-cell outcome, while the lexical history as a whole remains analogically disturbed.
