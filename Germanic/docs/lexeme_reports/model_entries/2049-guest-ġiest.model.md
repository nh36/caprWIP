### Reconstruction and comparative evidence

Campbell and Ringe-Taylor treat the noun as an ordinary i-stem whose West Saxon development shows palatal diphthongization, while non-West-Saxon evidence preserves forms of the [`gest`]{.iv lang=oe sort=gest role=comparison_form} 'guest' type [@Campbell1959; @RingeTaylor2014].

### Old English evidence

Bosworth-Toller and Clark Hall record the word under forms such as [`gist`]{.iv lang=oe sort=gist role=comparison_form} 'guest', [`gest`]{.iv lang=oe sort=gest role=comparison_form} 'guest', [`giest`]{.iv lang=oe sort=giest role=comparison_form} 'guest', and [`gyst`]{.iv lang=oe sort=gyst role=comparison_form} 'guest' [@BosworthToller1898; @ClarkHall1960]. The selected target [`ġiest`]{.iv lang=oe sort=giest role=target_form} 'guest' is the normalized West Saxon form within that attested family.

### Development to Old English

From [gástiz]{.recon} ‘guest’, Anglo-Frisian brightening gives a [`gæst-`]{.iv lang=oe sort=gaest role=comparison_form} 'guest' stage, and i-mutation affects the front vowel before the lost high-vocalic ending. In West Saxon the initial palatal environment then produces [`ie`]{.iv lang=oe sort=ie role=comparison_form} 'guest', so the regular outcome is [`ġiest`]{.iv lang=oe sort=giest role=target_form} 'guest' [@Campbell1959; @RingeTaylor2014].
