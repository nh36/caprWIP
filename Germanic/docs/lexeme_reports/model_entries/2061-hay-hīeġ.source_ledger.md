# Source extraction ledger — hay / hīeġ

## Witnesses and limits

Hecht's readings below are from his two-column text and apparatus, not
fresh manuscript transcriptions. The original translation's association
with Worcester does not assign a dialect to a spelling in a later copy.
The other spellings are dictionary or transcription reports at the
stated level of verification; dictionary accents express editorial
quantity, not an accent newly verified in the manuscript.

No further securely identified token was found in the Mark glosses
checked or in bounded OCR searches of Cockayne's *Leechdoms*, vol. II,
and Liebermann's *Gesetze der Angelsachsen*, vol. I. The two plausible
law-text *hig* hits have feminine antecedents, and apparent *fenum*
is the second half of split *heofenum*. This is not an exhaustive
negative concordance. The DOE Web Corpus was not available for direct query.
Exclude homographs
for ‘hedge’, the plural pronoun *hig*, and translations by *gærs* from
the evidence for this noun.

Ringe and Taylor (2014: 53, 173, 246) contrast the inherited direct
*hawi* and the protected geminated stem; p. 173 identifies the
source of Old High German *houwi* as the oblique stem, not one named
case in English. Campbell (1959: 46 §120.2; 231 §579(4)) gives the
labial development and invokes the other cases collectively. The
English evidence does not identify an inherited genitive or plural
capable of replacing the selected direct cell. Wright (1914:135–136,
§270 and note; cf. §357) suggests loss of intervocalic inherited *j*
after a long nucleus, insertion of a later consonantal glide in
hiatus, and extension of that *g* from inflected forms to the
nominative. Ringe and Taylor derive consonantal *j* on the protected
route itself and extend the stem, not just an inserted segment.
Wright prints genitive *hīeges* and dative *hīege* as paradigm
examples without manuscript citations, not as identified tokens;
their independent attestation is unverified.

The model contrast is phonological: A's final *i* is the short vowel
created by stranded-glide vocalization and remains syllabic after
loss of *w*. B2's *īe* is a long nucleus, and its final *ġ* is
consonantal /j/. The reported spellings cannot turn A's token into
either a diphthongal offglide or a consonant. The English historical
analyses support long West Saxon *īe*, Anglian *ē* and a palatal
consonant, not A's short /e.i/. This is evidence against equivalence,
not proof that an unlevelled English singleton never existed.
