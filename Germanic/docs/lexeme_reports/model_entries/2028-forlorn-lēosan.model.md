### Reconstruction and comparative evidence

Kroonen reconstructs the verb under `*leusan-` and cites prefixed daughters such as Gothic `fra-liusan` 'forlorn' and Old English [`forlēosan`]{.iv lang=oe sort=forleosan role=target_form} 'forlorn'; Orel likewise gives Old English [`for-leósan`]{.iv lang=oe sort=forleosan role=comparison_form} 'forlorn' [@Kroonen2013; @Orel2003]. The inherited verbal base is therefore clear, though the daughter set often appears with the prefix.

### Old English evidence

The direct Old English evidence behind English `forlorn` 'forlorn' lies in the prefixed verb [`forlēosan`]{.iv lang=oe sort=forleosan role=target_form} 'forlorn' and especially in the participle [`forloren`]{.iv lang=oe sort=forloren role=comparison_form} 'forlorn', recorded by Ringe and Taylor and in the dictionaries [@RingeTaylor2014; @ClarkHall1960; @BosworthToller1898]. The simplex infinitive [`lēosan`]{.iv lang=oe sort=leosan role=comparison_form} 'forlorn' represents the verbal base itself.

### Form note

As a base-form comparison, the simplex infinitive is [`lēosan`]{.iv lang=oe sort=leosan role=comparison_form} 'forlorn', while the English adjective continues the prefixed Old English family [`forlēosan`]{.iv lang=oe sort=forleosan role=target_form} 'forlorn' / [`forloren`]{.iv lang=oe sort=forloren role=comparison_form} 'forlorn' [@RingeTaylor2014].

### Development to Old English

From [léusaną]{.recon} ‘forlorn’, Old English diphthong leveling gives [lēosaną]{.recon} ‘forlorn’, and later nasal apocope and weak-tail reduction yield [`lēosan`]{.iv lang=oe sort=leosan role=comparison_form} 'forlorn' [@RingeTaylor2014]. The prefixed forms follow the same verbal base with added `for-`.
