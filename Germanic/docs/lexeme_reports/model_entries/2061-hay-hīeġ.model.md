# hay — OE hīeġ

PROTO: *xáwją
PROTOFORM: *xáwją
COUNTERPART: hīeġ
DERIVATION_CLASS: regular

### Old English evidence

Hecht's edition of the Old English *Dialogues* prints direct [heig]{.iv lang=oe sort=heig role=comparison_form} ‘hay’; compare [hig]{.iv lang=oe sort=hig role=comparison_form} ‘hay’ and apparatus [heg]{.iv lang=oe sort=heg role=comparison_form} ‘hay’ [@Hecht1900, p. 36, ll. 2, 13]. The normalized comparison is *hīeġ*. The phrase *mid hig* attests oblique use but not a preserved dative ending.

### Development to Old English

B2 retains the nasal ending through gemination of the glide-bearing stem and preserves palatal conditioning on the labial before low-vowel resolution and mutation. With A's early nasal loss, the inherited direct singular instead follows \emph{*hawj → hawi → hæwi}; CAPR extends that singleton route to the experimental endpoint [*hei*]{.pred}, not the manuscript spelling *heig*. Ringe and Taylor distinguish Old High German *hewi* ‘hay’, the inherited direct singular, from *houwi* ‘hay’, with the geminated stem extended from obliques. Their English account likewise distinguishes the singleton route from the protected stem \emph{*hawwj- → hauj- → hēaj-} that gives West Saxon *hīeġ* ‘hay’ and Anglian *hēg* ‘hay’. They do not identify a single English donor case. Campbell similarly explains the direct singular by influence from the other cases [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 53, 173, 246; @Campbell1959, p. 46, §120.2 and p. 231, §579(4)].

Both models produce the protected stem. The *Colloquy* phrase *mid hig* ‘with hay’ identifies dative use, but the endingless spelling does not establish retention of the inherited dative ending or a dative origin for the remodelled stem. Neither a manuscript genitive corresponding to predicted [*hīeġes*]{.pred} nor a securely identified protected plural is established. The inherited direct comparison therefore stays in both denominators: B2 derives the target directly; under A the attested direct stem requires morphological extension, not an additional sound law.
