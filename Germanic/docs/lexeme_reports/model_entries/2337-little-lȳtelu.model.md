### Old English evidence

The Vespasian Psalter gloss has
[lytelu]{.iv lang=oe sort=lytelu role=comparison_form} 'little' in
*netenu lytelu 7 micel* 'small and great animals', glossing Latin *animalia pusilla et magna*
at Psalm 103:25 [@Sweet1885, p. 336]. The adjective agrees with the
neuter plural noun 'animals': this is the strong neuter plural, not
the formally identical feminine nominative singular. CAPR selects the
shared nominative–accusative plural cell; the nominative interpretation
belongs to this clause's enumeration of the creatures there.

The normalized comparator
[lȳtelu]{.iv lang=oe sort=lytelu role=target_form} marks quantity that
Sweet's diplomatic spelling does not mark. Long \emph{ȳ} and the
grammatical identification are independently supported by Fulk's
West Mercian discussion and Goering's comparison
[@Fulk2010, pp. 133--134; @Goering2016, pp. 176--177].
Fulk contrasts the retained medial vowel here with masculine accusative
plural [lȳtle]{.iv lang=oe sort=lytle role=comparison_form} 'little'.
The forms differ in the quantity of the following syllable at deletion,
not in whether a heavy root happens to permit an exception.

### Comparative evidence and the reconstruction problem

The Germanic evidence does not establish a single, unproblematic vowel
correspondence. Three principal groups must be distinguished:

| Evidence | Quantity and formation | Comparative implication |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Gothic *leitils*, Old Norse *lítill* 'little' | long front vowel; the Gothic spelling *ei* represents \emph{ī} | supports \emph{*lītila-}, with nominative masculine \emph{*lītilaz} |
| OE *lȳtel* 'little', including the selected *lȳtelu* | long rounded front vowel, regularly from \emph{*ū} before an umlauting vowel | supports a back-vowel antecedent \emph{*lūtila-}, not \emph{*lītila-} |
| OE *lyttel* 'little', Old Saxon *luttil*, Old High German *luzzil* | short root vowel with a geminate or its High German shifted continuation | requires the history of the short-vowel/geminate formation to be explained separately |

These are comparative dictionary forms, not three identical inflectional
cells or three contemporary manuscript spellings. The Gothic and Norse
forms are masculine citation forms; the directly identified OE plural
has its own evidence above. The grouping follows the dictionaries and
Fulk's explicit contrast, not an assumption that all three groups descend
regularly from one complete word [@Orel2003, p. 248; @Kroonen2013, p. 339;
@Fulk2018, pp. 15, 127].

The distribution is also less tidy than a division into Gothic–Norse
and West Germanic might suggest. Heidermanns records the rarer OE
*lītel* beside *lȳtel*, Middle Dutch *littel* beside *luttel*, Old
Frisian *lītik* beside *littik*, and Old Saxon and Middle Dutch
*luttik*, with a different suffix. His Dutch evidence for *littel* and
*luttel* begins in Middle Dutch, not in an attested Proto-Germanic or
Old Dutch paradigm. He also records Old High German *liuzzil* and
*liuzilu*, together with the abstract *liuzila*, and reports Matzel's
analysis of these as mixtures of the front-vowel and geminate stems.
These spellings cannot simply be assigned to an otherwise uniform
short-\emph{u} series. Nor is written High German *zz* an unmediated
transcription of Germanic \emph{*tt}: Heidermanns specifically identifies
Notker's *zz* here as an affricate
[@Heidermanns1993, pp. 371--372, 390--391]. Lühr additionally lists
Old Saxon *lītil*, but identifies no textual witness in that passage;
Heidermanns' corresponding entry does not list this Old Saxon form.
Its evidential status should therefore remain distinct from that of
the directly identified Mercian plural [@Luehr2017, p. 153;
@Heidermanns1993, pp. 371, 390].

Orel prints the alternatives
[lītilaz]{.iv .recon lang=pgmc sort=litilaz role=source_protoform},
[lūtilaz]{.iv .recon lang=pgmc sort=lutilaz role=source_protoform}
and [luttilaz]{.iv .recon lang=pgmc sort=luttilaz role=source_protoform}
'little' [@Orel2003, p. 248]. The long-\emph{ū} alternative is the
comparative citation retained here. It is not a vowel invented to make
the transducer work: the ordinary fronting of inherited \emph{*ū}
before \emph{*i} accounts for the independently established OE
\emph{ȳ}.

The three alternatives register a comparative problem; the tildes do
not supply derivations connecting them. Orel derives the family from
his \emph{*luttiz ~ *luttjaz}, but this cross-reference alone does not
explain how the long front vowel would arise from that base
[@Orel2003, p. 248].

Kroonen instead heads the entry with
[lītila-]{.iv .recon lang=pgmc sort=litila role=source_protoform}
and explicitly leaves the distribution of long-\emph{ī},
long-\emph{ū} and short-\emph{u}/geminate variants unexplained
[@Kroonen2013, p. 339]. His entry is not quoted as a long-\emph{ū}
headword. His choice gives a representative entry for the Germanic adjective
family, with a front-vowel reconstruction directly supported by Gothic
and Norse. It is not a demonstrated common phonological ancestor of
every reflex listed beneath it: the accompanying argument expressly
withholds that solution. Indeed, Kroonen rejects the comparison with
'stoop' and Irish 'little finger' as a satisfactory etymology of the
whole family precisely because it leaves the front-vowel variant
unaccounted for. Selecting Orel's supported back-vowel alternative
does not settle that objection [@Kroonen2013, p. 339].

### Antonymic formation and the older etymologies

Meillet already denied that Gothic *leitils* and Old High German
*luzzil* could simply be identified with one another. In a discussion
of the unstable naming of 'small', he considered neither adjective
securely etymologized. What he did identify was the influence of the
opposite adjective 'great': Gothic *leitils : mikils*, OE
*lȳtel : micel* 'little : great', and the corresponding High German
and Norse pairs. His Norse comparison extends to the masculine
accusatives *lítenn* and *mikenn* and to shorter adverbial formations.
The claim concerns characteristic endings and morphological
association, not a sound change between the two root vowels
[@Meillet1902, pp. 172--174, especially p. 173].

The distinction matters for the later literature. Melazzo reports
Thurneysen's analysis of a suffix \emph{-lo-} shared by Gothic
*mikils*, *leitils* and High German *luzzil*, within a survey of the
Greek adjective μέγας, μεγάλη, μέγα 'great'. His note explicitly
distinguishes the root vowels of the Germanic 'little' forms.
This is useful evidence for the history of suffixal analysis, not a
new etymology of 'little', and Thurneysen's original discussion has
not been independently consulted here [@Melazzo2014, p. 10, n. 3].
Shared suffixes and the existence of shorter formations give the
antonymic comparison a morphological basis; they do not by themselves
establish the direction or every step of the proposed remodelling.

Krogmann's dedicated study attempts something different: a remote
relationship between two initially distinct root families. He begins
with Germanic \emph{*līt-} and \emph{*leut-}, explicitly not immediately
reconcilable, and associates both 'small' and 'deceitful' with bending,
crouching and lying concealed. Norse *lúta* and OE *lūtan* 'bend down'
anchor the back-vowel comparison. His proposed external relatives
include Baltic words for dejection and Celtic words for smallness;
the transitions from 'bent down' to 'small' and from 'lying concealed'
to 'deceiving' supply the semantic argument
[@Krogmann1935, pp. 44--45].

He does not simply accept every earlier comparison. On pp. 45--46
he rejects both the Greek 'revile/play' comparison and an association
with Latvian 'creep', assigning the latter to the family of 'slide'.
For the front-vowel group he instead invokes Indo-European
\emph{*lei-} 'crouch, diminish', with words for ceasing, weakness and
hunger; for the back-vowel group he uses \emph{*leud-} 'bend'.
Rejecting an unsupported common \emph{*lē-} 'bend', he ultimately
derives the two from the \emph{i}- and \emph{u}-bases
\emph{*elei-} and \emph{*eleu-} of \emph{*el-} 'bend', each with a
dental extension. His conclusion is a distant relationship, not the
regular descent of one Germanic vowel from the other
[@Krogmann1935, pp. 46--48]. The proposed root extensions and the
wide semantic grouping carry that conclusion; the paper does not
provide a complete conditioned history of the three adjective stems,
their vowel quantities and their suffixes.

Heidermanns separates those dimensions more explicitly: the root
vowels, single versus geminate dental, and the suffixes represented
by the Norse, Frisian and West Germanic formations. His explanation
of adjective formation again uses 'great' as a model, with Gothic
*leita* 'a little' and OE *lȳt*, Old Saxon *lūt* 'little, few' as
possible shorter bases. He retains the transmitted Gothic adverb
against its conjectural expansion to an adjective. For the
short-vowel/geminate variants he invokes expressive gemination;
for the deeper etymology he offers only a tentative connection with
'crouch' and acknowledges the lack of close comparisons at the same
formation level [@Heidermanns1993, p. 372]. That is a documented
proposal, not a specified, exceptionless phonological environment.
It cannot be adopted as a sound law merely because the word means
'small'. His separate treatment of High German *luzzi* also leaves
open whether its geminate was inherited or arose on transfer to the
\emph{ja}-class [@Heidermanns1993, pp. 390--391].

### Two roots, or a more remote relationship?

Lühr's alternative is not a new way to turn \emph{ī} into \emph{ū}.
She explicitly denies cognacy between the front- and back-vowel
groups. In her 2017 account the latter belong with 'bend, crouch':
OE *lūtan*, preterite *lēat*, and Norse *lúta*, preterite *laut*.
She compares Lithuanian *liūdnas* 'sad, dejected' with
\emph{*lud-no-}, Welsh *llwdn* 'young animal' with Celtic
\emph{*lud-no-}, and Welsh *lludd* 'weariness' with
\emph{*loud-o-}. The Germanic short-vowel formation is
\emph{*lut-ja-la-}, with a glide-bearing stem before the added
\emph{l}-suffix; OE *lȳtel* instead presupposes
\emph{*lūtila-}. These are distinct formations and grades, not
interchangeable spellings [@Luehr2017, pp. 152--153].

For the front-vowel group she proposes Indo-European
\emph{*leih₂-} 'cease, diminish', with Gothic dative singular
*undarleijin* 'one who is still less than the least' and Greek
λῖμος 'hunger' as comparisons. The proposed Germanic base is
\emph{*lī-ja-}, not an inherited whole adjective
\emph{*lītila-}. Remodelling after \emph{*lūtila-} could supply
the latter's extended formation; influence from \emph{*mekila-}
'great' is another possibility [@Luehr2017, p. 153]. A zero-grade
root with \emph{*i} before a laryngeal makes long \emph{ī}
phonologically intelligible. It does not independently establish
the membership of the Gothic comparison or prove the proposed
suffixal replacement. Lühr herself records a competing etymology
of *undarleijin*, linking it instead with High German *liz*
'pretext' and the Gothic noun for 'hypocrisy'
[@Luehr2017, p. 153, n. 4].

The earlier Hildebrandlied commentary already contains the two-root
analysis and the proposed remodelling. It is considerably fuller:
it considers an original \emph{i}-stem, transfer to a
\emph{ja}-formation to account for the West Germanic geminate,
and subsequent addition of the \emph{l}-suffix. It explicitly
objects that direct gemination before \emph{l} has not been
demonstrated for the required antecedent. For the front-vowel
formation, it also supplies a paradigmatic motive: loss of the
glide before an inflectional \emph{i} could expose a stem
\emph{*lī-}, to which reanalysed \emph{*-ti-} or
\emph{*-tila-} could be attached
[@Luehr1982, II, pp. 382--387 in the author's reissue].
The later article therefore restates the central separation and
remodelling hypothesis, while presenting a more compact antonymic
account and an explicitly indexed laryngeal. It is not an
independent second demonstration of the same history.

There are unresolved premises even on this account. The earlier
commentary discusses analogical explanations of the long vowel in
the associated 'bend' verb; short \emph{*u} in
\emph{*lud-} does not automatically produce long \emph{*ū}.
The Baltic and Celtic comparisons motivate a semantic family but
do not alone establish the complete long-vowel adjective
[@Luehr1982, II, p. 383, n. 2384 in the author's reissue;
@Luehr2017, pp. 152--153]. Two roots avoid an unsupported
\emph{ī}–\emph{ū} sound correspondence, but their particular
identification and the proposed morphological convergence still
require evidence.

Levitsky offers a different remote unification. His methodological
discussion expressly treats the relation of \emph{i}- and
\emph{u}-extended roots under Persson's account of root extensions
and what he calls anomalous ablaut, rather than ordinary
\emph{e/o/zero} ablaut [@Levitsky2003, pp. 37--38]. In the
discussion of 'little', he connects \emph{*lei-} and
\emph{*leu-} with a wider \emph{*el-} family. Against Krogmann's
'bent' to 'small', he prefers 'cut, break into pieces, reduce to
small pieces' to 'small', within a broader proposed semantic
association of cutting, binding and bending
[@Levitsky2003, pp. 58--60, especially p. 59].

That offers a genuine alternative to the crouching semantics and
a hypothesis about the prehistory of the root families. It does
not supply a conditioned Germanic \emph{*ī > *ū}, or an independently
demonstrated derivation of all three adjective stems. The parallel
root extensions and semantic transitions would have to be
established independently; grouping words under a sufficiently
broad semantic prototype cannot itself establish the required
sound correspondences. The proposal therefore remains a
remote-etymological possibility, not the phonological basis of
the selected OE derivation.

### West Germanic shortening and gemination

The long-back-vowel versus short-vowel/geminate problem must not
be conflated with the front- versus back-vowel problem. Fulk
discusses OE *hlūtor : hluttor* 'clear' beside
*lȳtel : lyttel*, Old Saxon *hluttar*, *luttil* and High German
*hluttar*, *luzzil*. Unlike the much disputed gemination after
long vowels before \emph{j}, gemination before liquids has
substantial evidence outside Upper German. His proposed account
uses shortening in a closed syllable, as in
\emph{*xlūtr}, and extension of the resulting stem through
the paradigm; the liquids, unlike \emph{j}, were not made
syllabic under Sievers' law
[@Fulk2018, p. 127, §6.15].

The account has phonological conditioning and a comparison outside
'little'; it is not permission to shorten any long vowel and
double any consonant. Fulk's further discussion reconstructs
alternation of *hlūtor* with \emph{*hluttr-}, followed by mixture of
stems, and distinguishes later analogical alternations and new
clusters created by syncope [@Fulk2018, p. 132, §6.18]. Applied
to 'little', the account requires independently justified
liquid adjacency, syllabification and subsequent paradigmatic
distribution. It competes with Lühr's glide-bearing formation,
whose geminate would arise in a different environment, and is
more specific than Heidermanns' expressive explanation. None of
these accounts, even if it resolves the back-vowel alternation,
explains the Germanic front-vowel series. No new general
shortening or gemination rule is established here.

### Assessment and selected cell

For the executable OE history, the long-back-vowel input remains
the defensible choice: it is explicitly among Orel's reconstructed
alternatives and gives the independently attested long \emph{ȳ}
by ordinary umlaut. Its placement at Proto-Germanic is a
comparative reconstruction adopted for this branch, not proof
that every Germanic language inherited the same adjective.
Heidermanns restricts the back-vowel evidence to West Germanic,
and the Gothic–Norse front-vowel evidence does not independently
establish a common long-\emph{ū} ancestor
[@Orel2003, p. 248; @Heidermanns1993, pp. 372, 390].
The current citation and input are therefore retained, not
silently corrected from a front-vowel form. 'Regular' describes
the selected input-to-OE history; it does not certify a
resolved Indo-European etymology or a unified history of every
member of the family.

The selected [lūtilō]{.iv .recon lang=pgmc sort=lutilo role=selected_input}
combines that independently supported stem with the ordinary strong
neuter nominative–accusative plural ending \emph{*-ō}.
Fulk explicitly reconstructs this ending in the strong-adjective
paradigm [@Fulk2018, p. 209, §9.2]. He also prints the complete
\emph{*lūtilō} as the predecessor of Mercian *lȳtelu*
[@Fulk2018, p. 92, §5.6]. This is a stated derivation, not a merely
illustrative “form like” the selected reconstruction. The independent
stem and paradigm evidence remains important: his printed plural does
not resolve Kroonen's uncertainty about the wider root variants.
Citation and selected
form are two Proto-Germanic cells, not different historical stages;
no transponent, analogical replacement or artificial accent is used.

### Development and discriminatory value

[SC019 PNWGmcFinalLongORaising](#rule-PNWGmcFinalLongORaising)
gives \emph{*lūtilu}; [SC055 OEIUmlaut](#rule-OEIUmlaut)
gives \emph{*lȳtilu}. The heavy first syllable is followed by two
light syllables. Both weak high vowels therefore survive
[SC063 OEHighVowelApocope](#rule-OEHighVowelApocope), the unified
quantitative high-vowel-deletion event. The same HLL retention is
the evidential point of the conservative plurals of 'head' and
'livestock' [@Fulk2010, pp. 133--138; @Goering2016, pp. 176--177,
186--188; @Goering2023, pp. 60--63, 67--68].

The following identity trace at
[SC065 OEMedialSyncope](#rule-OEMedialSyncope) makes no second deletion.
The short-root condition of
[SC066 OELAdjacentSyncope](#rule-OELAdjacentSyncope) likewise does not
reach this long-root form. Later
[SC074 OEMedUnstressedILowering1](#rule-OEMedUnstressedILowering1)
gives \emph{*lȳtelu}. The complete cascade thus reaches the attested
comparator without an analogical restoration.

An indiscriminate rule deleting a medial high vowel after every heavy
root instead yields [*lȳtlu*]{.pred}. That wrong alternative changes
none of the previously selected histories, but fails on this plural.
An overgeneralized final apocope in HLL instead gives
[*lȳtel*]{.pred}, likewise without changing any previously selected
history. Thus the attested plural independently protects both its
medial and final retained high vowels.
The word is therefore a positive historical witness of retention and
a genuine nonapplication control for deletion. It does not replace
the selected singular history of 'head', nor does it imply that every
form of 'little' should retain its medial vowel.
