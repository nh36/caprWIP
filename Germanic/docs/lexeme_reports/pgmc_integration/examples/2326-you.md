# You: an inherited oblique does not settle its origin

CAPR uses \emph{*ízwiz} as both its Proto-Germanic reconstruction and
selected input for Old English *ēow* 'you', the plural oblique pronoun.
Its later development is regular within the model. The formation of the
inherited pronoun, however, remains a substantive comparative problem.

Kroonen starts from an older oblique \emph{*iwe} and proposes that
\emph{*izwiz} arose by metathesis from \emph{*iw-siz}, with a dative
suffix abstracted from the corresponding first-person plural. He also
distinguishes an older dative \emph{*iwiz}; his separate dual account
is a comparison within the pronominal system, not another plural input
[@Kroonen2013, pp. 275--276].

Fulk judges a different reported account probably most plausible:
Krahe and Meid's reduplication of \emph{*wiz} to \emph{*wiz-wiz},
followed by initial-*w* dissimilation. He explicitly states that there is no
consensus and discusses alternative dissimilations and analogical explanations
[@Fulk2018, pp. 187--188]. This is Fulk's assessment of a reported theory;
the original Krahe and Meid argument has not been independently verified
here. It is not a demonstrated named rebuttal of Kroonen's later dictionary.

Ringe, following Katz, instead develops the oblique system from
Indo-European formations including \emph{*uswé}, combining changes in
case formation with sandhi-conditioned loss and later prothetic *i*.
He also records a dissimilatory alternative associated with Weiss
[@Ringe2017, pp. 233--235]. The instrumental/dative history and the
relations between the two persons are load-bearing premises in that account,
not incidental spelling differences.

These explanations concern the ancestry and segmentation of the same
relevant plural oblique formation. They deserve discussion even though
they can converge on an inherited input from which the later English
history proceeds. A successful derivation from \emph{*ízwiz} cannot choose
between suffix abstraction and metathesis, reduplication and dissimilation,
or the alternative sandhi and case-formation history.

Ringe and Taylor's later account supplies coronal assimilation, glide
reanalysis and English realization. Their final-high-vowel loss is conditioned
by a heavy syllable in an unstressed, phonologically final word, not by
pronoun status or by absence of an acute accent
[@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 41--42, 55, 57--58, 171--175].
CAPR selects that weak final context explicitly; its root accent remains a
different notation axis. Campbell distinguishes West Saxon *ēow* from
earlier and northern *īow* [@Campbell1959, p. 283].

The model demonstrates the selected pronoun's later sound-law path under
its stated context. Publication must separately preserve the rival accounts
of its origin and their unsettled premises. Neither the chosen weak context
nor the correct English output resolves the Proto-Germanic formation.
