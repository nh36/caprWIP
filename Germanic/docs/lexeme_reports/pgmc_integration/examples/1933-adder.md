# Adder: a regular derivation and an unsettled feminine formation

CAPR selects \emph{*nḗdrōn}, both as its Proto-Germanic citation reconstruction
and as its transducer input, for Old English *nǣdre* 'adder'. The model produces
the selected form regularly. That computational result leaves a separate
philological question: which inherited feminine formation is being represented?

Orel prints two feminine alternatives, \emph{*nēđrōn} and
\emph{*nađrōn}, and describes their relationship to the masculine snake
formation in terms of ablaut [@Orel2003, p. 286]. Kroonen gives the feminine
\emph{*nēdrōn-}, alongside a masculine \emph{*nēdra-} underlying a
Northumbrian form. His separate masculine \emph{*nadra-} entry identifies
the long-vowel feminine as an ablauting formation, while leaving the proposed
connection with sewing and an original meaning 'winder' uncertain
[@Kroonen2013, pp. 381, 386]. The masculine comparanda and deeper
West-European antecedents are not interchangeable feminine inputs.

The long front vowel and feminine n-stem in CAPR's selection therefore have
direct comparative support. The acute accent belongs to CAPR's input notation;
the cited headwords do not independently certify that accented full word.
Orel's additional short-*a* feminine is a published alternative, not an OCR
variant or merely a stem cited without its ending. Nor does its presence prove
that Orel and Kroonen advocate mutually exclusive histories: both admit
ablauting formations, and Orel retains alternatives within his own account.
The unresolved question is what reflex distribution and formation history
justify the short-*a* feminine in relation to the selected long-vowel form.

Old English consonant and quantity variation requires another distinction.
Fulk discusses gemination before *r* in *næddre*, probably accompanied by
shortening, but calls its motivation obscure in forms with an etymologically
long vowel. He entertains analogical extension from alternating paradigms;
he does not reconstruct a new complete Proto-Germanic adder word
[@Fulk2018, p. 132]. That account can illuminate the later variant without
explaining the entire difference between inherited *ē* and *a*.

CAPR has demonstrated a regular path from its selected long-vowel feminine
to *nǣdre*. Publication should retain that result while briefly presenting
the genuine feminine alternative and its missing explanation. It should
neither declare the reconstruction uniquely settled nor inflate masculine
comparisons and uncertain remote etymology into additional rival histories
of the selected feminine.
