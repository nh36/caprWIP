# Thought: an explicit finite reconstruction and an unresolved formation

The selected item is Old English *þōhte* 'thought', the preterite third
singular of 'think', not the noun 'thought'. CAPR uses
\emph{*θánxtē} for both its Proto-Germanic citation and its selected input.
Its acute and dorsal-fricative notation are CAPR's representation;
the corresponding scholarly reconstruction is \emph{*þanhtē}.

Ringe explicitly prints the principal parts \emph{*þankijaną},
\emph{*þanhtē} and \emph{*þanhtaz}. This is complete inherited-word
evidence, not a past form assembled by CAPR from a cited present root and
a general weak ending. His account identifies exactly five reconstructable
Proto-Germanic class-I weak verbs without a linking vowel in the past
[@Ringe2017, p. 281]. Ringe and Taylor likewise distinguish that inherited
group from its later expansion [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 71--75, 97--99].

The deeper explanation is less secure than the finite reconstruction.
Fulk illustrates the connectorless preterite with \emph{*þaŋxta} and
the cluster \emph{*þaŋk-t-}, then reviews why the oldest weak verbs lack
the connector. Retention of a present-only Indo-European suffix is attractive
but does not by itself explain the restricted stem distribution. Loss of an
earlier connector through a phonological development is an alternative;
analogical remodelling of the remaining stems is another part of the
discussion. Fulk explicitly reports the absence of scholarly consensus
[@Fulk2018, pp. 299--301].

Those arguments must not be converted into additional complete ancestral
words attributed to Fulk. In particular, his counterfactual buy form
\emph{*bu3-dē} tests the dental formation by predicting the wrong Gothic
outcome; it is neither a thought reconstruction nor an endorsed buy input
[@Fulk2018, p. 300]. Nor is a debate over the extent of later connectorless
formations proof that the inherited think preterite itself lacks evidence.

The nasal is retained in CAPR's input. Its loss before the dorsal fricative
and the resulting long nasalized low vowel are derived rather than supplied
as an already nasal-less form. Fulk names *þōhte* as evidence that the
lengthened low vowel remained distinct long enough to develop to *ō* in
Anglo-Frisian, rather than merging with long *ā* of other origins
[@Fulk2018, p. 55]. Ringe and Taylor discuss the subsequent English
connectorless weak-verb history separately [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 141--143].

CAPR demonstrates the selected finite form's phonological development,
including the nasal-loss and rounding distinction. It has not thereby
explained the origin of the weak preterite formation. The appropriate
extended discussion is a qualified account of that missing premise,
not an assertion that every cited stem is a competing full-word reconstruction
or that successful sound changes establish the morphology's ancestry.
