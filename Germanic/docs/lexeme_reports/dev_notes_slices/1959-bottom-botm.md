## Development-note summary

The bottom note starts from a plain mismatch with a non-plain cause. The earlier row used `*budmăz`, but the FST produced `bodm`, whereas OE needs `botm`. DEV_NOTES argues that the missing piece is inherited paradigmatic allomorphy, not an isolated OE consonant rule. The note writes out a PIE mn-stem with nominative `*bʰudʰ-mḗn` and genitive `*bʰudʰ-mn-ós`, then the genitive dissimilation `*bʰudʰ-mn-ós → *bʰut-n-ós`, then Kluge's Law to PGmc `*buttaz`. Kroonen is quoted on the result: "`*budmṓ, *buttaz` gave rise to multiple stem variants, i.e. OS bodom < `*budma-`, OE botm < `*buttma-` and ON botn < `*buttna-`" [@Kroonen2013, p. 82].

The center of the note is the question that matters specifically for Old English: why does `*buttma-` contain both the oblique `-tt-` and the nominative `-m-`? Kroonen 2006 is used as the main answer. DEV_NOTES carries over his claim that "`*but(t)ma-` received its t analogically from `*buttaz`" and that this only makes sense if "`*bud-` and `*but(t)-` must have been two allomorphs at a certain stage" [@Kroonen2006, p. 22]. It also preserves his explanation that the oblique allomorph `*butt-` came from loss of `m` in the genitive and spread analogically, while the nominative `-m-` was never lost in the strong case [@Kroonen2006, p. 22]. OE `botm` is thus treated as an early analogical stem reshaping, not as a simple one-case reflex.

DEV_NOTES also uses bottom to sharpen project method. Campbell's `botm` example among West-Saxon `pm > tm` forms is kept, but it is not allowed to control the analysis by itself. Campbell's Anglian comparison, "`In Angl., however, pl, pm remained after short vowels and the spirant became voiced... *bopm`" [@Campbell1959, §§419--420], is used to show that the West-Saxon `t` is better read as inherited oblique `*tt` plus degemination than as a fresh OE hardening from `d`. The final row policy follows from that: the cognate-set headword can stay at the nominative-style reconstruction, but the OE row uses a cell-specific pre-OE input `*búttmaz` / DEV_NOTES `*buttmăz` because that is the form that directly yields `botm`.

Text or paraphrase:
This fragment contains the key quotations that justify the row's `early_analogy` label. Kroonen writes: "`The fact that *but(t)ma- received its t analogically from *buttaz can nevertheless only be understood if the two root forms were still part of one and the same paradigm after Kluge's law. In other words, the roots *bud- and *but(t)- must have been two allomorphs at a certain stage`" [@Kroonen2006, p. 22]. He then adds: "`the Germanic allomorph *butt- must be explained from loss of the m in the genitive and some other oblique cases: *bʰudʰ-mn-ós > *bʰudʰ-n-ós ... which by Kluge's law became *buttaz`" [@Kroonen2006, p. 22]. DEV_NOTES uses those quotations to spell out the historical point in plain prose: the `-m-` of the nominative was never restored because it was never lost there, and OE `*buttma-` is an analogical nominative created by combining oblique `*butt-` with the old nominative suffix `-mōn`. That is why the row is not treated as a pure one-step sound-law reflex from the nominative stem.

## Superseded or diagnostic material

The superseded state here is the earlier row policy that treated `*budmăz` as sufficient for Old English. The later note does not discard the nominative-style stem as etymologically wrong; it demotes it from row-specific input to cognate-set background and replaces it with a pre-OE analogical form that better matches the actual OE development.
