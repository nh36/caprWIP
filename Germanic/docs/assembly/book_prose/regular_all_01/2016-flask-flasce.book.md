The selected form is nominative plural *flascan* 'flasks', in *twā fatu
... flascan gehātene* 'two vessels called flasks' [@Hecht1900,
C p. 141, line 27]. The comparative citation \emph{*flaskō} is retained,
but the scored input is the full inherited weak feminine nominative
plural \emph{*fláskōniz}; its stem formation and ending have separate
warrants [@Orel2003, p. 104; @Ringe2017, pp. 312--313].
The separately attested dative singular *be þǣre flascan* 'by the flask'
tests \emph{*fláskōni}, not the plural ending [@Hecht1900,
p. 142, lines 2--4].

Old final-n loss precedes the apocope which exposes secondary final
\emph{n} in both inputs. Root fronting and restoration give
\emph{*flæskōn > *flaskōn}. Long \emph{*ō} before final \emph{n}
escapes early shortening and unstressed fronting; existing late
shortening and final lowering give \emph{*flaskon > *flaskan}.
Both cells thus yield *flascan* through normal entry. This is the
regular northern ending history among Ringe and Taylor's alternatives,
not a claim that the inherited accusative or dative plural is also
resolved [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 163--164].
Hill's common West Germanic raising followed by redistribution remains
a historical alternative [@Hill2010, pp. 433--443].

Nominative singular *flasce* 'flask' remains a separate problem:
the inherited singular input gives predicted [flæsċe]{.pred}.
Campbell and Luick explain the back root by levelling from inflected
forms; Wright and Wright instead propose retention before a cluster
followed originally by a back vowel [@Campbell1959, p. 61, §158;
@Luick1914, pp. 152--153, §161; @Wright1908, pp. 37--38, §57].
CAPR declines to adopt the retention rule without excluding its
historical possibility. Plain written *sc* does not settle
palatalization, while *flaxe* and *flaxan* require the separate
hard-cluster and metathesis history [@Campbell1959,
pp. 177--178, §§440--441; @RingeTaylor2014, p. 192].
