# OE -un > -on in the strong-verb pret.pl.: Lautgesetz or analogy?

## Question

The cogset target for OE class II strong-verb pret.pl. is `bugon`,
`sċufon`. The FST in its current form outputs `bugun`, `sċufun`. The
existing dossier `widuwe-u-preservation.md` (Appendix D) blocks
unstressed-`*u` lowering after a stem-syllable `*u` + single consonant
(Brunner §44 Anm. 7, Luick §326.2). On that conditioning, `bugun`
(stem `u` + single `g` + `-un`) is exactly parallel to `wuduwe`,
`munuc`, `duguþ`: the lowering is blocked, and `-un` is the regular
output. The question pursued here is whether the literature
unambiguously confirms that picture, i.e. whether the WS lWS form
`bugon` ~ `sċufon` is **analogical levelling** of the productive `-on`
ending, or whether some other phonological conditioning environment
(closed final syllable; verbal-ending status; etc.) makes `-un > -on`
in the pret.pl. a Lautgesetz that bypasses stem-`u` harmony.

## Handbook canvass

### Campbell §49, §373, §735(e)

**§49** (line 2262):

> "Also o is written for unaccented u with increasing frequency,
> **especially before a consonant** (e.g. past pl. in -on, older -un),
> but also in final position (e.g. neut. pl. of nouns in -o, -u)."

Campbell treats `-un > -on` as a graphic/phonetic tendency that
intensifies through OE. The phrase "older -un" is explicit: the
earliest stratum is `-un`; `-on` is an OE-internal substitution.

**§373** (line 10189):

> "Unaccented u is preserved in all instances in the early North.
> short texts, BH, and LV. In Ep., however, protected u > o very
> often, e.g. uuiloc-, helostr, deatlicostan, suornodun, and
> occasionally in absolute finality, e.g. seto (neut. p.), seado. In
> later sources u has an increasing tendency to change to o, but it
> was far more stable in absolute finality than when protected. VP
> preserves even protected u extensively, e.g. heafud, wuldur,
> past indic. pl. -un (very rarely -on), and it remains frequent in
> lNorth. and Ru.1. Ordinary OE forms are, however, e.g. heafod head,
> heofon heaven, tungol star, **past indic. pl. -on**, weak past of
> Class II -ode, superl. -ost, but n.s. of wa-, o- and u-stems -u,
> n.p.n. of a-stems -u, nW-S 1st sg. pres. indic. -u. o for
> unprotected u is increasingly common in late texts.
>
> **u is always well preserved after accented u**, e.g. sunu, wudu,
> dugup; before m, e.g. mapum, d.p. -um, -sum as suffix; in the suffix
> -ung; in the suffix -uc of whatever origin, e.g. beallucas, bulluc,
> gafeluc, hassuc, mattuc, munuc."

This is Campbell's authoritative statement. He locates the lowering of
"protected" `*u` (= pre-consonantal) in the period running from the
earliest texts to lWS, and lists pret.pl. `-un > -on` as one of its
exemplifications. The blocking environments he names — after accented
`u`, before `m`, in `-ung`, `-uc` — are stated as exceptionless
("always well preserved"). Crucially, Campbell does **not** carve out
the strong pret.pl. as exempt from that protection: the rule, as he
states it, would protect `bugun`-type forms (stem-`u` + single
consonant + `un`).

The label "analogical" is **not** used in §373 for the substitution of
`-on` for `-un`. Campbell's wording in §49 ("older -un") is neutral and
his §373 wording ("Ordinary OE forms are … past indic. pl. -on")
descriptive; he does not state a mechanism.

**§735(e)** (line 20229):

> "The pl. of the past indic. has -un in the earliest texts, and this
> remains in Cp. and VP beside -on … Ru.1 has usually -un, beside
> frequent -on."

Pure attestation history; no mechanism.

### Brunner §44 Anm. 7 + §364.2 Anm. 4

**§44 Anm. 7** (line 2092):

> "u erhält sich in der Endung -um und der Ableitungssilbe -unz bis
> ans Ende der ae. Zeit; im unmittelbaren Auslaut kommt spätws. zwar
> -o und später auch -a vor, doch zeigt sich selbst noch spät
> Schwanken zwischen -u und -o; **nach einem -u der Tonsilbe bleibt
> es überhaupt in der Regel bewahrt** (z. B. wudu Holz, sunu Sohn,
> aber 3(i)efu, -o Gabe, hofu, -o Pl. Höfe, rīcu, -o Reiche). Im
> Inlaut vor anderen Konsonanten außer -m und -nz ist -o- im Ws.
> schon früh durchwegs durchgeführt, **nur nach einem u der
> Stammsilbe steht vor einfachem Konsonanten -u-**, daher munuc
> Mönch, duzuð Tugend, aber hēafod Haupt, nacod nackt, tunzol Stern
> usw. In den angl. Texten ist auch hier -u- viel häufiger (heafud,
> wuldur usw.)."

Brunner's formulation has no exception for inflectional endings. By
Brunner's rule, pret.pl. `*-un` after a stem-syllable `u` + single
consonant (i.e. class II `bug-un`, `sċuf-un`, `lug-un`, `flug-un`)
should preserve `-u-`.

**§364.2** (line 14580):

> "Die älteste Endung des Ind. Plur. Prät. ist -un, idg. -nt
> (ursprünglich nur der 3. Pers. zugehörig, ebenfalls eine Aoristendung),
> wofür später -on und -an eintreten."

**§364.2 Anm. 4**:

> "Von den ältesten Texten hat Ep. nur -un (1 -on ist unsicher
> bezeugt), Corp. dagegen bereits öfter -on neben überwiegendem -un.
> In den altws. Texten ist -un bereits selten, namentlich im Or. Es
> herrscht überall -on vor … Im Merc. herrscht -un vor; der Vesp. Ps.
> hat daneben öfter -on … In R¹ treten die -an neben -un, -on etwas
> stärker hervor … Von den nordh. Texten hat R² häufig -un, daneben
> -on; dagegen herrscht -on in L und Rit."

Brunner's wording is descriptive — "wofür später -on und -an
eintreten." Neither *Analogie*, *Ausgleich*, nor *morphologisch* is
used in connection with the substitution of `-on` for pret.pl. `-un`.
A search of Brunner's full text (`grep -niE "analog|Ausgleich"`)
yields hits only for stem-vowel levelling within the pret.pl.
paradigm (e.g. §367 ff. on "Ausgleich des 3. Stammes nach dem 2.")
and for unrelated phenomena. Brunner has no explicit statement that
ending-vowel `-un > -on` is itself an analogical/levelling change.

### Bülbring §364 + §§386–410

**§364** (line 7164):

> "Wie i (§ 359 b) so hat auch unbetontes u, soweit es nicht gemäß
> § 358 b und § 388 weggefallen war, eine **große Neigung zu
> weiterer Aussprache**. Daher erscheint schon in frühester
> historischer Zeit häufig o daneben: smeoru, -o 'Schmer' Corp.,
> uyiluc, uuiloc 'Purpurschnecke' Ep., sadul, sadol 'Sattel' Corp.
> Später tritt neben u und o auch a auf, **außer vor m und ŋ, welche
> das u schützen**: dagum Dat. Plur. 'Tagen' § 366, 2, monung
> 'Mahnung' § 391 b. … Inlautend dagegen bleibt a außerhalb des Kent.
> selten: brōður, brōdor, brōðar 'Bruder', **budun, budon, budan
> 'boten'**."

Bülbring frames it as a phonetic *Neigung* (tendency) — i.e. a
Lautgesetz, not an analogy. Blocking environments: before `m`, before
`ŋ`. Bülbring §364 does **not** mention stem-`u` harmony. The class II
pret.pl. `bud-un / bud-on / bud-an` is presented unproblematically as
an instance of inlautendes `u/o/a` variation, with no harmony-blocking
treatment. **This is the strongest single piece of evidence in any
handbook that the pret.pl. is treated as a pure inlaut case, with no
stem-`u` exemption.** Bülbring uses no word from the *Analogie* /
*Ausgleich* family in §364.

**§§386–410** (the unstressed-vowel section): §391 covers original `ō`
in medial syllables, §394 covers `ou (au)`, §397 ff. the
composition-junction vowels. None of these sections raises a separate
analogy-vs-soundlaw question for the pret.pl. ending. They treat the
ending as a regular reflex of `*-un` subject to the §364 lowering.

### Luick §326

**§326** (line 17291), the locus classicus:

> "**§326.** Eine entsprechende Bewegung ergriff um dieselbe Zeit auch
> das u, wofern es nicht vor m oder ŋ … stand …
>
> 1. Im Auslaut hat u wohl nur eine geringe Senkung erfahren … **Durch
> ein u der Tonsilbe ist es überhaupt bewahrt worden**: die Schreibung
> weist in solchen Fällen fast ständiges u auf. So: me(o)du, sunu,
> wudu …
>
> 2. Im Inlaut scheint im Westsächsischen die Stufe o erreicht worden
> zu sein; **doch wirkt auch hier ein u der Stammsilbe (vor einfachen
> Konsonanten) bewahrend**. So: (urg. u) nacod, hēafod, heofon, hamor,
> **ridon, wǣron**, h(e)afoc, nafola, afora; (u nach §317) sāwol,
> wundor, tungol; (u nach §312) huntoð, geogoð, locod, locode, leofost,
> leofosta; **aber munuc 'Mönch', duguð 'Tugend', iuguð 'Jugend'**.
>
> In den anglischen Dialekten war dagegen die Senkung geringer: in der
> Schreibung erscheint hier u viel häufiger, im Psalter sogar
> vorwiegend: **heafud, heofun, wērun, wuldur, leofuste** usw."

This is the single most important passage. Two things to note:

1. Luick lists `ridon`, `wǣron` — both pret.pl. forms — as exemplars
   of the WS inlaut `*u > o` lowering. So the lowering of `*-un >
   -on` is, on Luick's account, the **same Lautgesetz** as that which
   produces `heafod` from `*hēafud`. (Anglian `wērun`, `heafud`,
   `wuldur`, `leofuste` confirm the Anglian retention pattern.)

2. The blocking condition Luick states is **stem-`u` + single
   consonant**. Class II `*bug-un`, `*sċuf-un`, `*lug-un`, `*flug-un`
   meet this pattern exactly. Luick does not anywhere note that
   inflectional endings (specifically pret.pl. `-un`) escape the
   harmony block, nor does he include any class II pret.pl. in the
   long list of WS lowering exemplars (he uses class I `ridon` and
   the wesan-pret.pl. `wǣron`, neither of which has a stem-`u`).

**§326 Anm. 2** (line 17339):

> "Dieser Vorgang wiederholte sich in der späteren Zeit, als seine
> Voraussetzungen neuerlich eintraten. Im zehnten Jahrhundert wurde
> vielfach die Endung -um des Dativplurals durch **analogische
> Einflüsse** zu -un umgestaltet und dieses zu -on gewandelt: ēagon
> 'den Augen', dagon 'den Tagen'."

Here Luick *does* use *analogisch*, but for the dat.pl. `-um` → `-un`
remodelling — not for `-un > -on` itself. The `-un > -on` change is
described as the operation of the §326 Lautgesetz on a newly
analogically generated `-un`. So in Luick's framework `-un > -on`
remains a phonological law; what is analogical is the substrate (the
prior *generation* of an `-un` from inherited `-um`).

### Hogg vol.1 §3.3.1.3 + §3.3.3.2

**§3.3.1.3** (line 4414):

> "A further exception concerns the back high vowel /u/. As indicated
> above, the spelling evidence suggests that unstressed /u/ was
> normally lowered and centralised. However, **word-finally after
> another /u/, as in sunu 'son', or before /m/, as in the dative
> plural inflexion -um, and also in the suffix -uc, -ung, e.g. munuc
> 'monk', costung 'temptation', the <u> was normally preserved.** The
> circumstances which combined to thus protect the high vowel and the
> phonemic status of unstressed [u] are equally obscure, see §3.3.3.2
> for further discussion."

Hogg lists the standard set of preservation environments (after `u`
word-finally; before `m`; in `-uc`, `-ung`) but **does not list
preservation of medial `-u-` after stem-`u` + single consonant** in
this enumeration. He explicitly calls the conditioning "obscure".

**§3.3.3.2** (line 5876):

> "/u/ had a strong tendency to lower, **especially when a consonant
> followed**, so we find, for example, **heofon 'heaven' in Early West
> Saxon rather than heofun**, although **if /u/ is in absolute
> finality, as in the nominative plural inflexion of a-declension
> neuter nouns, e.g. scipu 'ships', it more usually remained**. The
> general rule when /u/ was followed by a consonant is that the later
> the text the more likely it is that o-spellings would prevail."

Hogg treats the change as a *tendency* (Lautgesetz with progressive
implementation) not as analogy. He singles out **closed-syllable**
contexts as the locus of lowering and **absolute-final** as the locus
of preservation. He does not address the strong pret.pl. `-un`
specifically, nor does he label the substitution `-un > -on` as
analogical.

### Ringe & Taylor §6.9.6, §6.9.4

**§6.9.6** (line 19031, "Mergers of unstressed vowels"):

> "For the most part unstressed a and u remained distinct in early OE
> (the latter often written o). However, they are beginning to be
> confused in 9th-century Kentish charters … and there are a few
> early WS examples of -a for expected -u. Moreover, the fluctuation
> between -ad(-) and -od(-) ~ -ud(-) in the past and past ptc. of
> class II weak verbs in early WS … and Kentish … suggests that **by
> 900 the contrast between the unstressed back vowels was breaking
> down in closed final syllables in Kentish and in all positions,
> except word-finally, in WS**."

R&T date the *merger* of unstressed back vowels to c. 900. This
post-dates the lowering rule that produces `heafod` from `*heafud`
(which is operative already in Ep./Erf., 8th c.). R&T are describing
the late phonemic collapse, not the Brunner-/Luick-lowering itself.
In **§6.9.4** (line 18383) they cite:

> "PGmc *dribun 'they drove' (Goth. us-dribun, ON drifu, OS dribun,
> OHG tribun) > North. for-driofon ~ for-drifon, **WS (fully
> levelled) drifon**;"

and (line 18426):

> "PGmc *bidun 'they waited' (Goth. *bidun, ON bidu, OS bidun, OHG
> bitun) > **OE *bidun > Merc. d-biodun, North. ge-biodon (but WS
> ge-bidon)**."

The "fully levelled" comment refers to back-umlaut `io` levelling out
of class I pret.pl., not to ending levelling. But note R&T's
intermediate reconstruction "OE *bidun" — they explicitly stage `-un`
as the OE-internal precursor of attested `-on`. This confirms that the
OE chronology is `-un` (early) > `-on` (later). R&T do not
characterise the change as analogical; they treat it implicitly as
phonological (part of the same `*u > o` lowering).

### Fulk Comp.Gram. §4.5, §5.6

**§4.5** (line 3907):

> "PGmc. u appears as u in Gothic, but as aú (/ɔ/) before r, h, ƕ … as
> in pret. 3 pl. -budun 'offered' (OE budon, **OS budun**) …"

PGmc inheritance is `*-un`. Fulk gives OE `budon` as citation form
but pairs it with OS `budun`, confirming that OS robustly preserves
`-un` (a key cross-Germanic data point — see below).

**§5.6** (line 5926, on later WGmc unstressed-vowel changes):

> "As in the other WGmc. languages, unstressed vowels tend to weaken
> and be variously spelt with the passage of time."

For OS specifically: "before m usually o is written, occasionally u,
as in wastom, -um 'growth.'" For OHG: "the opposition between u and o
is weakened early in favor of o." Fulk treats all of this as
phonological *weakening* (Lautgesetz family), not as analogy.

## Cross-Germanic evidence

The cognate languages decisively indicate that PGmc/PWGmc `*-un` is
robustly preserved in the early OS, OFris, OHG record:

* **Old Saxon** (Heliand, Genesis): `wurdun` 'they became', `dribun`
  'they drove', `bidun` 'they waited', `budun` 'they offered'. OS
  has `-un` essentially exceptionlessly through the ninth century.
  (Fulk §4.5; R&T §6.9.4.)

* **Old High German** (Tatian, Otfrid): pret.pl. `-un` ~ `-on`
  variation throughout, with `-un` heavy in Tatian and `-un ~ -on`
  in Otfrid and the early Bavarian record. Cognates: `tribun`,
  `quāmun`, `wurtun ~ wurdun`. OHG, like OE, exhibits an internal
  `-un > -on` raising/lowering tendency, but OHG never standardises
  on `-on` the way late WS does.

* **Old Frisian**: the dat.pl. `-um` is robustly preserved as such
  (Fulk §5.6), and the pret.pl. ending is `-on` ~ `-en` already in
  the earliest OFris records (which are c. 1300, hence post-OE). The
  OFris stratum is too late to bear directly on OE chronology.

* **Old Norse**: pret.pl. is `-u` (`drifu`, `bidu`), reflecting
  apocope of `-n` on a `-un` stratum (cf. §6.9.4 above, "ON drifu").

The cross-Germanic picture corroborates Brunner's chronology in §364.2:
PGmc/PWGmc `*-un` is the inherited form; `-on` is an OE-internal
substitute. OS preservation of `-un` is essentially exceptionless,
which (a) confirms `*-un` as the WGmc input to OE and (b) shows that
the change to `-on` is **OE-specific**, not inherited.

## Chronology

From Campbell §735(e), Brunner §364.2 Anm. 4, Bülbring §364, Luick
§326:

| Period / text | Pret.pl. ending |
| -- | -- |
| Épinal (8th c., Mercian) | only `-un` (one doubtful `-on`) |
| Erfurt (8th c.) | `-un` dominant |
| Corpus Glossary (c. 800, Mercian) | `-un` dominant, `-on` already frequent |
| Vespasian Psalter (mid-9th c., Mercian) | `-un` dominant, `-on` esp. in weak-pret. cl. II |
| Rushworth¹ (10th c., Mercian) | `-un` usual; `-on`, `-an` frequent; rare `-en` |
| Lindisfarne, Rushworth² (10th c., Northumbrian) | `-on` dominant; some `-un` (more in R²); rare `-an`, `-en` |
| Ritual (Northumbrian) | `-on` dominant; one `-an`, one `-en` |
| early WS (Or., CP, Chron.) | `-on` dominant; `-un` rare; `-an` already frequent in Or. |
| late WS, Kentish glosses | `-on` ≈ `-an`; `-an` increasing |

Earliest `-on`-spelling: a single doubtful `-on` in Épinal (≈ 8th c.);
Corpus has it frequently. Latest `-un` in Mercian Rushworth¹ (10th c.).
By mature WS (c. 900 onward) `-on` is the orthographic norm, with
`-an` increasing through the 10th–11th c. as part of the broader
collapse of unstressed back vowels (R&T §6.9.6).

The dialect distribution is clear: Mercian (Anglian) preserves `-un`
robustly into the 10th c.; West Saxon shifts to `-on` by the early 9th
c.; Northumbrian shifts very early (Lindisfarne, Ritual: dominant
`-on`).

## Verdict

**Mixed, with the operative literature converging on a clear picture.**

* **Lautgesetz with a phonological conditioning environment? — Yes,
  with stem-`u` harmony as the blocking environment.** The general
  lowering `*u > o` in unstressed inlaut, and specifically in closed
  final syllables (`-un#`), is a WS Lautgesetz operative from the
  early 9th century. Brunner §44 Anm. 7 and Luick §326.2 give the
  rule: lowering applies in inlaut before any single C except `m`,
  `ŋ`; **blocked when a stem-syllable `u` precedes a single
  consonant**. The rule produces `heafod` ← `*heafud`, `nacod`,
  `tungol`, and (per Luick's own list) `ridon`, `wǣron`. Stem-`u`
  harmony preserves `munuc`, `duguð`, `iuguð`, and (by parallel
  derivation) `wuduwe`.

* **Pure analogy / morphological levelling? — Not for the lowering
  itself, but yes for the lWS-attested `bugon`/`sċufon`-type forms
  in class II.** No handbook (Campbell, Brunner, Bülbring, Luick,
  Hogg, R&T, Fulk) explicitly characterises `-un > -on` *qua sound
  change* as analogical. The only `Analogie`/`Ausgleich` label
  attached to anything in this region is Luick §326 Anm. 2's claim
  that **dat.pl. `-um → -un`** is analogical (with a subsequent
  Lautgesetz `-un > -on`). However, *applied to class II strong-verb
  pret.pl.s* (`*bug-un`, `*sċuf-un`, `*lug-un`, `*flug-un`), the
  Brunner/Luick stem-`u` harmony rule predicts preservation of `-un`
  exactly as it predicts preservation of `-u-` in `wuduwe`, `munuc`,
  `duguð`. The lWS attested forms `bugon`, `sċufon`, `lugon`,
  `flugon` therefore violate the harmony rule, and the natural
  explanation is **paradigm-internal levelling on the dominant `-on`
  ending of all other strong-verb classes** (cl. I `ridon`, `drifon`;
  cl. III `bundon`, `holpon`; cl. IV `nāmon`, `bǣron`; cl. V
  `wǣron`, `sǣton`; cl. VI `sċōpon`; cl. VII `hēoldon`), reinforced
  by the productive weak pret.pl. `-don`. None of those models has a
  stem-`u`, so none was subject to harmony-blocking; their `-on` is
  Lautgesetzlich and dominant.

* **Mixed / unclear?** Strictly, the literature is unclear only in
  the sense that **no handbook explicitly names the class II strong
  pret.pl. `bugon`-type as analogical** — they all silently treat it
  as a normal exemplar of the lowering, never confronting the harmony
  exception. The harmony exception itself is uncontroversial (Brunner,
  Luick, Campbell §373, Hogg §3.3.1.3 all assert it for nominal
  forms). What is missing in the handbook tradition is an explicit
  reconciliation of (i) stem-`u` harmony with (ii) the universal
  attestation of `-on` in class II pret.pl. The reconciliation
  proposed in Appendix D of `widuwe-u-preservation.md` — that (ii) is
  analogical — is the only one consistent with the explicit harmony
  rule in (i) and with the cross-Germanic and chronological evidence
  surveyed above. R&T's "fully levelled" comment on `drifon`
  (§6.9.4) is suggestive but applies to back-umlaut `io`, not to the
  ending vowel.

## Implications for FST design

The phonological cascade should output `bugun`, `sċufun`,
`lugun`, `flugun` — the early-OE Lautgesetz forms preserved by
stem-`u` harmony, identical in conditioning to `wuduwe`, `munuc`,
`duguð`. This matches the FST's *current* output. The lWS attested
forms `bugon`, `sċufon`, etc. are best treated as a **morphological
overlay** lying outside the phonological cascade.

Concretely:

* **Do not patch the FST to produce `bugon`/`sċufon`.** A patch that
  forced lowering in pret.pl. `-un` would have to either (a) apply a
  morphological diacritic to inflectional `-un` (effectively encoding
  the analogical levelling as a sound change), which is theoretically
  unmotivated and would have no support in any of the seven canvassed
  handbooks; or (b) drop the stem-`u` harmony block and re-break
  `wuduwe`, `munuc`, `duguð`. Both options are wrong.

* **Add `bugon`, `sċufon`, `lugon`, `flugon`, `gulon`** (and any other
  class II / cl. III stem-`u` strong pret.pl.s in the cogset) to
  `oe_known_problems.tsv` (or the equivalent overlay file) with the
  rationale: *lWS analogical `-on` for regular `-un`; paradigm
  levelling on the dominant `-on` ending across other strong-verb
  classes; cf. Campbell §735(e), Brunner §364.2 Anm. 4, Luick §326
  Anm. 2; the FST's `-un` output is the philologically correct
  early-OE form (Ép., Cp., VP, Ru.¹).*

* **Cogset-wide audit.** Enumerate all rows whose target ends in
  stem-`u` + single C + `-on` in a strong-verb pret.pl., and tag
  them as analogical-overlay rather than phonological output. The
  affected paradigms are class II throughout (root vowel `u` in the
  pret.pl.) and the `u`-grade of class III strong verbs whose
  3rd-stem also has `u` (`bundon` is fine — the pret.pl. stem-vowel
  is `u` but it is followed by `nd` in a cluster, *not* a single
  consonant, so harmony does not apply and `-on` is Lautgesetzlich).
  Sample audit set: `bugon`, `sċufon`, `lugon`, `flugon`, `gulon`,
  `crupon`, `lutton`/`luton`. These all have stem-`u` + single C +
  `-un` and so the harmony rule would predict `-un`.

* **Anglian alignment.** The FST output `bugun`, `sċufun` is
  philologically correct not only as the early-OE stratum but also
