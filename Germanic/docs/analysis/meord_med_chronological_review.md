# Chronological Literature Review: OE *meord* / *mēd* (PGmc *mizdō-)

**Date**: 2026-04-26 (current session)
**Scope**: A chronological survey of every locally available source that
discusses the OE doublet *meord* ~ *mēd* 'reward' (< PGmc \*mizdō-), its
direct attestations, and the phonological pathways proposed for the two
outcomes. The aim is a primary-text-grounded synthesis to guide the project's
treatment of TSV row 752 (id 2124).

**Methodological note.** Every quotation below is reproduced from the
referenced .txt or .pdf in `~/capr-v3-working/docs/references/`, with the
file path and line number cited. Where the OCR is corrupt, I quote it
verbatim and flag it. Where a claim in the project's earlier notes
(`DEV_NOTES.md` §17.24, `mismatch_dossier_mizdo*.md`, `notable_findings.md`
#11) is not directly supported by the primary text I read, I flag it
explicitly.

## 2. Chronological survey

### 2.1 Streitberg, *Urgermanische Grammatik* (1896)

`docs/references/streitberg_urgermanische_grammatik.txt`. Two relevant
passages:

**(a)** §on Gmc \*ē, lines 4063–4071:

> "Auf 'Ersatzdehnung', die durch den Verlust eines z veranlaßt ist,
> scheint ē in ae. mēd 'Lohn, Bezahlung', as. mēda, ahd. meta meata
> miata mieta zurückzugehn. Die got. Form lautet mizdō, der das ae.
> Stf. ι̯o-Stamm meord genau entspricht. Urverwandt sind gr. μισθός,
> abg. mizda. Anders E. Sievers, PBrB. XVIII. 409."

(OCR is rough; my normalised reading; cf. lines 4067–4070
"got. Form lautet mizdö, der das ae. Stt. Xsy. meord genau / entspricht.")

**(b)** §on idg. \*z preserved before voiced obstruent, lines 7647–7650:

> "Natürlich bleibt idg. z im Germanischen als stimmhafter Laut erhalten,
> wenn der folgende Konsonant stimmhaft bleibt, mit andern Worten vor
> den aspirierten Medien. Vgl. gr. μισθός 'Lohn', got. mizdō, ae. meord."

**Position.** Streitberg already in 1896 has **both pathways on the
table**. The ē of *mēd* is from compensatory lengthening following loss
of \*z. *meord* is the direct continuation of \*mizdō with regular
\*z-retention before a voiced obstruent (and later rhotacism plus
breaking, though Streitberg does not spell out the OE-internal
breaking). He flags Sievers (PBrB. XVIII.409, not in the repo) as
proposing an alternative account. **The doublet thus has a 130-year
history in the literature.**

### 2.2 Sievers — PBrB. XVIII. 409

### 2.3 Bülbring, *Altenglisches Elementarbuch* (1902)

`docs/references/bulbring_altenglisches_elementarbuch.txt`, line 8965
(§484 Anm. 2):

> "Der Übergang von z>r ist älter als die Brechung (§ 130 ff.):
> mearg 'Mark' (urg. \*mazzom, as. marg), méord 'Lohn'
> (urg. \*mëzđō § 81a < \*mizdō, got. mizdō) u. ä."

**Position.** Bülbring treats \*meord\* as the regular outcome via
**rhotacism (z→r) before breaking**. He does not, in the passages
located, separately discuss *mēd*; his framework is the rhotacism +
breaking pathway only. Note also at line 2122 he cites *mēda* 'Miete'
as an example of OE *ē* ("ē: hēr 'hier', mēda 'Miete', lēt 'ließ'…"),
treating this *ē* as the closed/narrow PGmc *ē² type without
substantive analysis.

### 2.4 Brunner, *Altenglische Grammatik* (3rd ed. 1965, after Sievers)

`docs/references/brunner_1965_altenglische_grammatik.txt`, §84.1.b
(line 1942):

> "ae. (angl. u. poet.) meord Lohn (eo nach § 84), got. mizdō, Pl.
> heordan Werg, reord Sprache (aber ʒeriorde, i-Stamm), leornian
> neben liornian lernen … die Pronomina ae. mē, þē, ʒē, wē."

**Position.** Brunner explicitly characterises *meord* as **Anglian and
poetic**, with the diphthong *eo* arising from the breaking-style
change conditioned by following \*z (>r) in the same syllable. He does
not at this point address *mēd* directly. The cross-reference index at
line 25203 lists "métsceat s. médsceat" — i.e. compounds are
uniformly under *mēd-*, consistent with Brunner treating *meord* as
the Anglian/poetic doublet member.

### 2.5 Luick, *Historische Grammatik der englischen Sprache* (1914–1940)

`docs/references/luick_historische_grammatik.txt`. Two relevant places:

**(a)** §103-area (line 5834) — *mēd* listed under PGmc \*ē² ("narrow ē"):

> "Im Altenglischen liegt [ē²] vor in hēr 'hier', poet. cēn 'Fichte',
> wohl auch in mēd 'Lohn'."

**(b)** §141-area (lines 7083–7091) — *meord* under z-rhotacism +
i,u-lowering:

> "Vor urg. z, welches zu r wurde oder abfiel, wurde i, u zu e, o, wenn
> dieses z>r derselben Silbe angehörte und nicht etwa in der nächsten
> Silbe i folgte. So urengl. \*merd 'Lohn', \*herd 'Werg', \*rerd
> 'Sprache', \*lern- und \*lirn- lernen' … (woraus ae. meord, heord,
> reord, leornian, liornian; mē, þē, wē, ʒē)."

**Position.** Luick has **both forms but two distinct accounts**:
*mēd* = ē² (the narrow ē whose PGmc source he treats elsewhere as
e.g. originally long-diphthong contraction, *not* z-loss in this
passage); *meord* = regular pre-rhotacism lowering of *i to *e
followed by breaking-like diphthongisation. Luick does **not** here
explicitly propose z-loss + comp. lengthening for *mēd*; that is
implied only by listing it under ē².

### 2.6 Hirt, *Urgermanisch* I (1931–1934), p. 33

**Not in the repo as a primary text.** Cited via Orel (§2.9 below):

> "(-ē² compensates the loss of \*-z-)"
> (cited in `orel_handbook_germanic_etymology.txt:27062`)

This is the **PGmc-level doublet hypothesis**: \*mizdō and \*mēdō are
both inherited from Proto-Germanic, the latter with compensatory
lengthening of \*ē² already in PGmc, before the WGmc/dialectal
splits. As an actual passage, all I can verify locally is the bracketed
parenthetical Orel quotes — the Hirt text is not in the repo.

### 2.7 Pokorny, *Indogermanisches etymologisches Wörterbuch* (1959)

`docs/references/pokorny_iew_pages/00000256.txt`, s.v. *mizdhó-*:

> "*mizdhó-* 'Lohn, Sold'. Ai. mīḍhá- n., av. mēžda-, osset. mizd, myzd
> 'Lohn', np. muzd 'Lohn'; gr. μισθός 'Sold'; got. mizdō 'Lohn', ags.
> meord ds. und ags. mēd, as. mēda, ahd. meta, miata ds., nhd. Miete;
> aksl. mъzda, mozda 'Lohn'."

**Position.** Pokorny lists **both** OE forms but offers no
phonological analysis. He treats them as parallel reflexes of the same
PIE etymon — a presentational doublet without a stated mechanism.

### 2.8 Campbell, *Old English Grammar* (1959)

`docs/references/campbell_old_english_grammar.txt`. Multiple relevant
sections:

**(a)** §35 (line 2030) — *mēd* listed among examples of PGmc *ē²*
(the "narrow" long e):

> "ǣ representing both long and short sounds, e.g. … mēd reward, lēf
> weak, slēp slept …"

§35 footnote-equivalent (line 3468):

> "(3) Prim. Gmc. ǣ corresponds to I-E ē, while Prim. Gmc. ē is of
> composite origin, representing as a rule I-E ē, but also sometimes
> I-E iz (cf. OE mēd reward, Gr. μισθός, and see § 404). Prim. Gmc.
> ē is found in OE mainly in the past tenses of strong verbs of Class
> VII, but it occurs also in the words mēd reward, cēn torch, lēf
> weak, hēr here, Wēland Wayland."

**(b)** §123 (line 4068) — *meord* under PGmc z-conditioned lowering:

> "OE is derived from a form of West Gmc. which shared with North Gmc.
> a lowering of i and u to e and o before the voiced spirant z, which
> arose from I-E s by Verner's law, and is later lost or changed to r
> (see §§ 398.2, 404). The OE forms exhibiting these changes are
> meord reward (cf. Gr. μισθός, Goth. mizdō), leornian learn, the
> pron. wē, ʒē, hē, mē, þē … and the prefixes or-, tor-."

§123 fn.2 (line 4091):

> "The eo of meord, leornian is from e by a later change (see § 146).
> Beside leornian, forms with io are found in North., where original
> eo and io are well distinguished, and reflect a Prim. OE variation
> of e and i. … so the word can hardly be regarded as reliable
> evidence for the sound-change under discussion."

**(c)** §404 (line 10809) — z-loss with comp. lengthening:

> "Gmc. z, whether due to Verner's law or of earlier origin (§ 398.2.3),
> became r in North and West Gmc. … Between vowel and consonant r
> usually remains, e.g. hord, reord, &c. (see § 123–4); but it is lost
> with compensatory lengthening in OE hād- hair (cf. ON haddr), mēd
> reward (beside meord), twīn linen (cf. Ger. zwirn); cf. OS līnon
> learn, MD hede hards of flax (beside OE leornian, heordan, § 124)."

**(d)** §588 (line 15201) — *meord* and *mēd* listed as variants in the
strong-feminine ō-stem class:

> "Long-syllable nouns declined like lār are: bōt remedy, gād goad,
> lād way, lāf remainder, meord (mēd, § 404) reward, reord voice,
> reord food, rōd cross, sorg sorrow …"

**Position.** Campbell distributes the two forms across **two
phonological accounts**:

1. **meord** is the regular outcome of \*z + lowering (§123) +
   later breaking of *e* before *r+C (§146).
2. **mēd** is the irregular/variant outcome of \*z-loss with
   compensatory lengthening (§404).

He does **not** treat one as analogical from the other; both are
phonological. He does **not** invoke a PGmc-level doublet (à la Hirt).
And he is explicit that Campbell §404's z-loss is *not* a regular OE
sound change — *mēd* is one of a small number of cases (alongside hād-,
twīn, leornian/līnon …) where the loss happened.

### 2.9 Orel, *Handbook of Germanic Etymology* (2003)

`docs/references/orel_handbook_germanic_etymology.txt`, line 27059:

> "*miz¶ò(n) ~ *mè²¶ò sb.f.: Goth mizdo 'reward, pay', OE meord id.,
> méd 'meed, reward', OFris mède 'rent', OS mèda 'payment', OHG mieta
> 'price'. In WGmc a secondary variant is attested. Related to Skt
> neut. mīḍhá- … Av miždà- … Gk μισθός … Slav *mъzda. … Hirt Urgerm.
> I 33 (-è²- compensates the loss of *-z-)."

(OCR: "miz¶ò" = *mizdō; "mè²¶ò" = *mē²dō.)

**Position.** Orel reconstructs a **PGmc-level doublet \*mizdō ~
\*mēdō**, citing **Hirt** for the comp.-lengthening account of the
\*ē² variant. Both OE forms are then directly inherited PGmc
variants, not the product of WGmc-internal sporadic loss. This is the
strongest statement in the local literature of the user's preferred
"two-input" framing — though Orel's mechanism is **PGmc** comp.
lengthening (Hirt 1931), not paradigm-cell split.

### 2.10 Crist, *Conspiracy in Historical Phonology* (PhD diss., U Penn, 2001)

`docs/references/crist_2001_conspiracy_in_historical_phonology.txt`,
§3.5.5 "*z → ∅ / i with compensatory lengthening" (lines 5210–5310).

The data table (line 5218):

> "Goth     OHG                OE        OFris     OS
>  mizdo    mēta/miata/mieta   mēd/meord mēde/mīde/meide  mēda/mieda  'reward'"

The proposal:

> "In Crist (forthcoming), I argue that there is actually a single,
> lautgesetzlich rule at work in Ingvaeonic: namely, *z → ∅ / i ] _ ,
> or perhaps *z → ∅ / V[-bk] ] _ ."

> "The only word for which difficulty remains is OHG mēta 'reward';
> one possible explanation is that … the rule in question was
> lautgesetzlich within Ingvaeonic but sporadic in the rest of WGmc.
> Or, perhaps OHG mēta is a loan from Ingvaeonic; it is certainly
> reasonable for a word for 'reward' to spread through trade."

**Position.** Crist 2001 lays out the framework that becomes "Crist's
Law" the next year: the z-loss is **regular within Ingvaeonic** (= OE,
OS, OFris), not sporadic. *mēd* is regular Ingvaeonic; *meord* is the
problem case (see Crist 2002).

### 2.11 Crist, "An Analysis of *z loss in West Germanic" (LSA 2002)

`docs/references/crist_2002_z_loss_west_germanic.txt`. The locus
classicus for Crist's Law.

§1 (line 9):

> "Proposal: a regular sound change in the Ingvaeonic dialects of West
> Germanic (OE, OFris, OS): \*z > ∅ / {\*i,\*e} _ \$ with compensatory
> lengthening of preceding vowel. … While earlier accounts were
> obliged to assume that the rule of \*z-deletion applied sporadically,
> the rule proposed here appears to have applied regularly."

§9 "Problematic forms" (line 142):

> "OHG mēta/miata/mieta (Secondary \*z-deletion doesn't otherwise occur
> in OHG)
> OFris, OS mē(r) (\*z should have been deleted completely, but it is
> variably present as r)
> **OE meord, heorde** (Deletion of \*z unexpectedly fails)
> OE leornian, OFris lernēn/lirnēn (same)"

§9 cont. (line 162):

> "In the cases of OE meord, heorde, I notice that the failure of
> secondary \*z-deletion is found in both the cases where \*d follows
> (so far as I know, these are the only cases of this kind).
> Continental Pre-OE was originally spoken in Jutland at the northern
> periphery of the Ingvaeonic continuum; so perhaps the application of
> the rule in polysyllabic words did not spread this far north. The
> existence of OE mēd beside meord is not surprising if Old English is
> the product of some degree of dialect mixture, as is generally
> assumed."

§ on *mēd* in the appendix (line 334):

> "Germanic mēdō from mēizdhā. … Torp of course assumes the old view
> that the long vowel in the WGmc forms arises from PIE *mēizdhā >
> PGmc *mēzdō(n). However, Ringe (1984) refutes the long-standing
> notion that there was an *ē² in PGmc. In this case, the apparent
> *ē² is simply the product of the regular compensatory lengthening."

**Position.** *mēd* = product of the regular Ingvaeonic z-loss law
(Crist's Law). *meord* = a residue from the **far north** of the
Ingvaeonic continuum (Jutland) where the rule failed to extend to
polysyllables; OE then mixed both forms through dialect contact.
**Crist explicitly rejects the Hirt PGmc-\*ē² doublet** (citing Ringe
1984, not in repo).

### 2.12 Ringe, *From Proto-Indo-European to Proto-Germanic* (vol. 1, 2006)

`docs/references/ringe_vol1_pie_to_pgmc.txt`, line 5473:

> "PIE *misdhó- 'reward' (cf. Gk μισθός; Skt mīḍhám 'prize') > PGmc
> *mizdō (cf. OE mēd ~ meord; Goth. mizdo has been remodeled as an
> n-stem)"

Footnote 16 in Ringe vol. 1 promises further discussion in vol. 2 (=R&T).
Ringe also (line 6948) explicitly states:

> "The proposal that Germanic '*ē²' reflects inherited *ey is without
> merit; see Ringe 1984a for fuller discussion."

(I.e. the historical *ē² in mēd cannot be derived from PIE *ey via
monophthongisation — it must be from later z-loss, consistent with
Crist.)

**Position.** Ringe vol. 1 simply notes the doublet without taking a
position on the WGmc mechanism; it defers to vol. 2 (R&T 2014). It
forecloses the Hirt-style "*ē² as inherited diphthong remnant" account.

### 2.13 Ringe & Taylor, *The Development of Old English* (vol. 2, 2014)

`docs/references/ringe_taylor_linguistic_history_vol2.txt`. Two
discussions:

**(a)** Line 5346 (Ch. 3, on rhotacism):

> "PGmc \*mizdō 'reward' (Goth. mizdō, remodelled as an n-stem) >
> PWGmc \*mizdu > OE meord ~ mēd, OF mēde ~ mide, OS mēda, OHG miata."

Followed by a general statement (line 5358):

> "Though the usual outcome is clearly r, we occasionally find loss of
> *z with compensatory lengthening of the preceding vowel, and it is
> striking that that vowel is always *i, which is sometimes lowered
> when lengthened (cf. Crist 2001: 102-3)."

**(b)** Line 16374 (later chapter, on OE outcomes):

> "PGmc \*mizdō 'reward' (extended as an n-stem in Goth. mizdo) >
> PWGmc \*mizdu (OF mēde, OS mēda, OHG miata) > OE meord ~ mēd."

**Position.** R&T 2014 follow Crist: rhotacism is the regular WGmc
outcome (giving *meord*); z-loss with comp. lengthening is **occasional
and conditioned by following \*i** (giving *mēd*). They are
non-committal on whether this is "Crist's-Law-regular within
Ingvaeonic" or genuinely sporadic — the description "occasionally"
suggests the latter framing — but they cite Crist 2001 for the
i-conditioning observation.

The R&T framing is the **immediate scholarly source** the project's FST
chronology was built against (cf. project DEV_NOTES §3, §6, §15).

### 2.14 Hogg, *A Grammar of Old English*, vol. 1 (1992)

`docs/references/hogg_vol1.txt`. Searched for "mizd", "meord", "mēd
reward", and other obvious tokens. **Hogg vol. 1 does not appear to
discuss the *meord*/*mēd* doublet explicitly.** General statements on
relevant changes:

§2.4.1 (line 2226): "Gmc /z/ yielded /r/ in intervocalic position in
Old English (rhotacism, for the process compare Lat. flos/floris
'flower'), but in final position it is generally lost." Standard
treatment, no doublet discussion.

§breaking (line 5062 ff.): general rule "*i > io, *e > eo, *æ > ea
before l+C, r+C, h", with Hogg's epenthetic-glide / velarised-liquid
analysis; no example of *meord* or comment on *mēd*.

The dossier `mismatch_dossier_mizdo.md` claims an explicit Hogg §2.66
quotation: "Gmc /z/ yielded /r/ in intervocalic position in Old English
(rhotacism), but in final position it is generally lost." The wording
matches what I found at line 2226. **The mismatch_dossier's claim that
this is §2.66 specifically is plausible but I have not been able to
verify the section number from the OCR of `hogg_vol1.txt`.** The
content is correct in any case.

**Position.** Hogg vol. 1 covers rhotacism and breaking generally and
does **not** weigh in on *meord*/*mēd* specifically. (The dossier's
implicit reading of Hogg as supporting "rhotacism-then-breaking gives
*meord*" is consistent with §123 of Bülbring/Brunner/Campbell but not
something Hogg himself states for this lexeme.)

### 2.16 Mees, "The Dziedzice inscription and West Germanic rhotacism" (2020)

`docs/references/mees_2020_dziedzice_inscription_wgmc_rhotacism.txt`,
lines 459–460:

> "The variation between Old English meord and mēd 'reward, meed'
> (< *mizdō) similarly suggests a West Germanic dialectal connection
> between *z-loss and rhotacism (Crist 2001: 101-5)."

Lines 552–556:

> "The differing treatment of inherited *z before *d in West Germanic
> forms such as Old English meord and mēd 'reward, meed' also
> suggests that the retention of sibilantic pronunciations preserved
> intervocalically in onomastic elements such as -gaisus is only
> relevant to the final stage of West Germanic rhotacism — i.e. that
> the rhotacism of *z before *d was an earlier development in
> Suebian/Alemannic than its rhotacism (or deletion) in other
> environments."

**Position.** Mees treats the *meord*/*mēd* doublet as **direct
evidence** that the chronology of *z-loss vs. rhotacism varied
**dialectally and by environment**. His specific contribution is to
push **rhotacism before \*d** earlier than rhotacism intervocalically
in some WGmc dialects, supported by the Dziedzice runic *hurd-* (early
2nd–3rd c. AD). For *mēd*/*meord*, this implies the doublet preserves
two snapshots of an in-progress dialect-level lenition of \*-zd-.
This is a **dialect-chronology** account, compatible with Crist but
not requiring his Ingvaeonic-internal sound law.

### 2.17 Kilday, "Crist's Law, Smith's Law, and English *wizen*" (2024 draft)

`docs/references/kilday_2024_crists_law_smiths_law_wizen.txt`.
Critical reassessment of Crist's Law.

Abstract (line 7):

> "Investigation shows that Crist's Law \*-(e/i)z(d/n)- > \*-(ē/ī)(d/n)-
> operated in Old Saxon and Old Frisian, but not directly in Old
> English, so it should not be ascribed to the Ingvaeonic stage."

On the *mēd* problem specifically (lines 47–67):

> "Complications immediately arise with the word for 'reward, payment'.
> The usual West Saxon form of this word is mēd, and poetic Modern
> English meed suggests that mēd was also the Mercian form. However
> meord (once written by error meorð) and meard are more common in
> Northumbrian, whose scribes typically confused -eo- and -ea-. Other
> West Germanic languages agree with mēd in deleting the \*z with
> compensative lengthening (against Gothic mizdō, and locally with
> *-izd- > *-ezd- first): Old Frisian mēde, mīde, meide; Old Saxon
> mēda, mieda; Old High German mēta, miata, mieta. Since there are
> no comparable examples of secondary \*z-deletion in OHG, Crist
> considered mēta to be a loanword from Ingvaeonic … His explanation
> for OE meord beside mēd is as follows: 'Continental Pre-OE was
> originally spoken in Jutland at the northern periphery of the
> Ingvaeonic continuum; so perhaps the application of the rule in
> polysyllabic words did not spread this far north. …' In other
> words, Crist was compelled to invent a new protolanguage,
> Peripheral Northern Ingvaeonic, spoken at the very tip of Jutland,
> which escaped his rule, and from which OE meord was, in effect,
> borrowed."

Kilday's counterproposal (lines 71–92):

> "Firstly, if OHG mēta can be an unremarkable loanword from
> Ingvaeonic … then so can Proto-OE *mēda > OE mēd be a loanword from
> the Saxon-Frisian area, with meord the regular OE outcome of
> inherited PGmc *mizdō-. That is, Crist's 'rule', which is actually
> a 'soundlaw', deleted *z between a high vowel and a dental with
> compensative lengthening only in Saxono-Frisia (or part of it),
> after the opportunistic band of Angles, Saxons, and Jutes (or most
> of them) had already settled in Britain. … The OE doublet
> meord/mēd is comparable to the one in Early Modern Dutch involving
> 'hards of flax', he(e)rde (Kiliaen) against hēde (Hexham), the
> latter obviously borrowed from East Frisian. The distribution of
> mēd against meord/meard follows from Wessex (and Mercia) trading
> on a greater scale with the Saxons and Frisians than Northumbria
> did."

**Position.** Kilday flips Crist on his head. The **regular OE
outcome** is *meord* (rhotacism + breaking, the Bülbring/Brunner/
Campbell account). *mēd* is a **continental loanword** from Saxono-
Frisia. The *z-loss + comp. lengthening law (Crist's Law) is real,
but it is **OS/OFris-internal**, not pan-Ingvaeonic and not OE.

This is also the first source in the chronological survey to give
weight to the **WS/Mercian vs. Northumbrian** distribution: he reports
*meord* and *meard* as more frequent in Northumbrian (where scribes
"typically confused -eo- and -ea-"). I have not separately verified
this distribution claim; the cited primary attestations in §1 above
are all from Anglian-leaning texts (Bede translation, Phoenix,
Gregory's Dialogues), consistent with Kilday but not strictly proving
his Northumbrian skew over a Mercian one.

### 2.18 Kroonen, *Etymological Dictionary of Proto-Germanic* (2013)

`docs/references/etymological_dictionary_of_proto_germanic_kroonen.txt`,
line 21890 (s.v. *mizdō(n)-*):

> "*mizdō(n)- f. 'reward' — Go. mizdō f. 'id.', OE mēd, meord f.
> 'id.', E meed, OFri. mēde f. 'id.', OS mēda f. 'id.', MDu. miede f.
> 'id.', OHG miata f. 'id.', G Miete f. 'rent' < *misdʰ-eh₂- (IE) …
>
> An IE compound of the root *mi(s)- 'to exchange (gifts)' and *dʰh₁-
> 'to put'. The PGm. form *mizdō- developed into WGm. *mīdō-
> (*mēdō-) due to (sporadic) loss of z before dentals, which is the
> source of the r-less forms OS mēda, mieda, OFri. mēde, mide, OHG
> miata. The dialectal distribution of z-loss is rather patchy,
> however, cf. OS linon 'to learn' vs. OHG lernōn < *liznōn-, and
> Old English preserved a (dialectally dependent?) doublet mēd:
> meord."

Earlier (line 1351, in Kroonen's introductory chapter on PGmc *ē²):

> "In West Germanic, additional cases of secondary PGm. *ē² arose by
> the occasional loss of *z after *i in some cases. The evidence for
> this loss is patchy, and the phonetic conditioning of the loss
> remains unclear. Perhaps it occurred only after i and before
> dentals."

**Position.** Kroonen explicitly **rejects** the Hirt PGmc-level
doublet: the *ē² is **secondary**, not inherited. He locates the
sound change in **WGmc** (post-Gothic split) and characterises it as
**sporadic** with **patchy dialectal distribution**. The OE
*mēd*:*meord* pair is, for Kroonen, a doublet preserved **within OE**
because WGmc sporadic z-loss did not pick the same lexical victims
across all dialects.

## 3. Synthesis: which authors support which hypothesis?

Recall the four hypotheses identified in the task brief:

- **(a)** Sporadic \*z-loss in this lexeme (one regular + one
  sporadic outcome, both within OE/WGmc).
- **(b)** PGmc-level doublet (Hirt/Orel): \*mizdō and \*mēdō are
  both inherited from Proto-Germanic.
- **(c)** Paradigm-cell split with later analogical extension (the
  user's preferred framing): different cells produce different
  lautgesetzlich outcomes within OE/WGmc, then analogy levels each
  variant into a full paradigm in different dialects.
- **(d)** Dialect-borrowing: one form is the regular native outcome,
  the other is a loan from a sister WGmc dialect.

**Key observations.**

1. **Hypothesis (a) — sporadic z-loss within OE/WGmc** is the modal
   position of the modern literature: Campbell, Crist, R&T, Kroonen,
   Fulk all converge on it. The differences between them concern only
   the *regularity*: Crist tries to make it lautgesetzlich within
   Ingvaeonic; Kroonen and Fulk are happy to call it sporadic; R&T
   straddle.

2. **Hypothesis (b) — PGmc-level doublet** is supported only by Hirt
   1931 and Orel 2003. It is **explicitly rejected** by Ringe (1984a,
   referenced in vol. 1) and by Kroonen 2013 (who treats the *ē² as
   secondary). The user's preferred framing partly aligns with (b)
   in spirit (avoiding "sporadic"), but (b) as stated in Hirt/Orel is
   compensatory lengthening at PGmc level, not paradigm-cell split.

3. **Hypothesis (c) — paradigm-cell split with later analogical
   extension** is **not articulated** by any author in the survey, in
   the form the user prefers. The closest analogues in the literature
   are general references to "paradigm levelling" (e.g. Crist's note
   that *mizdō* could have analogical restoration in some forms,
   §2.10/§2.11 above). No author proposes that, e.g., *mizdai*
   (dat.sg.) gives *meorde* lautgesetzlich while *mizdō* (nom.sg.)
   gives *mēd* lautgesetzlich, with later levelling.

4. **Hypothesis (d) — dialect borrowing** is the position of Kilday
   2024 (mēd = continental loan, meord = regular OE) and partly Crist
   2002 (mēd from "south" Ingvaeonic, meord from "north"). It is the
   **newest position in the survey** and represents the most aggressive
   challenge to the standard handbook account.

5. A **fourth/fifth distinct hypothesis** in the literature, not
   strictly captured in (a)–(d): **Mees 2020's chronology-of-rhotacism
   account** treats the doublet as snapshots of a long, dialectally
   uneven WGmc lenition of \*-zd-, with rhotacism-before-\*d earlier
   in some dialects and z-deletion preferred in others. This is
   compatible with (a) but is more ambitious — it makes the doublet
   evidence about the **dating** of WGmc rhotacism, not just about
   sporadic lexical exceptions.

## 4. Crist's Law evaluation

**Crist's Law as formulated:** \*z > ∅ / {\*i,\*e} _ \$ with comp.
lengthening of preceding vowel, regular within Ingvaeonic (= OE +
OS + OFris), pre-rhotacism.

**Does it cover \*mizdō?** Yes — the input \*mizdō has \*i preceding
\*z preceding (after final-vowel apocope) the \$/syllable boundary,
satisfying Crist's environment. The predicted output is \*mīzdō →
\*mīdō with comp. lengthening, then \*mīdō → \*mēdō by lowering of
long \*ī, then OE *mēd* by apocope of the heavy-stem ō-stem. This
matches *mēd* exactly.

**The problem.** Crist's Law also predicts \*meord should be
\**mēd* — i.e. Crist's Law cannot be both regular within Ingvaeonic
AND produce both members of the OE doublet. Crist 2002 §9 and §10
acknowledges this and proposes the "Continental Pre-OE in Jutland"
dialect-mixture solution.

**Kilday 2024's argument.** Kilday rejects the Jutland sub-dialect
solution as ad hoc ("Peripheral Northern Ingvaeonic … which escaped
his rule"). Instead, Kilday confines Crist's Law to **OS and OFris**:
the law is **NOT directly OE**. Under Kilday's account:

- \*mizdō → OE *meord* by regular rhotacism + breaking (no Crist's
  Law in OE).
- \*mizdō → OS *mēda*, OFris *mēde*, etc. by Crist's Law (operating
  in Saxono-Frisia ca. 500 AD).
- OE *mēd* is then a Saxono-Frisian loan, transmitted via WS/Mercian
  trade contacts.

**Evaluation.**

1. Kilday's argument that OHG *mēta* must be a loan from Ingvaeonic
   (since Crist's Law cannot be OHG-internal) is strong; that
   already establishes a precedent for trade-loan transmission of
   this particular lexeme.

2. Kilday's geographical argument (Wessex/Mercia have closer
   continental contact than Northumbria, so *mēd* dominates the
   south while *meord* persists in the Anglian-leaning north) fits
   the **directly attested distribution** in §1: every directly
   attested *meorde*/*meorda* is from an Anglian-leaning text.
   But the WS prose dominance of *mēd* could also fit the standard
   sporadic-z-loss account (with WS having lost \*z in this lexeme
   regularly).

3. The argument is not fully decisive. Crist's i-conditioning
   observation (z-loss ONLY after \*i, never after back vowels) is
   striking and suggests phonological regularity rather than
   borrowing — borrowed words don't have phonological constraints on
   their inputs. Kilday addresses this only obliquely.

**Project consequence.** The directly attested OE oblique forms
(*meorde*, *meorda*) are all consistent with Kilday's account: they
preserve the regular OE rhotacism + breaking outcome. The WS *mēd*
is consistent with EITHER Crist (regular-Ingvaeonic) OR Kilday
(Saxono-Frisian loan). The data alone underdetermines the choice.

## 5. FST-probe relevance

The compiled FST cascade currently produces (per `DEV_NOTES.md`
§17.24.11):

| PGmc input | FST output |
|------------|------------|
| `mizdō`    | meord      |
| `mizdai`   | meorde     |
| `mizdōz`   | meorde     |
| `mizdōn`   | meorde     |
| `mizdą`    | meord      |

The relevant pre-OE rules in the cascade are: PGmcRhotacism (\*z → \*r
intervocalically and before voiced obstruent), OEBreaking (\*i → \*eo
before \*r+C), and the heavy-stem ō-stem apocope.

**Interaction with the literature.**

1. The FST output `meord` from `mizdō` is the **Bülbring/Brunner/
   Campbell §123 lautgesetzlich pathway**. It is also the regular
   pathway under **Kilday 2024's** account.

2. The FST output `meorde` from `mizdai` matches the **directly
   attested** OE form (Bede 4.17, Phoenix l. 17, GD 227.16). This is
   the strongest result: a primary-text-attested form derived
   lautgesetzlich from a defensible PGmc dat.sg. input.

3. The FST does **not** model z-loss before dentals (Crist's Law /
   Kroonen's "sporadic z-loss"). To produce *mēd* lautgesetzlich
   from \*mizdō, the cascade would need either:
   - an Ingvaeonic-or-Saxono-Frisian z-loss rule (Crist/Kilday),
   - or a PGmc-level alternative input \*mēdō (Hirt/Orel),
   - or an OE-internal sporadic z-loss applied to specific lexemes.

4. The FST output of `meorde` from BOTH `mizdai` (dat.sg.) and
   `mizdōz` (gen.sg.) is striking: it means the **paradigm-cell
   targeting** approach (the user's preferred framing under
   hypothesis (c)) gets a free win for the oblique cells. The
   contrast with `mizdō → meord` is real but is the contrast between
   nom.sg. and obliques, not between two different roots.

5. **An observation the literature does not make.** The FST output
   shows that *meord* and *meorde* differ NOT because of a
   competing sound change (z-loss vs. rhotacism) but simply because
   of paradigm-cell-conditioned vowel quality in the inflectional
   ending. If we accept the FST, *meord* is the regular nom.sg.
   outcome, and the doublet's "other form" *mēd* must therefore be
   accounted for **outside** the cascade — by borrowing (Kilday) or
   by sporadic z-loss (Crist/Kroonen/R&T) or by PGmc-level
   alternative input (Hirt/Orel).

The FST is, in other words, **agnostic on hypotheses (a), (b), (d)**
but **already correctly implements** the regular rhotacism +
breaking pathway for both nom.sg. and obliques.

## 6. Recommendation for TSV row 752 (id 2124)

Given:

- The **directly attested OE forms** are oblique: *meorde* (dat.sg.),
  *meorda* (gen.pl.). The bare nom.sg. *meord* is reconstructed.
- The **FST already produces *meorde* lautgesetzlich** from PGmc
  dat.sg. \*mizdai.
- The literature is **divided** on the analysis of *mēd*: most authors
  (Campbell, Crist, R&T, Kroonen, Fulk) treat it as the irregular
  product of sporadic-or-regular z-loss + comp. lengthening, but
  Kilday 2024 makes a defensible case that *mēd* is a continental
  loan and *meord* is the regular OE outcome.
- The user's preferred framing (paradigm-cell split + analogical
  extension) is **not articulated in the literature in this form**
  for this etymon, but is compatible with the FST's behaviour.

**Recommendation (with explicit uncertainty).**

I recommend the project adopt the **paradigm-cell-targeting solution**
proposed in `DEV_NOTES.md` §17.24.11:

1. Set TSV row 752 PROTOFORM = `mizdai` (dat.sg.).
2. Set TSV row 752 target = `meorde`.
3. Both PROTOFORM input and target are **directly attested** (the
   first as a defensible PGmc dat.sg. form; the second as the literal
   OE attestation in Bede 4.17 / Phoenix 17 / GD 227.16).
4. The FST is **already correct** for this pair — no rule changes
   needed.
5. The mismatch between this row and the modern lemma "meed" /
   handbook headword *mēd* is recorded in a TSV note as a
   **dialectal-doublet case** parallel to §17.21 (swustor/swester),
   citing this review.

This solution has the following virtues:

- It uses the **actually attested OE form** rather than a
  lexicographer's reconstructed nom.sg.
- It is **agnostic** on the *mēd* mechanism — it does not commit the
  project to Crist's Law, Kilday's loan account, or Hirt's
  PGmc-level doublet. Any of those can be the eventual solution to
  the *mēd* question.
- It is **lowest-disturbance**: no FST changes, no rule additions,
  no PGmc-input-level analogical hacks.
- It matches the **paradigm-cell-targeting precedent** the project
  has already accepted for §17.16 (spere/speoru, with target the
  back-umlauted plural form) and §17.21 (swustor/swester, with
  Anglian relic targeting).

**Honest uncertainties:**

- I am NOT confident enough in any one of (a)–(d) to recommend
  changing the TSV target back to *mēd* with a sporadic-z-loss
  pre-OE rule. The literature does not converge.
- Kilday's loan account is the most economical and the only one that
  fully explains the i-conditioning of the WGmc \*z-loss (since
  borrowed lexical items inherit their phonology), but it is from a
  2024 *draft* (not yet a peer-reviewed publication on the file in
  the repo) and has not been independently evaluated.
- The user's "paradigm-cell split + analogical extension" framing is
  **not stated in the literature for this etymon**. Adopting it as
  the project's analysis would be an original contribution — not
  unreasonable given the FST evidence, but it should be flagged as
  such.
- The Hirt PGmc-level doublet (hypothesis (b)) is **explicitly
  rejected** by Ringe and Kroonen on independent grounds (the alleged
  PGmc *ē² is uniformly secondary in their analyses). I would NOT
  recommend adopting it.

**Strongest single recommendation.** Adopt the paradigm-cell switch
(PROTOFORM `mizdai` → target `meorde`) for row 752, with a TSV note
citing this review and §17.24.10–11. Treat *mēd* as a separate
lexical-doublet item for a future TSV row if needed (PROTOFORM
`mēdō` or `mēdai`, target `mēd` or `mēde`).
