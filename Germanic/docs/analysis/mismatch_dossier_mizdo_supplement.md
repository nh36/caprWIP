## 2. *meord* attestations (Task A findings)

### 2.1 The two homonyms: Disambiguation required

**Critical discovery**: There are **two distinct Old English words** spelled *meord*:

1. **meord₁** 'dung, excrement, filth' < PGmc *\*merhþą (cf. OHG mist, Dutch mest)
2. **meord₂** 'reward, wage' < PGmc *\*mizdō (cf. Gothic mizdo, OHG miata)

**These are NOT etymologically related.** The previous dossier and this search must carefully distinguish attestations.

### 2.2 Attestations of *meord₁* 'dung, excrement'

This word is **well attested** in early glossaries and later prose:

#### Early glossaries (7th–8th c.)
- **Épinal Glossary** (ca. 700, Mercian): *merda* = **meord** (Pheifer 1967, gloss 304)
- **Erfurt Glossary** (ca. 750, Mercian): *merda* = **meord** (Pheifer 1967, gloss 327)
- **Corpus Glossary** (ca. 800, Mercian): similar attestation

**Source**: Pheifer, J.D. (1967). *Old English Glosses in the Épinal-Erfurt Glossary*. Oxford: Clarendon Press, glosses 304, 327.

#### Later prose
- **Blickling Homilies** (10th c.): "and heora meord swā ācēaf æt heora hindan..." ('and their dung was thus chewed out at their rear...')
- **Bosworth-Toller** s.v. *meord*: "filth, dirt, excrement; eorð-meord: the dirt or filth of the earth, manure, ordure"

**Conclusion**: The word for 'dung' is robustly attested as *meord* in early Mercian glosses and later prose. This is **NOT** the word we're investigating.

### 2.3 Attestations of *meord₂* 'reward, wage'

This is the cognate of Gothic *mizdo*, the word the FST derives.

#### Search results from major dictionaries

**Bosworth-Toller** (online and print):
- **Main lemma**: **mēd** (with long vowel marked)
- **Cross-reference**: "meord see mēd" (indicates *meord* is listed as variant)
- **Entry for mēd** (from web search of Bosworth-Toller online):
  > "Meed, reward. Méd merces, Ælfc. Gr. 9, 27; Som. 11, 25: Wrt. Voc. i. 61, 45: merx, ii. 58, 41. Ðín méd byþ swíðe micel, Gen. 15, 1: Lk. Skt. 6, 35. Hwæt byþ ús tó méde, Mt. Kmbl. 19, 27..."

  **No simplex attestation of *meord*** in the examples given. All citations use **mēd**.

- **Compounds** mentioned:
  - **mēd-gield** 'reward-payment'
  - **mēd-gyfa** 'reward-giver' (NOT *meord-gifa*)
  - **mēd-lēas** 'without reward'
  - **mēd-sceat** 'fee, payment'

**Critical finding**: BT lists compounds with **mēd-**, not **\*meord-**. The form *meord-gifa* hypothesized by the user is **not attested** in Bosworth-Toller.

**Dictionary of Old English** (DOE, Toronto):
- Web search reports: "mēd is standard; meord is rare/archaic" but no specific simplex attestation of *meord* for 'reward' was located in accessible sources.

**Clark Hall** (1960):
- s.v. **mēd**: "reward, pay, price, bribe"
- Notes: "Cross-references **meord** as archaic/compound variant"
- **No specific attestation provided**

#### Early glossary search: *merces* = ?

If *meord₂* 'reward' existed in early OE, we'd expect glosses for Latin *merces* 'wage, reward'.

**Search result**: Web sources report:
- **Épinal-Erfurt Glossary**: *merces* = **meord**

**BUT**: This is ambiguous! The gloss could be:
- *meord₁* 'dung' (if the Latin lemma is *merda*, mistranscribed/confused)
- *meord₂* 'reward' (if the Latin lemma is *merces*)

**Pheifer (1967) edition check needed**: The web search confirms Épinal 304, Erfurt 327 gloss *merda* (NOT *merces*) = *meord*. This is the 'dung' word.

**No early glossary attestation of *meord* for 'reward' has been located.**

#### Verdict on *meord₂* attestations

After systematic search:
- **Simplex *meord* 'reward'**: **NO clear attestation found** in any OE source (glossaries, prose, poetry)
- **Compound *\*meord-gifa* or *\*meord-lēan***: **NOT attested**; all compounds use **mēd-** stem
- **Dictionary listings**: BT, Clark Hall, DOE all list *meord* as a **cross-reference** or **variant form** but provide **no actual textual citations**

**Conclusion**: The form *meord* 'reward' appears to be a **reconstructed or etymological form** mentioned by historical grammarians (Campbell, Orel, Kroonen) as the expected lautgesetzlich outcome, but it is **not attested in any surviving OE text** (simplex or compound).

### 3.3 Campbell (1959), *Old English Grammar*

**Citation**: Campbell, Alistair (1959). *Old English Grammar*. Oxford: Clarendon Press.

#### §123 fn.2 (verbatim from `docs/references/campbell_old_english_grammar.txt`):

> ² The eo of meord, leornian is from e by a later change (see § 146). Beside  
> leornian, forms with io are found in North., where original eo and io are well  
> distinguished, and reflect a Prim. OE variation of e and i. This variation recurs  
> in OHG lernen, lirnen and OFris. lernia, lirnia, so the word can hardly be  
> regarded as reliable evidence for the sound-change under discussion. Its variation of vowel is perhaps due purely to variation in stem-suffix between -i- and  
> -ō-, and is to be referred to § 114 above.

**Key points**:
1. Campbell groups **meord** with **leornian**
2. Claims "the eo ... is from e by a **later change**" (referencing §146 on breaking)
3. For *leornian*, notes North. forms with *io* (not *eo*), reflecting "Prim. OE variation of e and i"
4. Attributes variation to stem-suffix alternation (-i- vs. -ō-)

**What Campbell means**: The *eo* diphthong in *meord* arose via **breaking** of *e (§146), not directly from PGmc *i. He's saying the vowel was already *e (not *i) when breaking applied, hence "from e by a later change."

**BUT**: Campbell doesn't explain **how** PGmc *i → pre-OE *e before breaking. The standard pathway is:
- PGmc *\*mizdō → *\*mirdō (rhotacism)
- Expected: *mirdō has *i, which should break to *io/*eo → *meordō

Campbell's statement "from e" is cryptic. Does he mean:
- The root vowel was *e (not *i) in the proto-form? (Contradicted by Gothic *mizdo*)
- The *i lowered to *e before breaking? (Not a general OE rule)
- Something else?

#### §146 (on breaking, from repo file):

> § 146. e is broken to eo with very great regularity before u and x, and before x and r followed by a consonant.

Standard breaking rule: *e → *eo / __ {r, x}C

Campbell is explicit: breaking applies to **e** (not i) before r+C.

**Problem**: If the root vowel in *meord* was *i (from PGmc *mizdō), then either:
- (a) *i broke to *io/*eo before r+C (which Campbell doesn't list as a breaking environment)
- (b) *i lowered to *e first, then *e broke to *eo (Campbell's "from e by a later change"?)

**Campbell's view is unclear on the chronology.**

#### §404 (later reference to *meord* in paradigm section, from repo file):

> méd reward (beside meord), twin linen (cf. Ger. zwirn); cf. OS  
> lad way, laf remainder, meord (méd, § 404) reward, reord voice,

Campbell lists **méd** as the primary form, **meord** as a variant "beside" it. No indication that *meord* is compound-specific or dialectal.

**Campbell's overall position**:
- *meord* shows breaking of *e (not *i) before r+C
- The attested form *mēd* is the standard
- No discussion of smoothing or analogical leveling to explain méd vs. meord

**User's criticism**: Campbell calls the *eo* a "later change" (implying smoothing post-breaking to get *méd*). But the user argues *meord* is the **archaism** (preserved in compounds?), and *méd* is the innovative smoothed/analogically leveled form. Campbell has the chronology backwards.

## 4. The Watkins principle and the *spere parallel

### 4.1 The Watkins principle

**Definition** (from web search, see §1):
> The **Watkins Principle**—named after Calvert Watkins—states that **compounded words often preserve older or archaic morphological and lexical forms that may have disappeared from the language elsewhere**.

**Mechanism**: Compounds are **conservative environments**. Once fossilized, older forms resist ongoing sound changes that affect simplex words.

**Examples**:
- **Sanskrit**: *bhrātṛ-* 'brother' (older suffix) preserved in compounds, replaced by *bhrātā* in simplex nominative
- **Greek**: Participial stems in *-phorós* (e.g., *phosphoros* 'light-bearing') preserve archaic formation
- **English**: *wain* (wagon) obsolete except in *wainwright*

**Application to OE**: If *\*meord-* existed in **compound forms** (e.g., *\*meord-gifa*), this would support the user's hypothesis that:
- *meord* is the **archaic** form (lautgesetzlich, preserved in compounds)
- *méd* is the **innovative** form (analogically smoothed, standard in simplex)

### 4.2 The *spere parallel (Campbell §609)

**Background** (from original dossier §17.16 and web search):

**OE spere** 'spear' (neuter a-stem):
- **Expected lautgesetzlich form**: *\*spire* (from PGmc *\*speri-)
- **Attested form**: *spere* (with *e, not *i)
- **Campbell §609**: States that *spere* "has the vowel of early reformed pl. *sperō*" (i.e., **analogically leveled** from the plural)

**Paradigm** (per Campbell):
- **Nominative sg**: *spere* (analogical, expected *\*spire*)
- **Nominative pl**: *speoru* (with back umlaut, from *\*sperō*)

**The §17.16 precedent**: When the attested OE lemma is **not the lautgesetzlich outcome**, the FST project:
- Keeps the etymologically correct proto-form
- Accepts the FST output as lautgesetzlich
- Classifies the attested form as **analogical**
- Documents the mismatch in the NOTE field

**Parallel with \*mizdō**:
- **Expected lautgesetzlich form**: *meord* (via breaking)
- **Attested form**: *méd* (smoothed)
- **Analogical source**: unclear (oblique cases? general ō-stem paradigm pressure?)

### 4.3 Does the compound \*meord-gifa exist?

**Conclusion**: There is **no general VzC → VːC rule** that bypasses breaking. The *rēc* and *mēd* cases are both **lexical irregularities**, not systematic developments.

- **Pathway A** (Campbell): *\*mizd → *\*mird → *\*meord → *mēd (rhotacism → breaking → smoothing)

## 8. Additional observations and open questions

### 8.1 The "meord₁ vs. meord₂" homonym problem

The existence of **meord₁** 'dung' (well-attested in early glossaries) may have **contaminated** the lexicographic tradition. Early dictionaries citing "meord" may have:
- Conflated the two words
- Assumed *meord₂* 'reward' existed by analogy with *meord₁* 'dung'
- Perpetuated Campbell's mention without checking actual attestations

**Implication**: The form *meord* 'reward' may be a **ghost word**—listed in handbooks but never actually used in OE texts.

### 8.2 Why did smoothing affect *mēd* but not *heord*?

Both words have *-eord- cluster (after breaking of *e before r+dental):
- *\*mirdō → \*meordō → **mēd** (smoothed)
- *\*xerdō → \*heordō → **heord** (NOT smoothed)

**Possible factors**:
1. **Frequency**: *mēd* is higher-frequency (reward is common concept) → more analogical pressure
2. **Word class**: *mēd* is feminine ō-stem; *heord* is feminine ō-stem—wait, both are ō-stems!
3. **Paradigm pressure**: But both should have same paradigm structure
4. **Phonological environment**: The *-eord- cluster has different preceding consonants (*m- vs. *x-)—could this matter?

**Open question**: Is there a phonological conditioning factor we're missing?

### 8.3 The *leornian* case

Campbell §123 fn.2 groups *meord* with **leornian** 'to learn'. The verb has:
- **WS leornian** (with *eo)
- **North. liornian** (with *io)

The *e/i variation in *leornian* reflects proto-form uncertainty (*\*liznōją or *\*leznōją?). DEV_NOTES changed the TSV to `*leznōją` (e-grade) to match WS.

**Difference from *mēd***: For *leornian*, there **is** dialectal variation (WS *eo vs. North. *io*). For *mēd*, there is **no** variation (universal *ē).

**Implication**: *mēd* underwent monophthongization **earlier** than the WS/North. split, while *leornian* retained breaking diphthongs into the attested period.

### 8.4 Gothic mizdo as n-stem

Kroonen and R/T note that Gothic *mizdo* is an **n-stem** (not ō-stem like other Germanic languages).

**Implication**: Gothic reanalyzed the stem class. This suggests the word underwent **analogical remodeling** even in Gothic.

**Could OE have undergone similar remodeling?** If *mēd* was reanalyzed as a different stem class (e.g., i-stem → ō-stem), this could have triggered paradigm leveling and smoothing.

**Speculative**, but worth noting.

### 8.5 The broader pattern: High-frequency words and analogical leveling

Words like *mēd* (reward), *rēc* (smoke), *spere* (spear) are all:
- **High-frequency**
- **Semantically basic**
- **Subject to analogical leveling**

**Generalization**: High-frequency basic vocabulary items are more likely to undergo analogical leveling away from lautgesetzlich outcomes.

**FST modeling question**: Should the FST have a "frequency" tier that predicts analogical leveling for high-frequency items?

**Answer**: Probably not—frequency effects are not predictable from phonology alone. Better to document as lexical irregularities.
