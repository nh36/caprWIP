# Place adverbs, inherited *r* and the limits of an ordering argument

## Identity

This is a research-only investigation of Old English *hēr* ‘here’,
*hwǣr* ‘where’ and *þǣr* ‘there’, on branch `update`, based on
`c92cbd9d541785986998ab2ab99a416f87da4f68`. It follows the identity,
question, diagnosis, literature, historical-analysis, verdict, propagation
and residue headings of the research adjudication template. It is not a
second production adjudication of SC015. The released unified
`PNWGmcILowering`, its position and its selected *leorn-* forms remain
authoritative.

The proposed lexical identifiers 2333–2335 are research labels only.
There are still 387 selected Old English rows, not 390. Comparative
Gothic, Norse, Frisian and continental forms below are evidence for the
Old English histories, not targets for their own computational pipelines.

## Question

The falsifiable hypothesis is that independently justified short
antecedents of the three adverbs, together with a general monosyllabic
lengthening before inherited final *r*, require early *r*-lowering and
distinguish an early unified SC015 from an early *r*-only component.
A subsidiary hypothesis is that Hill's close-mid notation requires a new
short-vowel symbol in CAPR.

The hypothesis fails in its strong form if a source-supported alternative
input removes the ordering dependency, if unified and split architectures
have the same relevant predictions, or if existing short-vowel symbols
with a different long output implement the histories without collision.
An experiment can establish those computational facts. It cannot, by
itself, establish the ancestral input, the dialectal scope or the date of
a phonological development.

## Literature and evidence discipline

The core source is Hill (2017), pp. 135–170, read in full by the
independent historical track, with the held native-text PDF used to
check its symbols. The central passages are pp. 137–149 on the
adverbial paradigm and quantity, and pp. 153–158 on lowering and
relative chronology. Ringe (1984), pp. 138–151, was read from original
scanned page images; the held excerpt does not include p. 152.
Ringe–Taylor (2014), pp. 35–37 and 64–66, supplies the strongest
full-paradigm competing analysis in the held material. Its held text
has damaged special characters: explicit prose about quantity is
usable, but missing macrons are not evidence of short vowels.

The dictionary citation reconstructions must be established separately.
The original scans were checked against printed folios: Kroonen (2013),
pp. 225, 263 and 534, treats *hiar*, *hwar* and *þar*; Orel (2003),
pp. 172, 198 and 416, gives *xē₂r* and the alternating
*xwar ~ xwēr*, *þar ~ þēr* entries. The earlier draft cited PDF-sheet
positions as printed pages (Kroonen 265/303/574; Orel 211/237/455);
those citations are corrected here and in the witness/probe files.
These entries are not merely alternative spellings of Hill's earlier
short *hir*. Fulk (2018),
pp. 51–52, places the ‘here’ problem within the multiple proposed
sources of Germanic *ē₂*.

Stiles (2004), especially p. 388 nn. 4–5, and Kortlandt (2006),
pp. 51–54, are pertinent but not independently available as verified
originals in this investigation. Their positions are reported through
the particular consulted authority, not passed off as direct reading.
The same restriction applies to unheld relevant EWA volumes and to
Grønvik's argument cited by Ringe–Taylor. This matters particularly
when one author's rebuttal is the only available account of a rival.

The independent historical handoff initially contained material errors:
it confused high quality with short quantity in Frisian *hīr*, treated
OE *hē* as short and described *meord* as retaining *i*. The lead
rejected those statements before any compilation. Their correction is
part of the provenance, not evidence against the underlying sources.
An attested form, its dictionary normalization, a reconstruction and an
experimental serialization remain different objects throughout.

## Historical analysis

### 1. What is independently attested?

The Old English targets are ordinary attested adverbs, not forms inferred
from a desired model. Clark Hall's dictionary gives *hēr* on p. 155,
*hwǣr* on p. 170 and *ðǣr* on p. 305; the original page scans were
used to check their quantity marks. CAPR's proposed *þǣr* normalizes the
dental spelling, not the vowel or the grammatical identity. A dictionary
entry with a normalized headword attests the lexeme and its lexical
quantity; it is not a diplomatic transcription of every quoted token.

Hill (2017), pp. 142–143, explicitly compares West Saxon *hwǣr,
ðǣr* with Anglian *hwēr, ðēr*. Both sides have long vowels.
The difference is quality, not the presence of lengthening in one
dialect and its absence in another. A concise dictionary's grouping
of texts under one lemma cannot independently prove the absence of
dialect variation. Accordingly, the general dialect comparison rests
on Hill's explicit account; the separately inspected dictionary entries
establish the selected lexemes without pretending to replace a study
of the Gospel or Psalter manuscripts.

The comparative ‘here’ set includes Gothic *hēr*, Old Norse *hér*,
Old English *hēr* and Old High German *hiar* (Hill 2017, p. 137;
Kroonen 2013, p. 225; Orel 2003, p. 172).
The dictionary entries disagree in the Old Frisian spelling: Kroonen
prints unmarked *hir*, while Orel prints *hīr*. This is a real
transcriptional difference, but neither dictionary headword is a
diplomatic manuscript record; the spellings therefore do not
independently settle Old Frisian vowel quantity. Ringe–Taylor (2014),
pp. 35–37, also discuss
Old Saxon high-vowel variants and explicitly regard a short reading
as unlikely. That observation separates two questions: where the vowel
became long, and where it acquired mid quality. A history that explains
only the quantity does not yet explain the distribution of quality.
The corrected historical handoff's claim that Kroonen independently
prints *hīr* is not accepted: its citation used PDF sheet 265 as a
printed folio, while the scanned sheet is printed p. 225 and shows
unmarked *hir*. Only Orel's inspected entry prints the macron.

For ‘where’ and ‘there’, Gothic *ƕar, þar* and Old Norse *hvar,
þar* stand beside the conspicuously long West Germanic adverbs.
Gothic spelling alone does not establish their quantity. Norse short
vowels are real comparative evidence, not an inconvenience that may be
discarded because Old English is long (Hill 2017, pp. 142–148).
Hill supplies a particular morphological account of the discrepancy;
accepting that account is an additional historical inference.

### 2. Three levels of reconstruction

At level A, the comparative citation form, Orel's *xē₂r* already has
a long mid vowel. The experimental `xēr` serializes that dictionary
form in CAPR's existing long-mid inventory; it is not a literal
transcription of the subscript. Kroonen's *hiar* makes a different
etymological claim. Neither should be silently relabelled ‘short *hir*’.
For ‘where’ and ‘there’, the short stem plus *r* is well supported
by comparison and morphology, while Orel's double headwords preserve
the long-vowel problem rather than resolving its date
(Orel 2003, pp. 172, 198, 416; Kroonen 2013, pp. 225, 263, 534).

At level B, the earlier historical antecedent, Hill proposes short
*hir*, parallel to *hwar* and *þar*. The argument is morphological:
the stems of the pronouns combine with the locative/adverbial *r*.
It is not that one may reconstruct short *i* merely because a rule
needs somewhere to apply. The parallel makes the form economical,
but rival analyses of the formation of ‘here’ remain possible
(Hill 2017, pp. 137–141).

At level C, an executable selected input, one must specify both the
historical entry stage and the actual symbols. Experimental `xír`
represents Hill's short antecedent using CAPR's `x` and acute root
vowel. Experimental `xēr` instead submits an already-long comparative
form. They are not two implementations of the same historical claim:
the second no longer tests how ‘here’ acquired its length.
Neither input is adopted by this report.

The distinction also prevents an apparent shortcut with Kroonen's
*hiar*. Writing native `xíar` does not implement the proposed
Germanic *ia/ē₂* merger. It merely feeds a sequence to a transducer
whose existing input grammar does not thereby acquire that historical
merger. A rejected or incorrect output from that diagnostic cannot
refute Kroonen's etymology.

### 3. Hill's positive morphological argument

The strongest part of the lengthening case is not the existence of
three long adverbs in isolation. It is the contrast between
monosyllabic local adverbs and extended directional formations.
Hill compares OHG *hwār, dār, hiar* with short-vowel *hwăra,
hwărōt, dăra, dărōt, hĕra, hĕrōt*
(2017, pp. 140–144). His use of later apocope evidence is intended
to establish quantity independently of spellings that do not reliably
mark it. A phonological generalization therefore has an observable
boundary: the presence of another syllable matters.

The North Germanic comparison is less transparent. Hill argues from
the contrast among inherited local and directional formations in
older Scandinavian material, then proposes levelling of the
‘where/there’ paradigms (2017, pp. 144–146).
This is a specified analogical proposal with a morphological model,
not a general licence to repair any unexpected vowel. Its extension
to all necessary forms remains a premise in the pan-Germanic
reconstruction. The present research cannot convert it into direct
attestation of long Proto-Norse ‘where’ and ‘there’.

Gothic *ƕarjis* and Norse *hverr* provide a different control.
Their extended interrogative formation need not have undergone
monosyllabic lengthening, even when its underlying pronominal stem
is related to *hwar* (Hill 2017, pp. 146–147).
They must not be forced through the Old English cascade and scored
against their Gothic or Norse surfaces. Their value is morphological:
they challenge a formulation that recognizes only a root string and
ignores the second nucleus.

Gothic *hiri* likewise needs its own morphological treatment.
Hill distinguishes it from the local adverb, while Kroonen associates
it with a separate earlier formation and an uncertain additional
suffix (Hill 2017, pp. 139–141; Kroonen 2013, p. 225).
It is not automatically a transparent disyllabic allomorph of *hēr*.
The experimental suite retains it as a comparative pattern, with no
Old English success target and no assertion that its suffix history
has been settled.

These observations support a real monosyllabicity question. They
do not, without the further comparative arguments, independently
select Proto-Germanic over a later West Germanic date.

### 4. Ringe's already-long account

Ringe (1984), pp. 138–143, proposes an already-long high vowel:
an old instrumental in *-ī* acquires adverbial *r*, yielding
phonemic */hīr/*. A lowering of long *ī* before absolute-final
*r* then produces the relevant mid realization. That is neither
Hill's short-*i* lowering nor his monosyllabic lengthening.
Ringe's claim that this final sequence was uniquely instantiated
does not turn the rule into a lexical whitelist; it states a
phonological condition with a very small proposed application domain.

One of Ringe's objections to short *hir* was the asymmetry between
a supposedly all-Germanic lengthening of ‘here’ and a West-Germanic-only
lengthening of ‘where/there’ (1984, p. 139). Hill's later argument
aims directly at that premise: establish that the other two were
also lengthened early, and the objection loses its force
(Hill 2017, pp. 142–148).
The chronological sequence of the publications matters. Ringe's
paper cannot be described as having considered and rejected Hill's
later detailed Scandinavian argument.

The independent attractiveness of the instrumental account depends
on the morphology, not on whether the current CAPR wrapper happens
to accept a long-i input. The already-long probe `xḯr` does not by
itself test Ringe's lowering. A separate Hill-order sensitivity
recipe applies an explicitly hypothesized long-`ī` to `ē` lowering
before final `r`; it does not implement Ringe's instrumental
morphology or establish that rule's historical domain or date.

### 5. Ringe–Taylor and non-shared lengthening

Ringe–Taylor (2014), pp. 35–37, infer short *hir* by the
parallel with the other pronominal stems, but qualify the historical
interpretation of its lengthening and lowering. The high long vowels
in Frisian and some Saxon forms make a simple common lowering
problematic. Their proposed Gothic parallel development is not
evidence that independent developments have been demonstrated;
it is their alternative interpretation of the shared forms.

For ‘where’ and ‘there’, their discussion is explicitly West
Germanic in scope (2014, pp. 64–66). It considers strong deictic
stress and a particular reanalysis involving reduced/allegro forms
and the long vowel of ‘here’. The references to Stiles on p. 388
are to different notes for different points. We have not independently
read those notes and do not expand the secondhand claim into a
newly verified source argument.

Prosodic conditioning is compatible with exceptionless sound change.
However, ‘these three words are emphatic’ is not yet an operational
prosodic condition for a whole reconstructed language. A defensible
proposal must state the stress domain and test other words in it.
The experiments therefore distinguish explicit weak-word contexts,
ordinary stressed citation inputs and synthetic boundary controls.
No variable lexical participation is added to explain away outputs.

This position is the strongest consulted comparator covering the
whole paradigm without Hill's common Proto-Germanic lengthening.
It does not amount to a single fully specified rival executable rule.
Its historical merits and its missing operational detail must both
be stated rather than treating a successful later wrapper as proof
of every proposed prosodic or analogical step.

### 6. Dictionary alternatives and inherited quantity

Kroonen (2013), p. 225, following Kortlandt, derives *hiar*
from a formation in *ki-or* and relates the Germanic vowel to the
*ē₂* reflex set. A formation containing *i* and a following
vowel across a morphological boundary should not be called an
inherited PIE diphthong without further argument. The relevant
claim is the proposed Germanic development and merger.
Kroonen separately invokes monosyllabic lengthening for the West
Germanic ‘where’ forms (p. 263), with ‘there’ cross-referenced
under its own stem-plus-*r* formation (p. 534).
Thus ‘here’ need not acquire its quantity by the same mechanism
as the other two on this account.

Orel's *xē₂r* entry connects the form with a different deeper
reconstruction and cites the long-vowel problem and Ringe's
lowering account (2003, p. 172). Its printed *hīr* differs from
Kroonen's unmarked *hir*, a dictionary-level discrepancy whose
manuscript basis remains unverified here. Fulk's survey likewise shows
that *ē₂* has not had a universally accepted single source
(2018, pp. 51–52).
These authorities justify an already-long comparator.
They do not prove that every reconstructed *ē₂* was phonetically
identical at every stage or that every competing etymology is
equally secure.

Hill's morphological rebuttal is important: the demonstrative
paradigm and the short directional forms have to be reconciled
with the proposed formation of ‘here’ (2017, pp. 139–141).
That is a positive reason to take his short antecedent seriously.
It is not a reason to suppress the dictionary citation forms or
report that they all independently reconstruct short *hir*.

### 7. What the *r/z* comparison establishes

Hill distinguishes lowering from quantity in the *z* examples:
the ‘he’ pronouns and the reward family do not all share the
same later length history (2017, pp. 138–139, 153–158).
OE *hē* is long. OE *meord* has *e*-grade breaking, not a
retained root *i*. OHG *er* has secondary *r* from an earlier
sibilant; it is not an inherited-*r* counterexample in the same
historical environment as *hwar*.

The OE wrapper's particular treatment of final *z* is separate
again. Its rhotacism rule needs a following symbol; it does not
automatically turn bare final *z* into *r*. Consequently the
Old English output of experimental *iz* cannot be interpreted
as an attempted derivation of OHG *er*. The source comparison
establishes a distinction in consonant ancestry; the negative
controls test what the actual wrapper remembers.

If one accepts short *hir* and a common short-vowel lengthening,
then *hir > her > hēr* requires lowering first. Lengthening
*i* first takes the form outside a rule restricted to short
*i*. This is a genuine conditional feeding argument
(Hill 2017, pp. 156–158).
It does not independently date lowering before *z* or *st*.
Nor does it decide whether the executable should contain one
definition, two definitions, or an unchanged later proxy, provided
the historical claims and executable effects are kept separate.

### 8. SC024 is a separate chronology question

The newly lengthened mid vowel of ‘here’ must not be confused
with inherited *ē₁*. CAPR already has different long-vowel
serializations: `ḗ`, consumed by SC024, and `ē`, not consumed
by it. This is a property of the present executable, not a
claim that spelling alone proves a historical phonemic contrast.

Mapping stressed short `é` to `ḗ` before SC024 deliberately
erases the distinction. A wrong result from that experiment
does not establish that lengthening historically followed SC024.
It establishes that this serialization put the vowel in SC024's
input class. Conversely, protecting it with a separate path does
not prove that lengthening preceded SC024.

The three adverbs can potentially date *a*-lengthening relative
to subsequent *ā*-fronting, once their inputs and the fronting
account are adopted. They do not demonstrate an interaction
between newly created close-mid length and inherited-*ē₁*
lowering simply by both containing long vowels.
The existing *dǣd* ‘deed’ selection, with input `dḗdiz`,
is the independent inherited-*ē₁* control throughout the
experiments. Its preservation is necessary but is not, on its
own, proof that every other vowel-class consumer is correct.

one additional mismatch each: selected row 2326 (*you*) changes from
`ēow` to `eoww`, leaving 378 exact matches. The marker survives into

The *hēr* trace identifies the operative divergence directly. With
`a-before-sc024-existing`, `XRLen` changes `*x*í*r` to `*x*ḯ*r`, and
later `PNWGmcILowering` leaves that long vowel untouched; the result is
*hīr*. With `a-after-sc015-existing`, `PNWGmcILowering` first changes
`*x*í*r` to `*x*é*r`, then `XRLen` produces `*x*ē*r` and *hēr*. The
unified early model performs the same short-vowel feeding before
`XRLen`. In the long-*ī* sensitivity recipe, `XRLen` first makes
`*x*ḯ*r`, and the extra `XRLongILower` then changes it to `*x*ē*r`.
Thus the output distinguishes the simple lengthen-first/short-only
model, but not Hill's two stated sequences once the additional
long-*ī* lowering is allowed.

The aligned corpus comparison locates the first intermediate divergence
at `XRLen` for the 59 rows changed by relocating unified lowering; their
final outputs all converge with baseline. The split-*r* model differs
from the unified early model on seven selected rows at that point, also
without final changes. The pairwise receipt preserves these full traces
rather than inferring a historical chronology from event positions.

The baseline has no `XRLen` firings among the 387 selected rows. The
supplementary controls therefore carry the direct evidence for this
candidate process: `*wiraz` yields *wer* under inherited-*r* lowering;
`*fiskaz` retains *i* as *fisċ*; and the *reward* control yields *meorde*.
The directional forms do not undergo the monosyllabic-final-*r*
lengthening. Late placement also overapplies to weak-final controls and
cannot distinguish inherited from post-rhotacism *r*. Those failures
limit a generalized late rule; they do not adjudicate the competing
adverb etymologies. The proposed hiatus probe `xíar` is rejected by the
current wrapper, so it provides no experiment result about Kroonen's
reported *hiar* analysis.

### 10. Adjudication and corpus consequence

The historical gate does not license treating *hēr* as direct evidence
for a short Proto-Germanic input. OE *hēr*, *hwǣr*, and *þǣr* (with the
recorded dialectal variants) are attested targets; Hill's early short
*hir* is a morphological reconstruction. Dictionary citation spellings
and Gothic comparanda do not independently resolve the relevant
quantity, while the Old Frisian spelling discrepancy remains open.
The evidence table therefore keeps attested forms, citation forms,
reconstructions, and experiment inputs in separate fields.

If Hill's short antecedent is accepted, adding *hēr* as a source-backed
positive witness would reject the simple order in which lengthening
precedes the only short-*i* lowering. It would not distinguish
lowering-before-lengthening from Hill's lengthening-then-separate-long-*ī*
lowering. The *where* and *there* probes confirm their *ā*-based outcomes
but do not constrain that *i* chronology. Consequently these adverbs
would add a conditional contrast, not a decisive new constraint on
unified versus split SC015 architecture. The experiment's `xír` is not
a justified new Proto-Germanic corpus input; no canonical row should be
added on its basis.

**Recommendation:** retain the protected production baseline and do not
adopt a rule, move SC015, or add corpus targets from this investigation
alone. Conditional on Hill's short antecedent, lowering before
lengthening is the economical working analysis because it uses the
existing short-*i* process; it is not historically proven over Hill's
other sequence. Adopting the latter would require independent evidence
for the long-*ī* lowering's domain, chronology, and interaction with
other long-*ī* inputs, as well as source-verified early input evidence.
The present output data cannot choose between those two accounts.
