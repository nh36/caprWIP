# Secondary stress: contested propositions and the evidence

This is research at `3cb109c981c8868bf383172e2be06cdc88c00fb0`, not a
replacement for any approved history. The supplied revised essay proposes the
questions; it is not evidence that their answers are correct.

## 1. Does the vowel of *boga* have an early regular explanation?

Yes. Campbell (1959: 43, §§115–116), Hogg (1992, held 2011 reissue:
54–55, §§3.10–3.11), Fulk (2018: 56–57, §4.3), and Ringe and Taylor
(2014: 27–29) describe lowering of stressed short *u* before a nonhigh
vowel in the next syllable. Intervening *j* and nasality restrict it.
Ringe and Taylor explicitly include the ancestor of *boga* (p. 28).
Kroonen (2013: 82, *bugan-*) identifies a masculine n-stem and the
comparative noun family. The stem citation is not itself a reconstructed
nominative ending.

Colman (2011: 95–97) makes the early explanation of ordinary *boga*
explicit. The independent noun is therefore not evidence that late
unstressed lowering supplied the compound's vowel. CAPR's existing
`*búgô` and compound `*-bùgô` encode the n-stem nominative in the
project's accent/quantity notation; the experiment retains that complete
ending and tests its actual development rather than substituting *boga*
into the input. Ringe and Taylor's Northwest Germanic reconstruction and
CAPR's PGmc-shaped serialization must not be conflated.

Fulk's *scolu* argument (2018: 56) places lowering before final *ō > u*:
the conditioning vowel was still nonhigh. This is relative chronology,
not an absolute date established for the particular compound *rainbow*.
The experimental extension acts at the existing early operation; the
recorded trace checks that the inherited ending still supplies its trigger.

There is an important precision limit. The current executable excludes a
nasal only as the first consonant after the target, while excluding *j*
throughout the intervening consonants. Ringe and Taylor describe the
nasal restriction in terms of the coda; Fulk additionally discusses
heterosyllabic nasals. Reusing the exact executable restriction does not
prove that it exhausts their historical conditioning. A synthetic
post-first-consonant nasal control makes this discrepancy visible. It is
not silently repaired in this assignment.

## 2. Does this establish lowering under subordinate stress?

Not directly. The decisive passages describe stressed roots, without a
specific derivation of *régna-bùgô*. Extending the same assimilation to
a subordinate-stressed root is a phonological inference. The second
constituent is itself a root, has the relevant vowel and a following
nonhigh vowel, and has no intervening nasal or *j*. Nothing in those
passages supplies a separate blocking condition simply because a
constituent precedes it.

This supports testing an exceptionless accent-conditioned generalization;
it does not license ordinary unstressed medial *u > o* at the early
date. The overbroad experiment intentionally tests that different claim.
Nor does deleting the hyphen in the input parser establish the historical
vowel-harmony domain. The explicit compound control requires a preceding
primary accent; in accepted compound inputs, the grave belongs to the
second constituent. It is not a recovered physical boundary symbol.

Analogy from independent *boga* is possible but not independently
demonstrated for this compound by the consulted passages. It is therefore
not inserted in preference to the regular account. Output identity cannot
decide between these histories: the production cascade already makes *o*
late. The relevant evidence is the ordered intermediate sequence.

## 3. Is *Buga* a decisive counterexample?

It is a real empirical caution, not a law that blocks lowering in names.
Colman (2011: 95–98) distinguishes the noun's *o* from the personal
name's *u*, and discusses both `<BVGA>` and `<BOGA>`. She also warns
about coin design, apparent `<BOIGA>`, transmission to die-cutters,
and uncertain dialectal inference. Counting each spelling or die as an
independent phonological control would misstate the evidence.

The harness deliberately gives the noun and the name diagnostic the
same *búgô* serialization. It consequently cannot derive both *boga*
and *Buga*. No invented alternative ending, name flag, or exception
conceals this limitation. The name does not refute assimilation in the
noun, but the complete distribution of retained *u* is not explained by
this extension. The attested *full*, *fugol* family, *wulf*, and the
sources' other retention cases must likewise not be erased by claims of
unqualified pan-dialectal regularity.

## 4. Can a legitimate inherited adjective cell be tested?

Fulk's strong-adjective table (2018: 209–210, §9.2) gives masculine
genitive singular *-as(a)*, neuter genitive *-as(a), -es(a)*,
masculine accusative *-anōn*, neuter nominative–accusative *-an,
-at-*, and feminine accusative *-ōn*. Thus the leading genitive input
is not an ending invented to satisfy the parser. Both *-as* and
*-asa* are tested, and the neuter *-es* alternative is kept distinct.
The model does not derive the entire adjective paradigm from a
single modern citation form.

Orel (2003: 85) supplies the comparative compound, printed with barred
*đ*. The harness uses CAPR's corresponding internal `ð`; its `θ`
in the first constituent is the project's rendering of *þ*. The
stop-`d` alternative is explicitly a diagnostic, not a silent replacement
of the source's consonant. Stopping an experiment at a rejected input
would not be a sound-change result; a separate private, general ending
extension admits the additional cells without changing downstream laws.

The expected OE genitive ending *-es* has comparative support in
Fulk's table, including OE *langes*. The joint assay demonstrates the
complete development from *-as*, its *-asa* alternative, and neuter
*-es* to predicted *eorþcundes*. It does not thereby establish a
manuscript occurrence. A correct medial *u* with a wrong ending or
extra linking vowel remains only a partial success in the other cells.

The feminine genitive is importantly different. Ringe and Taylor
(2014: 22–23) prefer remodelling of the *-aiz-* endings on pronouns
to an otherwise unsupported early shortening of *ē*. Fulk (2018:
211) discusses competing inherited *-izōz* and *-aizōz* analyses.
The attested *eorþcundre* is therefore a valuable difficult control,
not the compulsory foundation of a purely phonological case history.

## 5. Does OE inflection create a defensible prosodic contrast?

Campbell (1959: 34–35, §§87–92) and Hogg (1992/2011: 49–50,
§2.89) distinguish full compound roots from obscured compounds and
suffixes. Hogg expressly includes *-cund* among elements whose
subordinate prominence depends on their syllabic/inflectional structure.
This does not mean that the PGmc grave necessarily survived unchanged
until late OE.

McCully and Hogg (1990: 323–333) require more than an informal
alternation of strong and weak syllables. Their account is cyclic,
with morphological domains, a left-headed Word Rule, marginal
destressing, and an increasingly explicit quantity condition. Reading
only the preliminary statement on pp. 327–329 misses the decisive
revisions on pp. 330–333. The final OESR requires a branching head,
or a branching foot at the left edge of the domain. Their contrast
between *æþelingas* and *cyningas*, and between *rīdende* and
*wesende*, excludes a rule that stresses every medial heavy-looking
string without first accommodating a light initial syllable.

The relevant derivational inference is:

| Representation | Syllabification/weight | Whole-word analysis tested |
|---|---|---|
| *eorþcundes* | eorþ.cun.des, H H H with closed final *-des* | initial H; medial H can head a subordinate foot; final syllable does not receive stress |
| *eorþcundne* | eorþ.cund.ne, H H L | initial H, then a subordinate H with following weak syllable |
| *eorþcund* | eorþ.cund, H H | second head is word-final and destressed in the ordinary analysis |
| *weruld(es)* | we.ruld / we.rul.des, L H / L H H | initial LH occupies the licensed initial foot; the second nucleus is not independently stressed |
| *æþelingas* | æ.þe.lin.gas, L L H H | initial LL, medial H |
| *cyningas* | cy.nin.gas, L H H | initial LH, no independent medial head |

These are reconstructed prosodic analyses, not readings of accent marks
in manuscripts. At other historical checkpoints the vowels and endings
differ; the actual machine receipt records the strings supplied to the
metrical operation rather than assuming these late spellings throughout.

Goering (2023: 79–84, §§4.5.1.2–4.5.2) independently argues against
an initial light foot followed by a secondary foot in *weruld(e)*.
His initial trimoraic licence and the metrical examples support treating
initial LH together. He distinguishes final feet from stress, and also
allows a qualified exceptional status for final overheavy *-ing* and
*-end*. A model that destresses every final heavy syllable is not a
complete implementation of his account. The experimental implementation
is explicitly the ordinary, single-domain projection, not a replacement
for all cyclic OE stress or a claim that final overheavy exceptions
have been resolved.

## 6. Does the metrical literature compel this phonological history?

No. Minkova and Zhou (2023: 86–90) explicitly resist using compound
avoidance and resolution in verse as a direct diagnostic of
weight-driven speech stress. Their 2023 *Nominal Compounds* article
is distinct from their 2022 functional-stress-shifts paper already
indexed in the repository. The original 2023 article was read directly
from Cambridge, DOI `10.1017/S1470542722000083`. Their findings do
not eliminate phonological quantity, but they prevent treating a
metrical scansion as automatic proof of a historical stress condition.

Russom's publisher abstract (2001: article pp. 39–64) disputes the
traditional medial/final distinction for synchronic compound roots,
while retaining a different treatment for nonroot material.
Bredehoft's verified publisher/Crossref abstract (2003: article
pp. 199–220) argues for stress on monosyllabic second members of
compound names using alliteration and manuscript spacing.
Neither abstract licenses extrapolating its complete argument to a
grammaticalized *-cund* suffix. Their full texts were not accessible
in the bounded retrieval attempts: Cambridge returned an abstract HTML
page for Russom; SAGE returned HTTP 403 for Bredehoft. No page-specific
claim about their internal argument is presented as a direct reading.

Fulk's *A History of Old English Meter* is bibliographically 1992
(Hogg 1996 cites it as 1993). A full local copy was not located.
The discussion of its §196 and pp. 170–171 here is second-hand,
through Hogg (1996: 5, 8), not a claim that those pages were directly
consulted. These access gaps remain limitations on a final adoption
review, not excuses for refusing the executable experiments.

## 7. Is a third stress category or a general dental-nasal block justified?

Neither is established. Hogg (1996: 3–12) deliberately constructs
and then criticizes the explanatory appeal to tertiary stress.
The section on suffix vowels (pp. 8–9) discusses possible segmental
support for *-full* and *-dōm*, and possible levelling for *-fæst*.
It is not a prescription to label every recalcitrant vowel
“tertiarily stressed.” The warnings on pp. 9–10 prohibit precisely
the circular assignment “this vowel survived, therefore it was stressed.”

His p. 11 n. 7 is especially decisive: a labial nasal may help preserve
*u*, but ordinary *-un > -on* demonstrates that a dental nasal
does not supply the same exemption. Hogg's grammar (2011:
239–241, §§6.55–6.58) likewise gives late lowering and its
qualifications, not a universal *nd* exemption. Consequently B4 is
adjudicated as lacking an independently justified new segmental law.
The existing labial-nasal exception is tested; no invented
“before *-cund*” or “before every *n*” alternative is an adoption
candidate. An unsupported alternative is not made respectable merely
by compiling it.

## 8. What would later stress computation mean historically?

A phonological grammar can assign stress to successive representations
after inherited accent annotations have ceased to be relevant. That is
different from asserting a second dated historical deaccentuation.
The experiment preserves the single inherited-grave deletion.
Computed graves in B2 are temporary encoding of a synchronic
classification around the late operation; their decoding is the inverse
of that encoding, not another sound law. B3 composes the same
classification and lowering as one relation.

The restricted whole-word projection can falsify the proposed phonological
rescue, but successful outputs alone cannot prove its full historical
domain. Transparent compound roots require independently motivated cyclic
domains; grammaticalized suffixes may belong to a single domain.
The parser deletes the compound separator early, and the production
`FootCtx` only protects a pretonic first syllable. The final experimental
metrical projection recognizes that prefix phonologically before
constructing the initial foot; it does not use `FootCtx` as a stress
assignment oracle. Neither the separator deletion nor that helper
provides a complete cyclic prosodic analysis. These limits must survive
in the verdict even if a particular cell happens to match.
