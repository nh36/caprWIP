# SC015: twine, numeral adverbs, and the limits of consonant-conditioned lowering

Internal supplementary research assessment, 16 October 2026; branch `update`,
base `ca1b3cd8c9a28d72b756ca4c5d8e90eb4bfc41e4`.

This supplement revises the recommendation in `REPORT.md`, not production.
The new evidence weakens the case for adopting its unified *r/z/st* rule.
In particular, the inherited *twizna-* family makes lowering before every
preconsonantal *z* untenable without an additional, independently defended
premise. The numerical adverbs are less decisive than the twine family,
but their morphology does not warrant dismissing them as fully explained.
The corrected traditional comparator also performs worse, not better.
Retain the incumbent executable temporarily; defer replacement, distinguish
the three historical components, and withdraw the claim that only the
*learn* selections stand between Hill's rule and adoption.

The governing procedure is `RESEARCH_ADJUDICATION_PROTOCOL.md`. No corpus,
selected input, target, registry verdict, chronological edge, production
definition, baseline or reader-facing source is changed. The earlier report,
its compressed experimental receipt, and its chronology receipt remain
historical evidence. Its directly relevant printed-page errors are repaired,
with the change of recommendation explicitly identified here.

## 1. New observations, provenance, and the falsifiable question

The stimulus is private scholarly correspondence from Eugen Hill to Nathan
Hill, 15 October 2026. Only the pertinent scholarly observations are
paraphrased here; the message, signature and contact details are not
reproduced. Hill identified High German *zwirn* 'twine' and the adverbs
*zwiro, zwiror* 'twice', *driror* 'thrice' as possible counterexamples
encountered after his article. He corrected the participle of Old Norse
*bíða* to *beðinn*, not *biðinn*, and referred to Noreen's discussion of
possible influence from *biðja*. He also cautioned that the original
weak-class-III paradigm behind *liornian/leornian*, *lirnēn/lernēn* is
insecure, and that treating *irh* as borrowed follows an existing proposal,
not his independent proof. These observations have different evidential
statuses: the printed forms can be checked independently; his reservation
about an argument is not itself a new sound law.

The testable question is whether consonant-conditioned lowering, formulated
as *i > e* before inherited *r*, preconsonantal/final *z*, or *st*, predicts
the newly examined inherited formations without unsupported restoration,
loans or exclusions. A secure *izn* formation retaining *i* on a branch
which otherwise lowers would refute the unrestricted *z* component.
Conversely, independent evidence that the formation lacked the relevant
conditioner, or demonstrably entered after the change, could remove it.
Merely obtaining *twīn* by choosing a convenient order cannot do so.

The canonical state is reproduced by the isolated full wrapper: 387 selected
rows, 379 exact targets and eight pre-existing mismatches (1973, 2013,
2030, 2162, 2224, 2240, 2298, 2300). The complete live SC015 application
population remains meed 2124, nest 2137 and world 2302. Wether 2283 retains
the authorized `*wéθruz` input and is not a lowering witness. The skip
contrast produces *miorde, nist, wuruld* instead of *meorde, nest, weorold*.
These are model-local observations, not evidence that the selected inputs
or SC015's following-vowel restriction are historically correct.

## 2. The inherited *twizna-* family

### Reconstruction and attestation

Kluge–Seebold reconstructs masculine Germanic *twizna-* 'twine', connects it
with the 'twice/twofold' family, and gives the early verb *zwirnēn* beside
the later noun *zwirn*. Its date rubric distinguishes the ninth-century
noun from eighth-century *zwirnon*. Thus "OHG *zwirn*" is useful family
shorthand, not permission to assign every later noun token an earlier
attestation [@KlugeSeebold2011, p. 1021, s.v. Zwirn].
Orel prints masculine *twiznaz*, with *tvinnr*, OE *twin*, Low German
*twern* and Middle High German *zwirn*, and a derived verb
*twiznōjanan*. He compares Latin *bīnī* and *duis-no-* [@Orel2003,
p. 414]. Kroonen's relevant entry is *twis*, not a nonexistent
*twizna-* lemma [@Kroonen2013, p. 530].

The common source-supported core is therefore short *i*, inherited
*z+n*, and a derivative in *-na-*. The exact nominal/adjectival history
and daughter inflections must remain distinct. The long vowel of OE
*twīn* is not evidence for an inherited long-vowel Germanic root.
Noreen records the Old Norse adjectives *tvennr, tvinnr* 'double', a
comparative formation rather than an Old English target [@Noreen1923,
p. 308, §459].

De Vaan explicitly derives both rhotacized *twirna-* and lengthened
*twīna-* from *twizna-*, with assimilation as a third treatment of
*zn*. His account distinguishes the geographical distributions of
*tweern* and *twijn* rather than reconstructing unrelated roots
[@deVaan2016, unpaginated, paragraphs beginning “Verwante vormen” and
“Zowel”]. This web article has no printed pagination; paragraph anchors,
not invented page numbers, identify the cited passages. Continental
comparisons here test the Germanic hypothesis only; no non-OE runtime
pipeline is investigated.

### The conflicting histories

Three daughter histories must be compared, not combined into a single
supposedly established sequence:

| Account | Earlier steps | Relevant consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Retention plus rhotacism | *twizna- > twirna-* | High German *zwirn-* preserves *i*. |
| Loss plus compensatory lengthening | *twizna- > twīna-* | Traditional native explanation of OE *twīn*. |
| Early lowering, then loss or rhotacism | *twezna- > twēna-* or *twerna-* | Does not yield an unmodified *twīn/zwirn-* history. |

Campbell groups *twīn* with *mēd* as loss with compensatory lengthening,
contrasting retained rhotics in related forms [@Campbell1959, p. 166,
§404]. The held Hogg grammar similarly distinguishes lowering,
rhotacism and loss [@HoggGrammar2011, pp. 59, 72, §§3.20, 4.15–16].
Crist proposes a deletion account and explicitly places it after lowering
and before rhotacism [@Crist2002, pp. 1, 3–6]. That order is problematic
for *twīn* if lowering applied to precisely the same *izn* input: deleting
*z* cannot restore the already lost high timbre.

Kilday instead restricts Crist's law to Saxono-Frisian and treats OE
*twīn* as borrowed, with expected native *tweorn*. His proposal discusses
the linen sense, trade and possible borrowing of *twīnen* before a
back-formed noun [@Kilday2024, pp. 1, 4–5]. This is a specific published
loan proposal, not an explanation invented here to save lowering.
Nevertheless it remains a proposal: the commodity sense supplies
plausibility, not independent proof of every borrowing step. It also
does not explain High German *zwirn-* away.

On the traditional native account, OE *twīn* directly challenges early
lowering before *zn*. On Kilday's account, the OE word is not an inherited
diagnostic, but the continental short-*i* family still challenges a
pan-Northwest-Germanic *z* rule. The Dutch rhotacized forms alone do not
decide whether *e* preceded rhotacism or arose later; they cannot outweigh
the explicit short-*i* evidence by spelling alone [@deVaan2016,
unpaginated, “Zowel”; @Kilday2024, pp. 4–5].

The conclusion is a genuine counterexample to the implemented broad
preconsonantal-*z* generalization, conditional only on the well-supported
inherited formation. It is not yet proof that every possible
syllable-sensitive version of Hill's claim is false. A proposed special
syllabification must be demonstrated independently, not read off the
desired exemption.

## 3. The numeral adverbs and the corrected Norse participle

### What the sources actually reconstruct

The numeral adverbs have a substantially less secure diagnostic prehistory
than *twizna-*. Hirt compares Germanic *twis* with the Gothic prefix and
Norse *tvisvar*, and *þris* with Greek *tris* [@Hirt1931, p. 116, §97].
Kroonen gives *twis*, but also *twiswar ~ twizwar*, expressly preserving
alternative sibilant shapes and an uncertain suffix [@Kroonen2013,
p. 530]. Orel has both *twis-wōz* and *twiz* [@Orel2003, p. 414].
Fulk's comparison of Norse, English, Saxon and High German forms begins
from *twis-wōz* [@Fulk2018, p. 275, §10.10]. These are not interchangeable
reconstructions of one bare monosyllable.

Noreen lists *tvisvar/tysvar*, *þrisvar/þrysvar*, and Campbell describes
the several English *twiwa/tuwa/tweowa*, *þriwa/þreowa/þriga* formations
and particular analogical relationships [@Noreen1923, p. 309, §463;
@Campbell1959, p. 287, §700]. Norse *s* is positive evidence for a
voiceless-sibilant formation. It does not prove that every High German
form also had *s* at the date of lowering, especially when Kroonen
retains *z* as an alternative.

The EWA entry *driror* limits its attestation to the Murbach Hymns,
reviews competing *w*-extensions, rejects a particular *-wauz* account,
and offers a *wa*-stem from *þriz (twiz)*. It specifically explains the
ending *-or* by comparison with comparative adverbs, with *oftôr*
among the models [@LloydLuhrSpringer1998, col. 784, s.v. driror].
The online article was read in full, including its objections, rather
than treating its final proposal as an uncontested derivation.

Crucially, explaining final *-or* is not explaining the root *i* or the
first *r*. EWA's own *þrizwa-/twizwa-* retains a potentially relevant
*z*. It would be incorrect to conclude that all the rhotics are merely
suffixal and therefore no *z*-lowering test survives. Equally, projecting
the later shortened *zwir* backwards as an inherited, stressed,
monosyllabic *twiz* would assume the very point requiring proof
[@LloydLuhrSpringer1998, col. 784; @Kroonen2013, p. 530].

Köbler's entries *zwiro, zwirōr, driror* supply source sigla and date
tags, including glosses and Murbach. They are an unpaginated finding
aid, not a manuscript-level dating of every form [@Koebler2014,
unpaginated, s.vv. zwiro, zwirōr, driror]. No argument below depends
on turning a dictionary's gloss date into a verified first token.

### What analogy can and cannot do

Three distinct proposals should not be conflated. Comparative-adverbial
remodelling of *-or* has explicit EWA support. Remodelling of particular
English adverb formations has explicit Campbell support. Restoration of
root *i* in every High German numeral adverb, or in *zwirn-*, has not
been established by either argument [@LloydLuhrSpringer1998, col. 784;
@Campbell1959, p. 287]. The transparent numerical family makes the last
idea conceivable; it does not demonstrate its direction, date or scope.
No analogical restoration is used as a computational exemption here.

Accordingly *zwiro, zwiror, driror* are unresolved conditional controls,
not three secure independent refutations and not three discharged
exceptions. If the relevant input had intervocalic *z*, Hill's
tautosyllabic formulation need not lower it. If it had *sw*, there is
no *z* conditioner. If it had coda *z* before *w*, it tests the broader
rule seriously. Distinguishing those histories requires an independently
dated formation and syllabification, not counting the surface rhotics.

### *Beðinn*, not *biðinn*

The correction matters: Noreen lists *beðenn* with *bíða* and considers
replacement of *-in-* by *-en-* with lowering, or influence from the
participle of *biðja* [@Noreen1923, pp. 326, 332, §§483, 495 n. 5].
Seebold calls the isolated class-I *e* participle either the expected
regular outcome or, more probably, contact with *bed-ja-*
[@Seebold1970, pp. 94–95]. Fulk finds the lowering interpretation
plausible, notes that **biðinn* is unattested, and does not regard the
analogy as proved [@Fulk2018, pp. 58–59, 286].

Thus the earlier table's *biðinn* cannot serve as a negative observation.
The real *beðinn* gives qualified support to lowering outside *r/z/st*,
but one debated participle does not establish a productive inherited
*-iðan-* lowering rule. The supplementary experiments do not rerun a
Norse grammar or mistake an OE suffix output for a Norse participle.
The correction is evidential, not a new corpus selection.

## 4. *Lizn-*, *twizn-* and *mizd-* compared

| Family | Inherited cluster | Main observation | Principal limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| *twizna-* | *zn* | High German *zwirn-*; OE *twīn* | OE native versus loan history; no demonstrated early lowering in High German. |
| *liznōn-* | *zn* | OE *leornian/liornian*, OHG *lernēn/lirnēn*, OS *līnon* | Original paradigm, mutation triggers and levelling remain disputed. |
| *mizdō(n)-* | *zd* | OE *meord/mēd*; continental lengthened forms | Precise loss/retention chronology and geographical domain. |

The dictionary evidence is Kroonen pp. 340, 370 and Orel pp. 247,
272, 414, supplemented by Kluge's twine entry [@Kroonen2013,
pp. 340, 370; @Orel2003, pp. 247, 272, 414; @KlugeSeebold2011,
p. 1021]. The similar clusters make this a useful comparison, not proof
that all formations underwent identical syllabification, losses or
analogical histories.

Hill explicitly derives mutated *liornian/lirnen* from earlier lowered
*leznijana*, allowing unmutated cells to retain *e* [@Hill2017, p. 153
n. 25]. Jasanoff supplies a serious class-III morphological account,
but reconstructing one possible inherited *i*-bearing cell is not the
same as establishing every selected infinitive and finite form
[@Jasanoff1973, pp. 865, 868]. Ringe–Taylor and Tarsi–Laker discuss
formation and later development rather than providing an uncontested
single original paradigm [@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 38, 84;
@TarsiLaker2026, pp. 42–43].

The supplement therefore withdraws the assertion that *learn* is simply
a solved lowering-plus-mutation case awaiting a technical input edit.
Neither the existing *liorn-* selections nor the experimental *leorn-*
outputs decide the inherited paradigm. Hill's private clarification
reinforces that limit; the published alternatives remain the evidence.

Likewise, a unified pre-dental *z*-loss cannot simply be assumed in all
three families. It would tend to erase the contrast between retained
*r* in *learn* and lengthened *twīn/mēd*, unless its geographical
distribution, timing or morphological interactions were independently
specified. Crist and Kilday offer different solutions to that problem
[@Crist2002, pp. 3–6; @Kilday2024, pp. 4–5]. The experiment's
`AFZnLoss` deliberately exposes the timbre/quantity consequence of
each order; it is not a newly endorsed general loss law.

## 5. The traditional nasal blocker

The earlier experimental A blocked every intervening nasal. That was
too strong an implementation of a familiar classical formulation.
Lloyd describes the received condition as following *a/o/ē*, with no
intervening *j* or nasal-plus-consonant [@Lloyd1966, p. 738].
Campbell explicitly says that redistribution fails before a nasal
followed by another consonant [@Campbell1959, p. 43, §§114–116].
Brunner likewise specifies nasal-plus-consonant, while recording the
many OE *i* retentions troublesome to a general lowering account
[@SieversBrunner1965, pp. 34–35, §45].

This matters for a singleton prevocalic nasal: *hina* is not protected
merely by containing *n*. Lloyd uses such adverbial retentions against
general lowering [@Lloyd1966, pp. 740–741]. Luick separately treats
intervocalic-*m* raising in *niman*, contrasting retained *e* before
*n*; *niman* cannot be treated as a transparent inherited-*i* example
without that premise [@Luick1914, p. 103, §74]. The driver consequently
labels *nímaną* an *i*-vocalism control, not a new source reconstruction.

Not every handbook has identical wording. Hogg's local summary says
“except before nasals”; Streitberg's broad formulation does not supply
all the same explicit qualifications [@HoggGrammar2011, p. 54, §3.9;
@Streitberg1896, pp. 77–78, §94]. Noreen's explicit covered-nasal
condition concerns Norse *u*-lowering; Kostakis's coda/geminate-nasal
analysis likewise concerns *u*, expressly distinguishing its evidence
from *i* [@Noreen1923, p. 56, §61; @Kostakis2025, pp. 4–5].
They corroborate the phonological distinction, not a demonstrated
Germanic *i*-lowering law.

Corrected A therefore blocks *N+C*, permits a final singleton nasal
before the trigger vowel, and retains the following-non-high-vowel
condition. It lowers *xína* and the *nímaną* control but preserves
*bíndaną, wíndaz, rínnaną*. It also admits *lizn-* and *twizn-*:
the last *n* is prevocalic, not followed by another consonant.
The live-corpus difference from old A is the three *learn* rows;
no live singleton-nasal row supplies an additional final difference.
This correction improves fidelity to the classical comparator, not
its historical success. A separate single-intervening-consonant
sensitivity excludes too much, including *nest* and *meed*; it is
not an adequate substitute for the covered-nasal condition.

## 8. Chronology: lowering, rhotacism, loss and quantity

The component sequences discriminate timbre without pretending that
the schematic `AFZnLoss` is a validated production sound law:

| Sequence | *twizna-* result | *mizdō* result |
|---|---|---|
| Broad *z*-lowering, then loss/lengthening | *twēna-* | *mēdō* |
| Loss/lengthening, then *z*-lowering | *twīna-* | *mīdō* |
| Broad *z*-lowering, then rhotacism | *twerna-* | *merdō* |
| Rhotacism, then *z*-lowering | *twirna-* | *mirdō* |
| Rhotacism, then unrestricted *r*-lowering | *twerna-* | *merdō* |
| Restricted *z*-lowering, then loss | *twīna-* | *mēdō* |

Acute/quantity signs and exact starred symbols are retained in the
receipt. The table abstracts them only to display the contrast.
Deleting *z* early preserves *i* in twine but also preserves it in
meed if both have the same loss domain. Rhotacism early removes the
*z* conditioner, but a still-productive unrestricted *r* rule then
lowers both words. Therefore neither “put loss first” nor “lower after
rhotacism” is a complete explanation of the asymmetry.

For monosyllabic *iz*, lowering before the existing final-*z* loss
produces *ē*; reversing the order produces *ī*. This is a conditional
feeding relation for the chosen input and rules, not proof that all
Germanic *z*-lowering shared one date. The production
`EAFRhotacism` component does not rhotacize bare final *z* in this
probe; its behavior must not be generalized into an independent
historical claim.

Hill's stronger dating argument concerns *hir > her > hēr*: short
vowel lowering precedes independently argued monosyllabic *-r*
lengthening, which he places in PGmc using the Gothic and Norse
comparisons [@Hill2017, pp. 148–149, 156–158]. It is a serious
common-ancestor hypothesis, not refuted by the twine evidence.
Nevertheless Gothic *wair* alone is ambiguous because Gothic
breaking could produce its timbre later. Hill expressly explains
this ambiguity before offering *hēr* as indirect evidence
[@Hill2017, pp. 156–158].

Nothing in that argument automatically dates lowering before *z* or
*st*. Separating inherited *r* from rhotacized *r* requires a bounded
historical episode, not an origin tag in a replacement rule. Its
termination relative to rhotacism remains a real question. Earlier
dental rules may create *st* in *nest*; unchanged component closure
and the old chronology receipt retain that test. The supplement
establishes no new dental edge, no new SC015–SC036 relation, and
no production reorder. The earlier world dependency remains a
selected-input/executable constraint, not newly demonstrated history.

## 9. Assessing *r*, *z* and *st* separately

**Inherited *r*: strongest positive case, conditional PGmc date.**
*Wer* and *hēr* provide complementary evidence; the former gives
comparative short-vowel timbre and the latter supplies the indirect
lengthening argument [@Hill2017, pp. 156–158]. Howell's phonetic
discussion strengthens the plausibility of consonantal influence,
not a date or an exact Germanic natural class [@Howell2024, pp. 80–82,
85, 90–91]. The *irh* issue must remain explicit: Kroonen accepts
inherited *irha-*, whereas Hill cites a borrowing analysis
[@Kroonen2013, p. 270; @Hill2017, p. 156 n. 30]. The present
supplement has not independently settled its donor history.
Consequently “probably PGmc” must identify Hill's premises rather
than announce an uncontested date.

**Before *z*: positive evidence but a refuted broad extension.**
*Iz/hiz* and *mizdōn* support lowering before final *z* and *zd*
[@Hill2017, p. 153]. *Twizna-* prevents treating every preconsonantal
*z* as securely included. Learn is not an independent clean confirmation
because its mutation-bearing paradigm is disputed. A narrower rule
is plausible, but the tested obstruent/sonorant restriction lacks the
independent syllabification and wider lexical census needed for adoption.
Its geographical scope cannot simply be labelled “securely NWGmc”
when a continental inherited-*i* formation contradicts the broad domain.

**Before *st*: a real positive item, weak generalization.**
The positive inherited formation remains *nest*. *Nistijan* and
*listiz* can be explained by later mutation under Hill's reconstruction,
but they do not add unmutated positive witnesses [@Hill2017, p. 153;
@Kroonen2013, pp. 339, 391; @Orel2003, pp. 246, 287].
Thistle's temporary experimental lowering and later restoration show
compatibility with mutation, not an independent date for the *st*
event. Removing *st* loses the selected *nest* output; that is not

## 10. Revised verdict, missing premises and implementation boundary

The new evidence **weakens adoption of Hill's broad unified
formalization**, without rehabilitating general vowel-conditioned
lowering. The seven requested answers are:

1. **Does *twizna-* contradict lowering before *z*?** Yes for the
   tested unrestricted preconsonantal-*z* domain. Its short *i* and
   inherited *zn* are well supported. Syllabification or chronology
   could narrow the hypothesis, but neither exemption is established.
2. **Are the adverbs independent counterexamples?** Not securely.
   Competing *s/z*, *w*-extensions and later remodelling prevent
   direct comparison with bare *iz*. They remain conditional
   challenges, not resolved nonexamples.
3. **Can all counterexamples be resolved non-arbitrarily?** Not yet.
   EWA independently supports the *-or* remodelling, not blanket
   root-vowel restoration. Kilday independently proposes a loan
   history for OE twine, not for High German *zwirn-*.
4. **Is one consonant-conditioned law sufficiently established?**
   No. The evidence supports investigating consonantal conditioning,
   not adopting the current union of all three contexts as proved.
5. **Would a split help?** Yes historically: distinguish inherited
   *r*, particular *z* environments and provisional *st*. No
   independently established order currently requires three networks.
6. **Does corrected traditional A remain viable?** As an honestly
   specified comparator, yes; as a demonstrated general OE law, no.
   Its 368 exact results and singleton-nasal overgeneration add to,
   rather than remove, its explanatory burden. *Beðinn* qualifies
   one negative argument but does not rescue the general rule.
7. **What should CAPR do?** RETAIN the incumbent temporarily and
   DEFER replacement. Do not adopt broad B/C or the score-preserving
   restricted diagnostic on this evidence.

Historical reconstruction should therefore retain the strong consonantal
positive arguments while withdrawing the claim of an established unified
domain. Stage and chronology should preserve Hill's PGmc *r* hypothesis
as a specifically premised account, leave *z/st* dates unresolved, and
add no canonical edge. Foma implementation remains byte-identical.
Reader-facing discussion should eventually distinguish these components,
correct *beðinn*, and avoid claiming that all comparative negatives
are discharged; this research task makes no book edits.

Before replacement, the missing work is precise: establish the
syllabification and relative formation dates of *twizn-/lizn-* versus
*mizd-*; discriminate native and borrowed OE twine histories without
assuming a universal loss law; reconstruct the numeral adverb's
conditioner at the relevant date; settle the load-bearing *learn*
cells and mutation triggers; assess *irh*'s loan evidence; and find
an independent unmutated *st* witness or retain that component as
explicitly provisional. These are separate questions, not an instruction
to resume a larger survey or change selected forms.
