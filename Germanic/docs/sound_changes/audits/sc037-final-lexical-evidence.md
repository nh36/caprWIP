## Question

Can seleweard or the older name evidence independently establish a
pre-SC037 light-root high-linker compound and a genuine old/new historical
discriminator? Inheritance supported by comparative evidence or a
defensible later reconstruction would suffice; explicit attestation of a
Proto-Germanic citation form is not required. An Old English spelling
alone does not establish a pre-change input.

The three propositions must remain separate: a reconstructable complete
Proto-Germanic word; an independently reconstructable later pre-SC037
word; and a suggestive attested compound with uncertain earlier date.
Only the first two license historical executable inputs. Alternative
reconstructions do not automatically disqualify a word.

### Seleweard

Orel 315 prints *sali-warđaz ~ *sali-warđuz, with Old Norse sal-vǫrðr
and Old English sele-weard. BOTH compounds have first-member i.
The a/u alternatives concern the second member's inflection, NOT
competing compound linkers. Orel 448 independently compares the guardian
noun across Germanic and explicitly identifies the Norse u-stem.
There is no need to invent a complete nominative ending.

Kroonen 424 reconstructs an inherited neuter s-stem, with nominative
*salaz and genitive *salizaz. Old Norse salr/salir and Old Saxon seli
support secondary i-stem *sali-; Old English salor reflects *salaza-.
A form *salaz is not by itself a thematic a-stem *sala-.
Orel's alternative simplex stems therefore cannot be substituted for
his two printed i-linked compounds. Orel's neighbouring *sali-gastiz
and Runic Swedish sali-gastiR independently corroborate an early sali-
combining form; they do not date the guardian compound or establish an
English ancestral stage of that particular word. No new rune date or
PGmc-to-OE guest derivation is claimed.

An inherited *sali-ward- compound is a defensible comparative hypothesis:
its components, first-member i, semantics and the daughter reflexes are
not inventions. However, the reviewed distribution supplies no diagnostic
shared innovation or fossilized morphology distinguishing inheritance
of this particular transparent compound from independent formation.
The latter is specifically reported by Orel via Carr, rather than
invented to avoid a computational difficulty. The Norse form does not
preserve the first-member vowel, while an Old English formation using
sele and weard after the relevant change predicts the same attested
first member. Neither history can presently exclude the other.

The conditional structure is primary-stressed *sá.li- plus a
subordinate-stressed guardian root; first syllable CV is light and the
unaccented linking i is high. This is the ordinary compound prosodic
analysis, not a measured pre-SC037 stress attestation. If inherited, the
structure is appropriate for the retention question. The missing
premise is its placement before SC037, not the availability of the
morphemes or the ability to write a grammatical ending.

Clark Hall 260 cites Beowulf 667. The Harrison--Sharp fourth-edition
public-domain Gutenberg text (9700; revised macron version 9701)
actually numbers the sele-weard line 668, immediately following
Grendle tō-gēanes; these edition-specific locators must not be silently
equated. Its “hæfde … sele-weard āseted” construction supplies masculine
accusative singular, surface-homonymous with the nominative. No fresh
manuscript reading or independent dating of the compound is claimed.
The two printed nominative hypotheses above are not silently labelled
that attested accusative. Ordinary case reconstruction would be
legitimate once the word's history and stem class were established;
it need not be explicitly printed in a dictionary.

Assessment: first-member morphology/high-vowel identity strongly
supported; inherited compound and pre-SC037 date plausible but not
sufficiently discriminated from the explicit parallel-formation
analysis. The earliest secure English evidence is the attested word,
not an independently dated pre-SC037 English ancestor. Stage labels
and confidence are independent descriptions; low confidence has not
been used to relabel an input as Proto-Germanic or Old English.

### Older names and contrary evidence

Sievers--Brunner section 168 note 1 explicitly gives light-root
high-vowel losses Sigfrið, Hyglāc, Cynwulf, Haðberht and Badheard,
contrasting retained forms. Campbell section 348 and Hogg 6.21
explicitly recognize the same distribution; Luick 305 treats metrically
old names, distinguishes chronological layers, and says that the
circumstances are not yet understood. These are positive historical
counterexamples to an EXHAUSTIVE heavy-first-root requirement.

Sweet's first-hand Liber Vitae transcription independently gives
uinifrith (LV 361, p.163), hyglac (including LV 167, p.158, and LV267,
p.161), and sigfrith (LV234, p.160; original printed page checked).
His introduction dates that hand to the late eighth/early ninth century
and excludes later additions; that is his palaeographic assessment,
not a new identification of a named individual's lifetime. The
manuscript evidence confirms real retained and contracted spellings,
but does not by itself date their formation or the loss. It must not be
conflated with Boniface or other similarly written names.

Winifrið/Winefrið is positive light-root retention, not proof that every
old high-linker compound retained its vowel. The reported contracted
names cannot all be dismissed by stem variation: Brunner's section 263
notes specific i-/s-stem histories, and Campbell/Hogg specifically
separate Selred/Selered based on sel/sele. That justified qualification
does not explain Sigfrið or Hyglāc automatically. Nor does an attested
contracted form prove a closed PRE-loss syllable: loss itself creates
the consonant cluster. The underlying short-root comparison and
second-member prosody must be considered independently.

The handbooks' high-vowel classification and light-root comparison are
material counterevidence to the reader's universal claim. They do not
yet supply independently dated complete inputs with sufficiently
specified first-stem form, historical prosody and selected cell to
choose one replacement general law. No name-conditioned law, lexical
diffusion, optional deletion, or unsupported analogical restoration
is adopted.
