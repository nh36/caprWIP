## A. Original nasal loss and subsequent loss of nasal contrast

Ringe, second edition (2017), 104–106, §3.2.2(ii), distinguishes final
`m > n`, loss of final `n` with nasalization in polysyllables, and later
creation of new final `n` by consonant loss. His principal examples include
the accusatives of yoke, wolf, wool, place, and son. The survival of `þan`
and the added pronominal particle supplies evidence concerning
monosyllables. Some nasal-vowel reconstructions are inferential: the
distinctive reflexes of final nasal long o are stronger direct comparative
evidence than those of every other nasal vowel.

The actual held original is the 2017 second edition. Its printed
104–106 must not be confused with 101–103 in another edition or an older
CAPR locator. The original was freshly extracted and consulted.

Ringe–Taylor (2014), 58–60, reconstructs `gebōz` and `gebǭ` together
as `gebā`, contrasting trimoric `gebôz > gebō`. Their `tungǭ > tungā`
and `hertǭ > hertā` are crucial: nasal long o does not remain a distinct
back-vowel trigger through English root restoration. This establishes
the loss of the relevant nasal contrast by the reconstructed PWGmc
long-a stage; it does not establish one instantaneous denasalization of
every quantity and vowel throughout West Germanic.

CAPR's SC023 is a citation-form normalization, not evidence for a late
historical nasal loss. Its present late `ǭ` representation, however,
has a phonological downstream consequence. SC072 eventually consumes
the symbol, too late to prevent spurious root restoration. Historical
dating cannot be inferred from the SC023 executable position.

## B. Quantity and inherited versus remodelled endings

Ringe–Taylor, 58–60 and 299–300, distinguishes surviving bimoric final
o (unrounded to a) from trimoric final o (shortened to bimoric o).
Final `r` belongs to the former unrounding environment, followed by
shortening; ordinary unstressed nonfinal environments have a different
quantity history. The nominatives of masculine and feminine weak nouns
are not interchangeable: `namô > namō > nama`, but `tungǭ > tungā >
tunge`. A feminine citation in `ōn` must not cause every n-stem ending
to be treated as the same quantity.

Hogg's monograph, 64–65 §3.32 and 227–231 §§6.26–30, shares neither
every quantity assignment nor exactly this reconstruction. Its ordinary
final long-o history, nasal-conditioned outcomes, and retained long-vowel
inventory must be distinguished. His trimoric examples include
ō-stem genitives, with an explicit qualification that not all endings
can safely be assigned that quantity.

Fulk (2018), 84–90 §§5.4–6 and 170–171 §7.31, presents the
bimoric/trimoric distinction, the alternative interpretation as
uncontracted sequences, disputed inherited origins, and remodelled
n-stem paradigms. His quantity table reproduces an ordinary
`ōz > e` versus trimoric `ôz > a` contrast; his discussion also
preserves the competing trimoric-genitive analysis. Kaluza's law cannot
be used mechanically to settle every bimoric/trimoric assignment.

Agreement that quantities were distinguished is not consensus about
every paradigm cell. This investigation keeps CAPR's inherited inputs
fixed. Its executable tests evaluate the Ringe–Taylor quantity hypothesis;
they do not independently adjudicate the PIE origin of every ending.

## C. Unrounding: environment, scope, and serialization

The source-supported Ringe–Taylor proposition is a merger of surviving
bimoric oral and nasal long-o outcomes in the relevant final
environment before English final-a fronting, followed by quantity-sensitive
shortening. Their printed 60 comparative `r` examples concern a related
unrounding environment, not permission to collapse all final-vowel laws.

CAPR's narrow SC042 is locally correct for oral-final rest. Combining
its final-o operation with the nasal counterpart is a coherent hypothesis,
but does not solve the before-final-r implementation or every quantity
question. The experiment preserves SC042's existing polysyllabic
consonant-separated context. A broader environment must be separately
justified before implementation.

SC020 must expose a protected oral ending in time; SC108 must follow
the merger; root restoration must not still see a fictitious nasal
back-vowel contrast. These are relevant relationships, not evidence
that every intervening executable stage is historically adjacent.

## D. Fronting and restoration: competing historical analyses

Ringe–Taylor, 167–193, especially 189–193, adopts fronting, breaking,
restoration, then phonological palatalization. `slēan` supplies their
argument for an earlier front vowel before breaking; comparable retained
back roots need subsequent restoration. A restored vowel requires an
actual back vowel at the restoration stage, not the mere presence of a
nasal label whose vowel has already merged and fronted.

Their printed 192 explicitly gives front-root feminine nominatives:
`flaskā > flæskǣ` and `marā > mærǣ`, beside back-vowel obliques
`flæskōn-` and `mærōn-`. Their final arrow is marked as a development
not reducible to ordinary sound change. It is not legitimate to omit
that notation and call the observed late nominatives regular outcomes
of a retained nasal back vowel.

Campbell (1959), 60–61 §§157–159, explicitly derives singular
`asce`, `flasce` after inflected `ascan`, `flascan`. Luick,
printed 152–153 §161, likewise identifies the oblique donors.
Hogg, 93–100 §§5.35–38, discusses nominative levelling in feminine
n-stems and genuinely different dialectal ordering: southern `slēan`
versus Northumbrian `slā`, with Mercian complications. These accounts
are not independent attestations of a universal absolute chronology.

Versloot (2021), 85–88, disputes using reconstructed intermediates as
independent evidence, and qualifies the runic arguments. Versloot's
final-paginated article (2026), 111–113 and 122–124, proposes restricted
early fronting, including blocking before a following back vowel,
and a different later breaking/mutation chronology. Its Mercian
evidence and omission of some traditional restoration environments are
relevant to flask, not a reason to import its complete reconstruction.
The held filename says 2025 because of online/copyright dating; the
issue citation is 30.1 (2026), 103–141.

The current experimental comparison tests the conventional account.
A whole conditional-fronting alternative would also change breaking,
dialect interpretation, and mutation assumptions. It cannot honestly
be evaluated by toggling one restoration line while keeping the rest
of that chronology conventional. No SC044 change is authorized here.

## E. Palatalization: original vowels, later vowels, and nasals

Ringe–Taylor, 203–204, distinguishes initial velars, medial stops,
singleton voiced fricatives, and sk. Noninitial stop palatalization
before a following vowel requires high front `i/ī`, not any front
vowel. Singleton g has wider front-vowel environments. Final and
preconsonantal contexts are separately formulated.

Their 208–209 `lenġu`, `lenġ`, `strenġ`, `genġe` provide positive
evidence against prohibiting ng palatalization wholesale.
Tongue's `ng` is a stop cluster; it is not the singleton fricative
in an intervocalic-g rule.

Hogg, 254–260 §§7.16–25, distinguishes stop and fricative eligibility
and allows palatalization transmitted through preceding n after i/j:
`drinċ`, `þinġ`, `hrinġ`. Brunner (1965), 179–180 §215, rejects
a palatal interpretation of many original-ng spellings, distinguishing
them from former ngi/ngj. The disagreement is material: nc/ncg
spellings cannot be mechanically equated with one pronunciation.

Hogg (1979), 89–113, is a separate study, not the 1992 historical-phonology
chapter or the monograph. Its argument about allophony versus
phonologization must not be silently converted into support for all
Ringe–Taylor chronology. Existing CAPR SC045/SC052 records preserve
this disagreement.

CAPR currently applies its stop-g proxy before any front vowel. That
overgeneralization predicts `tunġe` after the early merger. Narrowing
the following-vowel condition to original high front vowels is
independently motivated, not a lexical exception for tongue. A separate
Hogg nasal-mediated component is a legitimate competing diagnostic,
not automatically an adopted rule.

Flask raises a different issue. Ringe–Taylor actually prints
`flasċe`, with a palatal mark. Campbell's plain sc and later x spellings
do not by themselves establish a velar pronunciation. Root-vowel
levelling is positively sourced; restoration of the consonant from
an oblique donor requires a separate argument.

## F. H-loss, allophones, and attested intermediates

Ringe–Taylor, 314 §6.9.3, places intervocalic h-loss after Anglian
smoothing and before contraction. Hogg, 271–275 §§7.45–51,
describes lenition and loss of singleton x between voiced segments,
with loss posttonic; geminate xx is excluded. His §7.46 gives retained-h
early glossary forms, including `thohae` and `uulohum`, while warning
about non-etymological h and analogical retention. Brunner,
181–182 §218, likewise distinguishes singleton loss, contraction,
and consonant-adjacent lengthening.

The loss concerns the historical h resulting from a velar or palatal
fricative. No reviewed account supplies a general exemption for
intervocalic palatal ç. CAPR's SC085 consumes x only. The prototype's
`tāhe` is therefore a legitimate intermediate, not evidence against
the early merger: the front ending makes x palatal, and the present
h-loss proxy misses it. Existing contraction already handles the
subsequent relevant vowel sequences.

This licenses a bounded x/ç allophone comparison, not unrestricted
h deletion. Initial h, geminates, consonant-protected h, and pretonic
stress-foot onset must remain controls. The inherited proxy's stress
scope must be disclosed rather than asserted correct merely because
the four disputed words are posttonic.

Hall (2007), 299–302 and the attestation table 313–314, distinguishes
weak mære/mare/maran from strong mær and separates textual witnesses
from copies of the same incuba gloss. The gloss family has early
textual origins, not that many independent early attestations.
Actual mare and maran occur in the Leechbooks: the riding-mare
passages and the maran list are different grammatical evidence.
His correction of the Epinal mera reading must not be replaced by
a speculative masculine mara paradigm.

The original Bosworth–Toller Supplement, 223, flasce, supplies
singular and oblique flask evidence, including Gregory's Dialogues
66.3–10 and 141.25–30, with flascan/flaxan and flascena/flaxena
variants. These demonstrate the donor forms; they do not establish
a securely dated early singular/oblique minimal pair.
Clark Hall's flasce/flaxe and mare/mære distinctions are lexical
corroboration, not independent chronology.
Kroonen, 505–506 (toe) and 526–527 (tongue), preserves comparative
formation alternatives. Orel's marōn entry supplies weak mære,
not an argument that its root vowel is long.

## Independently checked held-out evidence

The companion witness file is fixed before new execution. Its strongest
new constraint is the clay h/hiatus contrast, alongside toe and the
dat.pl. fringe control. All remain outside the accepted 394 forms.
The glossary edition and the grammatical reconstruction are separate
evidence layers. In particular, the Latin lemma identifies clay,
but does not itself prove the nominal case.

Sweet's original printed 39 was image-checked: Corpus 141 reads
`allox: tahae`, not an invented modernized tāhe. Ringe–Taylor's original
314 was image-checked for the corresponding clay, toe, roe, and
fringe sequences. Different glossary copies are not a longitudinal
record of one speaker or dialect. The retained-h and hiatus forms
prove that intermediate stages need representation, but not an
absolute change date inferred solely from manuscript age.

## Models and predictions fixed before new execution

Baseline: unchanged CAPR, 394 singly generated selected forms,
386 matches and eight inherited mismatches.

Model A: authenticated early-merger-only prototype, reused. It merges
oral/nasal low-root controls, preserves the trimoric distinction, and
changes the four already recorded finals. It is not the complete
historical account: it retains neighboring stop-g and h-allophone defects.

Model B: A plus restriction of the following-vowel stop-g condition
to high front vowels, and intervocalic loss of both x and ç.
Prediction: oral/nasal bimoric controls behave identically through
every later sampled stage; trimoric controls differ. Tongue returns
to tunge; toe reaches tā; regular flask and mare retain front-root
nominatives. The three changes are phonological, not lexeme lists.
Other changed corpus forms must be disclosed, not suppressed.

Model C: B plus Hogg's preceding-i-through-n eligibility for k/g.
Prediction: original high-front/nasal controls palatalize where B
does not; ng before nonhigh front vowels without preceding original
i remains velar. This is a competing phonetic eligibility analysis,
not a new late palatalization triggered by mutation.

Model D is not a productive transducer patch: distinguish regular
flask/mare nominatives from attested later paradigm levelling. No
word-specific rule or automatic accepted-target change is proposed.

Refutation conditions: loss of quantity contrast; a nasal/oral
postmerger difference; palatalization of tongue's ng solely before
secondary nonhigh front vowels; failure of posttonic palatal h-loss;
deletion of geminate/initial h; unexplained missing/multiple forms;
or historical witnesses that contradict the predicted intermediate.
Model C must be evaluated separately against the disputed original-ng
evidence, not selected by its match count.

## Consultation limits

This memorandum reports particular original passages, not complete
cover-to-cover consultation of every requested grammar. Full DOE
dictionary/corpus authentication is not claimed. Sweet's original
edition is available; Pheifer's manuscript-specific notes and Hall's
separately cited editions are not all independently checked here.
The proposed witnesses therefore retain manuscript/date and
case-identification qualifications.

Boutkan, Schrijver, Hill, and Kaluza-law studies are bibliographic
leads reported through Fulk, not newly read originals. They constrain
how confidently quantity consensus can be claimed, but the executable
comparison does not choose among their complete inherited paradigms.
No inaccessible original is represented as consulted.
