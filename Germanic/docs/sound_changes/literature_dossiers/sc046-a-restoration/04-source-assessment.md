### The *slēan* inference

Campbell §157 and Ringe--Taylor pp. 189--190 reason from *slēan*
'slay': if its diphthong arose by breaking of front *æ*, then a fronted
antecedent existed even before *x*. A following back vowel therefore
did not generally block fronting. The same phonetic conditioning is
then extended to earlier *dægum*, with *dagum* explained by later
retraction. The first step is a derivation under an adopted breaking
analysis; the second is a regularity inference, not an attested
*dægum* stage. Hogg §5.38 makes this a southern (West Saxon/Kentish)
argument, not an invariant chronology for every Old English variety.

Versloot (2021:93--95) supplies a concrete alternative: fronting,
intervocalic *x*-loss and contraction can yield the diphthong without
the same prehistoric breaking operation. This still fronts the vowel
of *slēan*; it does not prove that *dagum* stayed back. It does mean
that a diphthong in a hiatus history cannot by itself prove the order
of breaking before *rC/lC*. Neither source's account can be rejected
merely because it requires an unattested intermediate.

Northumbrian *slāa* allows restoration before breaking under Hogg's
account. Hogg prefers this over wholesale analogical replacement
where unlevelled survivals would be expected; Fulk (1998:150--151)
explicitly makes the Northumbrian inference conditional on that
interpretation. Campbell's levelling account is a competing
explanation, not observationally disproved by one form. Neither
Northumbrian *slāa* nor Mercian glossary distributions are evidence
for simply reversing CAPR's West Saxon order.

### Conditioning, assessed class by class

| Environment | Source-supported observation | What does not follow |
|---|---|---|
| One consonant before a historically back vowel | Campbell §158, Hogg §5.35 and Ringe--Taylor pp. 189--192 support restoration in the ordinary open-syllable domain. *Sadol, gafol, hara* and the *dæġ/dagas* paradigms are central. | An attested root *a* alone does not distinguish retraction from blocked fronting. A later *e* in the ending does not demonstrate an originally front trigger. |
| Geminates | Campbell lists *cassoc, hassuc, mattoc, crabba, hnappian, racca, lappa*. Hogg §5.36 preserves both positive examples and *cæppe, tæppa, tæppian, hættian*, with *hnappian/hnæppian* and *læppa/lappa* variation. | Neither a universal ban on geminate restoration nor a mechanically selected exception for *pp* is established. Hogg himself doubts that the apparent *pp* tendency is nonaccidental. Positive *crabba/racca* must constrain any restrictive candidate, independently of the corpus's troublesome *tæppa*. |
| *sC* | *Wascan/ascan/flascan* support *sk*; *brastlian* supports *st* with further consonantal complexity. Hogg distinguishes these from *sp*. | Campbell's examples do not license every arbitrary *sC* as equally established. A consonant class remains a modelling generalization until its negatives have been checked. |
| *fC* | Campbell's *saftriende* is the familiar example. Hogg's §5.35 discussion identifies the very narrow basis (*saftriende*, AntGl1249; *aft*, Rune4), and doubts its reliability. | This is particularly weak evidence for a productive all-*fC* rule. It is also insufficient by itself to abolish that class in the West Saxon production model. Record the uncertainty rather than invent a lexical exception. |
| Consonant plus liquid | Campbell notes a few *appla/apla, watrode, acc(r)as/acras* cases, while other clusters generally resist restoration. | A few exceptional histories do not prove an unrestricted *Cr/Cl* law. Barrack's compressed feature environment must not silently expand the handbook's evidential domain. |
| Target stress | Ringe--Taylor explicitly restrict retraction to stressed *æ*. Their *hegtis/hægtesse* history (p. 192) uses an unstressed second *æ* remaining front until mutation and syncope. | “Stressed target” does not mean “stressed following vowel.” Root stress and trigger-vowel quality/history are separate variables. A late proxy for stress must also respect pretonic and secondary-stress representations. |
| Following vowel | Campbell §159 includes following vowels later fronted or lost (*gaderian, staþelian*, class-II histories). Weak nominal and verbal paradigms show that the surviving ending need not preserve the old trigger. | Neither all modern back-looking endings nor only surface back vowels are a historically adequate trigger criterion. CAPR's oral/nasal and weak-tail distinctions require explicit local tests. |

Barrack rule 157 targets a short stressed low front vowel; the matrix
and its compressed intervening-consonant specification were checked
in the original page image. Rule 162 instead concerns a long vowel
and a West Saxon non-dental restriction. It is not a second version
of the same short-vowel law. His theoretical use of free variation
does not authorize grammatical conditioning or lexical diffusion
in CAPR.

### Levelling and synchronic representations

Ringe--Taylor's pp. 193--196 explain substantial analogical
redistribution in strong verbs, nouns and adjectives. Hogg §5.37
explicitly distinguishes those synchronic paradigm patterns from the
earlier phonetic change. Use that positive paradigmatic evidence
where it applies; do not extend it to *tæppa* simply because the
current rule predicts *tappa*. The selected corpus row's older
analogy annotation is an inherited hypothesis, not independent
proof that a regular phonological account is impossible.

Dresher's restoration-loss explanation of Mercian second fronting is
documented separately in `01-dresher-1985.md`. Its nonalternating
*habuc/gabul/sadol* and *hwalas* argument concerns the grammar
acquired by later speakers. It is not reducible to a claim that the
three early glossaries are successive phonetic snapshots. Seiler's
common-exemplar transmission analysis limits that inference.

Versloot's 2026 article explicitly excludes West Saxon from its
early-Anglian experiment (p. 107). His ST implementation omits
geminate/*sC* restoration, partly to improve performance; pp. 122--125
and n. 27 make that choice visible. The high agreement of the two
accounts (pp. 128--129) and unresolved *hebuc* remain important.
The author's statistical fit, probabilistic treatment and appeal
to lexical diffusion do not select an exceptionless CAPR law.

## Predictions registered before the bounded comparisons

1. *Bake/bast*: the live oral following vowel should trigger retraction
   in *bake*, whereas the nasal weak tail in *bast* should not. This
   diagnoses CAPR's trigger implementation, not a proof of an oral/
   nasal distinction in all historical restoration.
2. *Day/craft*: reuse SC041's removal of the final trigger as bleeding
   evidence. They should already lack that vowel before SC046 and
   remain front. Do not duplicate its whole-corpus displacement assay.
3. *Bake/wade*: hold SC047 fixed; applying restoration before SC048
   should retain *a*, reversing only SC046/SC048 should lose that
   opportunity and retain *æ*. Report intermediates and remainder,
   not an inferred final spelling. The result establishes a local
   implementation dependency; historical ordering requires the
   sources independently reviewed above.
4. *Tæppa* and geminate controls: omitting geminate restoration
   should retain *tæpp-* but also incorrectly retain front vowels
   in source-backed *crabba/racca*. *Flascan* must keep its oral
   protected ending trigger; *mære* must not acquire one.
5. Stressed-target candidate: use the existing post-fronting stress
   representation to prevent retraction of a genuinely unstressed
   linker while preserving first-root and explicitly secondary-stressed
   retraction. On *rainbow/earthly*, expect a different linker
   intermediate if the current rule overgenerates. Whether syncope
   erases that difference or preserves an endpoint delta is to be
   observed, not specified after seeing the result.
6. Every proposed restriction gets a row-wise full current-corpus
   comparison of output sets, rejection and ambiguity. A match gain
   cannot resolve the historical geminate problem. These finite-input
   assays do not implement a whole alternative early-Anglian chronology.
