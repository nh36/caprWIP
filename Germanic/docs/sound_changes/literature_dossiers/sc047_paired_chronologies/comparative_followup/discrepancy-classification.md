# Historical classification of the five frozen SC047 differences

This is a research interpretation of the frozen 394-row comparison, not a
second lexical catalogue. A and B use immediate stranded-glide vocalization;
B2 changes only B's post-nasal-loss treatment of already geminated *wwj*.
No stem transfer is implemented or counted in any arm.

No row is assigned an established explanation merely because an experimental
output is closer to its target. The unresolved direct-cell history of hay and
hue is not paid for by the matching B2 endpoints.

## What the labial experiment can and cannot choose

Campbell §120.2 and Hogg §§3.18, 4.11–14 place diphthong formation
after gemination and retain palatal conditioning through mutation. Hogg
explicitly keeps *j* into that stage, then loses it after the high diphthong,
normally losing *w* after the low one. He calls the late conditioning
obscure and gives *show* with the opposite glide loss; the length in the
*sew* evidence is uncertain. His §3.19 concerns short vowel plus *u*, not
the *i.i* left by A, and §§5.131–134, 5.149 chiefly concern the later
resolution of hiatus with a long stressed nucleus. This is compatible with
lengthening followed by loss of the second vowel, but is not an independent
demonstration of the particular early short-*i.i* rule. The *glī* and *nī-*
comparisons in Ringe–Taylor p. 259 warrant the bounded diagnostic, not a law
for all short high-vowel sequences. Section 5.32(4) gives a different kind of transfer in
*searu : searwes*: there the oblique supplies a broken vowel, not a doubled
stem. Section references are from the held reissue of the 1992 text.

Ringe–Taylor pp. 53, 246–250 posit a still-palatalizing fronted semivowel
on the protected *iwj* route to West Saxon *hīew*, and expressly leave its
phonemic analysis open (p. 250). FNG encodes the availability of that
conditioning, not an observation of an independent surviving *j* or proof
that an inherited English direct cell carried it. The relevant West
Saxon diphthongs and Anglian reflexes need dialect-specific mutation and
can be inherited from a protected or a direct stem. The experiment does
not choose between those histories or provide a dialect-neutral sound law.

Fulk pp. 71–72 §4.10 and p. 72 n. 1 challenge the consonantal-*w* starting
point for *strew* and *new*. He derives *strew* from inherited *au* and
predicts a *w*-retaining West Saxon reflex if the cluster were consonantal;
he permits regularization for *new*. Ringe–Taylor pp. 53, 173, 246 instead
posit a reversion from the palatalized geminate to *au*. Fulk §§6.15–16,
pp. 126–130, establish the wider phonological environments, not a resolution
of this disagreement. A securely classified West Saxon class-I *strew*
form that distinguishes retained *w* from a palatal would help; Anglian
*stregan* and the remodelled class-II *streowian* do not supply that test.
A secure early direct-versus-protected contrast with localized spelling
and independently established stem structure would test whether the
conditioning was inherited there rather than introduced by stem transfer.
