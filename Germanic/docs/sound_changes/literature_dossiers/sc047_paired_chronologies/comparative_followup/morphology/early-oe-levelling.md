# SC047 — early Old English stem alternations and the direction of levelling

**Decision: keep the chronology open and retain production.** Early
English attests a singleton singular beside doubled plurals in *mete*,
including both shapes on one line of the same ninth-century gloss.
That gives positive evidence for cell- or number-specific restructuring
in nominal paradigms. It does not document a geminated oblique replacing
the inherited vowel-final direct singular of a light neuter ja-stem.
The comparative preference for early loss still rests primarily on
continental *peti/bedi*; the new English evidence does not independently
shift the balance between early and split loss.

See the [witness register](early-oe-witnesses.md) for individual readings,
cases and exclusions, and the [intake and reading record](../../../../../../../docs/references/sc047_early_apocope/nominal-levelling.md)
for the originals. This supplements, rather than retroactively changes,
the [earlier primary-source recommendation](primary-review.md).

The discriminating claim is that both inherited short low endings
were lost early, so final *j* vocalized before it could double the
preceding consonant, whereas a following vowel allowed doubling in
protected cells. An independently identified light ja-stem direct
singular in *-Ci/-Ce*, alongside a protected donor and later doubled
tokens of that same direct cell, would support the early-loss plus
levelling history if class change, regional divergence and secondary
formation could be ruled out. Conversely an early inherited doubled
nasal accusative beside a securely inherited singleton oral nominative
in the same masculine paradigm, before possible transfer, would
favour split loss. A mixed written paradigm alone shows neither step.

## Dahl (1938): what split loss does, and what it assumes

Dahl pp. 81–82 separates masculine nom. sg. *-jaz* from masc. acc.
sg. and neuter nom./acc. sg. *-jaⁿ*. In his derivation the oral ending
is lost before gemination; the nasal ending survives, then drops.
The newly final glide vocalizes after a consonant; high *i* is kept
after a light syllable and lost after the syllable made heavy by
gemination (pp. 85–86). Thus the masculine outcomes differed before
syncretism: *\*husjaz* gives *hysi* in the nominative, but the nasal
accusative gives hypothetical *hyss*. The neuter *cynn* is regular
in the singular as well as in its independently protected plural.
The distinction is one of inherited vowel history, not a sound rule
conditioned by case. Dahl leaves open whether gemination preceded
West Germanic differentiation and treats the nasal apocope as dialectal
(p. 85). He does not make pan-West-Germanic timing follow from English.

His strongest argument is cumulative, not circular by design. The
expected survival of nasal over oral vowels has a typological and
comparative rationale (p. 82), but the contrast in bimoric vowels
is not short-vowel evidence, and Gothic and Proto-Norse only show
that the timing need not have been uniform. The endingless OE
neuter type, and OS/OFris *bed, flet*, are economical on B (p. 83).
Weser *kunni* appears to place a doubled consonant early, before
OS high apocope, **if** the sequence is a free inherited direct
singular and the double is not secondary. Dahl recognizes the
interpretive uncertainty but treats the reading and early age as
arguments against irregularity (Lasch 1930–31 pp. 174–175, cited there).
The modern [object record](https://www.runesdb.de/find/205) still does
not make its segmentation, case or freedom from levelling independent.
A reading of two n-runes is not a phonetic or morphological analysis.
Dahl rejects Walde's alleged OS *kuni* as unattested and takes later
OS *kunni, beddi, fletti* and OHG *kunni, betti* as possibly modelled
on *ija*-stems (pp. 83–84). Those ending-bearing forms are therefore
not additional unmodified retentions in **his** chronology.

On pp. 84–86, *hyse, mete, ile*, and late *dyne* motivate an earlier
overlap in *-i* between masculine ja- and i-stem nominatives. Shared
shape would facilitate class interaction. It supports early **oral**
loss if inherited ja-stem identity is secure; it does not itself
test **nasal** loss. Dahl assigns *hyse* to the ja-stems on Ekwall's
analysis and the inflected distribution reported by Bäck (1934
pp. 176–179). But the proposed derivation from *hos* is conjectural,
the early names are equivocal, and a productive double from ja-stem
influence is an alternative to inheritance. At pp. 84 and 166 Dahl
himself calls the doubled inflection of *mete* and *ile* secondary
in his treatment of i-stem plurals. *Dile* has continental ja-stem
comparanda and genuine English *dili* beside *dil*. The Corpus
manuscript, f. 8v, confirms singleton *l* plus *i*; it cannot
identify the gloss as a particular inherited direct singular.
*Dyne* is not early in Dahl's sample
and the North Germanic comparison does not fix English inflection.

Under B, widespread masculine nom./acc. identity gives an incentive
for syncretism (pp. 85–86); the accusative, all the other doubled
cells and the neuter model together favour the *hrycg*-type.
Conversely the nominative can generalize with i-stem support in
the *hysi*-type. Attributing the double of *hyll* specifically to
its accusative is the application Hogg makes of Dahl's reasoning;
no early *hyle : hyll* nom./acc. pair is cited. Dahl's pp. 74–81
already admit secondary doubling, simplification and uncertainty in
particular suffixal and scribal settings. His *heri* control
(pp. 86–90) is not a diagnostic for the nasal date: *r* does not
geminate. The inherited oral nominative *huljaz → \*hyle* is the
more direct experimental control in Hogg's argument, but it is a
prediction shared by A and B, not an actual early singleton token.

Dahl p. 82 reports that Dal relies on OHG *hewi, gewi*, barely
addresses the labial groups as groups, and omits Weser. Dahl
objects to extrapolating those isolated regional/labial forms
across West Germanic. That is a real objection to the scope of the
inference; it does not explain the independently parsed nonlabial
OHG *peti/bedi* collated in [the earlier review](peti.md).
Dal's actual formulation and possible controls remain **mediated**;
neither the 1934 original nor its 1971 reprint was found here.

## Hogg (1979): independently useful morphology, dependent chronology

Hogg §5 distinguishes regular *j*-gemination, syllabification,
loss of *j* after a heavy stem, and unstressed lowering (pp. 68–71).
Table (23), p. 69, is a **hypothetical pre-analogical** paradigm
with nom. *\*hyle*, acc. *hyll*, gen. *hylles*, dat. *hylle*, and
doubled plurals. Hogg explicitly contrasts it with Brunner and
Campbell, under whom both direct cases would give *\*hyle*. He
accepts Dahl's nasal timing to obtain the doubled accusative.
He expressly says the distinct direct-case paradigm is absent
from extant texts (p. 70). In the wider paper (§§2–4, 6–7), his
case studies and conclusion concern when sound alternations cease
to be rule-governed for speakers, not independent evidence for an
English prehistoric accusative. In particular he rejects
reordering synchronic rules as an explanation merely because it
can generate the desired output (pp. 71, 73, 82–83).

Hogg's independent contribution is to compare plausible **directions**
of syncretism. The unmarked or frequent nominative and the i-stem
model *wine* favour the singleton. The numerous doubled cells,
stem uniformity and the neuter model *cynn* favour the geminate.
He judges the latter preference slight, not predictive for every
word (p. 72). The coexistence of *hyll* and *hyse*, with doubled
non-direct *hyssas* (also *mete/mettas* and *ile/illas*), is evidence
that a uniform synchronic rule ordering is a poor explanation of
the lexical contrast (p. 73). *Maldon* independently supplies
*hyse*, gen. *hysses*, and plural *hyssas* beside *hysas*;
see the [witness register](early-oe-witnesses.md). It is not a sequence of observed
forms in which an accusative or an oblique demonstrably replaces
the nominative. Treating *cynn* as originally uniform in that
argument would itself presuppose the result being tested.

If both endings went early, *hyll* still has protected doubled
donors and could level in their direction; *hyse* could preserve
its direct shape, if inherited ja-stem, or simply preserve
an i-stem singular while adopting ja-inflection elsewhere.
*Mete* is independently an i-stem (compare Gothic *matis*);
*ile* lacks equivalent independent assurance of ja-stem ancestry
in the English cells. Thus the observed outcomes are compatible
with A and B, even though A lacks the special accusative donor
and pressure to resolve a distinct nom./acc. pair. Hogg supplies
a plausible structural bias, not proof of a productive
neuter-class extension. Neither his proposed *\*hyle* nor his
discarded *\*hyss* can be entered as a manuscript attestation.

## Adamczyk (2018): observed distributions, not transplanted rates

Chapter 1 distinguishes interparadigmatic transfer from extension
within a paradigm (§§1.3.2–4), productivity from the frequency of
an individual token (§1.4.5), and token conservation from phonetic
reduction (§1.4.6). The procedures (§1.5) count identifiable
competing inflections, not every historically innovative form.
Shared exponents and a replacement already complete at the first
attestation can disappear from the innovation denominator.
Distribution of an incoming inflection and reconstruction of the
direction of transfer are therefore distinct claims.

The exact comparable counts below are **tokens**, not lexemes,
with innovative / eligible in each cell. The OE row re-expresses
Table 3.2, p. 149; the continental rows are Tables 4.5 and 5.5.
All three rows concern light **i-stems**, not the light neuter
ja-stems under adjudication.

| Sample and class | Nom. sg. | Acc. sg. | Nom. pl. | Acc. pl. | Eligible total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| OE light masc./neut. i-stems, p. 149 | 18/311 (6%) | 29/635 (5%) | 33/49 (67%) | 133/168 (79%) | 213/1163 (18%) |
| OFris light masc./neut. i-stems, p. 265 | 16/127 (13%) | 5/26 (19%) | 8/9 (89%) | 5/5 (100%) | 34/167 (20%) |
| OS light masc./neut. i-stems, p. 338 | 3/90 (3%) | 0/105 | 1/13 (8%) | 4/19 (21%) | 37/334 (11%) |

OE Table 3.2 also lists 171 gen. sg., 55 gen. pl., 465 dat. sg.
and 86 dat. pl. outside the calculable total. The gen. sg. is
already remodelled in the sample; Adamczyk estimates that including
it would reduce measured singular archaism from 95% to 80%.
Dat. sg. *-e* may be inherited or borrowed (p. 147 n. 118);
common gen./dat. plural endings obscure their origin. In OS the
total includes 11/73 (15%) innovative dat. sg., 5/5 dat. pl.
and 13/29 (45%) instr. sg., alongside the direct counts. Only
3 OS gen. sg. tokens are recorded, none included as contrasts.
OFris Table 4.5 includes only three light neuter tokens (all
*spere*); the shared or ambiguous gen./dat. cells are not in the
total. The wider OFris i-stem summary (p. 268, Table 4.7, omitting
exceptional *liude*) shows 163/513 innovations in eligible
singulars and 201/215 in plurals; it is not a light-neuter sample.

Adamczyk reports doubled stems in **86% of eligible plural vs.
4% of eligible singular forms** of the OE subgroup capable of
exhibiting gemination (p. 150). The passage does not give those
subgroup denominators; they cannot be recovered from Table 3.2,
which counts broader inflectional innovation. Her central
example is *mete* (pp. 147, 150, 490–491): the doubled stem is
largely plural, with occasional endingless/doubled singulars
(*mett*, also *-wlitt* in the wider group, in Northumbrian or
Mercian material). That is evidence for penetration of another
stem pattern, not a dated chain from one internal oblique to
the direct singular. It is also evidence **against assuming**
that once a double was available it spread immediately or
preferentially to the singular. Table 3.6 (p. 155) pools
different eligible i-stem subsets: nom. sg. 6% of 311; acc. sg.
15% of 1,252; nom. pl. 78% of 105; acc. pl. 80% of 305.
These are the published rounded percentages, not exact
innovation counts for those pooled rows.

The period and dialect controls are essential. Early Mercian
material retains *-i* in relevant light i-stems (pp. 146, 150);
the rare Anglian doubled direct forms show that conservatism is
not absolute. For heavy feminine i-stem plurals, Anglian *-e* can
be either inherited or borrowed after ō-stem syncretism; Early
West Saxon tends toward *-a* in the donor ō-stems. Late unstressed
vowel convergence obscures the distinction again (pp. 148,
151–154); an exclusion from tabulation does not mean absence.
The general early/late comparison for **minor stems**, Table
3.59 p. 234, has 28% of 1,705 eligible early tokens and 41% of
9,156 late; it does not supply a diachronic count for the light
ja-stems. See pp. 130–133 on attribution, mixture and the
preponderance of West Saxon material. Low-frequency cases can
innovate early (especially the genitive singular and oblique
plurals), while the frequent nominative usually resists
replacement (pp. 497–498); formal overlap in that same
nominative can **enable** transfer elsewhere (pp. 487–489).
Stem-class productivity, contrast between singular and plural,
and transparent exponents can matter separately from frequency.

OS is comparatively conservative in light i-stem directs but
shows particularly strong ja-/jō-stem attraction where the *i*
element gives formal overlap (pp. 338, 343–344). OFris shows
markedly more plural innovation than singular and, in a different
subclass, direction-sensitive restructuring among feminine
obliques and directs (§4.5.2.3, pp. 268–273; OE comparison p. 155
n. 127). The comparative conclusions (pp. 519–527) make
gradual, class- and cell-sensitive transfer credible. None of
these corpora makes the donor direction or pre-literary date a
portable rate for English neuter ja-stems.

## Comparative judgment, propagation and residue

| Hypothesis | What the evidence requires |
|---|---|
| **A — both losses before gemination** | Regular vowel-final singleton direct, doubles in protected cells. Donors such as *nette, bedde, wedde* are real. A class pattern is possible, not eight independent sound exceptions, but productivity and direction remain unproved. Either a doubled stem spreads before high apocope (making the syllable heavy), or an already doubled bare stem, plausibly like a direct plural, replaces the earlier direct. Neither step is witnessed for the principal neuters. |
| **B — oral loss before, nasal loss after gemination** | Direct neuter doubles inherited; the masculine oral/nasal split supplies an extra doubled accusative donor. Best local economy for the attested English neuters. The relevant unlevelled masculine contrast is reconstructed, and the regional or secondary origin of the southern singleton-plus-high-vowel needs independent support. |
| **C — gemination before both losses** | Direct doubles can be inherited in both cases. The southern singleton-plus-high-vowel still requires Boutkan's independent simplification/vocalism or inflectional account. Neither *mete* nor any other new witness establishes that southern development. |

**Historical classification:** final short-vowel apocope and early
West Germanic doubling remain distinct regular sound changes;
prosodic weight, glide adjacency and boundaries must be justified
independently. The English morphology is attested by the ninth
century, not dated to the preliterary continental phonological
stage. The evidence does not independently demonstrate either
edge between nasal loss and gemination, and the executable order
does not entail a historical edge. *Mete* is positive evidence
for a mixed paradigm and secondary ja-stem influence; it is
not positive evidence that *nett* once had *nete* or that
all eight neuters were levelled. Final spelling variation,
reclassification and the feminine suffixal parallels are
different phenomena from within-class stem replacement.

**Research verdict:** DEFER the historical edge; RETAIN the
executable. Canonical metadata is unchanged:

`Registry-verdict: SC047=REFORMULATE/RETAIN`

**Propagation:** only this dossier, register and private-holding
metadata. No new corpus entries, generated publication changes,

**Next useful evidence:** (1) establish the inherited English
class and syntax of *dili/dile* independently of their predicted
phonology, preferably with a protected English form from the
same paradigm; (2) acquire Dal and check her original rationale
for the geography, morphological classes and *-i* evidence;
(3) establish a second nonlabial Continental singleton beyond
the bed lexeme, especially *weti*, with a manuscript, case and
date, or a firm new interpretation of the Weser sequence.
Only a genuinely discriminating direct form or dependency would
justify another run. The extant comparisons already produce the
relevant alternate outputs; a fresh sweep, or reopening *hay*
and *hue*, would not decide the philological question.
