## Traditional short-i lowering and its criticism

The older tradition relates lowering to a following non-high
vowel, with differing consonantal restrictions and degrees of
common inheritance. The comparable *u > o* development cannot
automatically establish an equally general *i > e* law
[@Streitberg1896, pp. 56--57; @Hirt1931, pp. 45--46;
@Luick1914, pp. 108--109; @Campbell1959, pp. 43--44;
@Fulk2018, pp. 55--59].

Lloyd's participles and adverbs challenge unlimited appeals
to levelling. Cercignani distinguishes phonemic inventory
from individual conditioned developments; the Norse velar
restriction is not a demonstrated general West Germanic
onset condition. Ringe–Taylor limit the secure early English
evidence and distinguish southern developments
[@Lloyd1966, pp. 738--745; @Cercignani1979, pp. 72--82;
@Cercignani1980, pp. 126--134; @RingeTaylor2014, pp. 33--36].

## Hill's proposal and its selected implementation

Hill places the explanatory weight on following consonants:
inherited *r*, tautosyllabic *z*, and *st*, contrasting *sk*.
Later mutation can conceal earlier lowering where its
conditioning morphology is independently justified.
The selected Foma implementation approximates tautosyllabic
*z* by preconsonantal/final *z*. That approximation is not
an independent reconstruction of syllable boundaries
[@Hill2017, pp. 153--156].

The rhotic argument is strongest. Howell supplies phonetic
plausibility, not proof of a shared historical date.
Hill's *hir > her > hēr* makes lowering precede monosyllabic
lengthening under his assumptions about the precursor and
the relevant Germanic lengthening. Ringe–Taylor preserve
alternative histories. None of this directly dates the
*z* or *st* components
[@Hill2017, pp. 149, 156--158; @Howell2024, pp. 81--82, 90--91;
@RingeTaylor2014, pp. 36--37].

## Counterevidence not discharged by adoption

The inherited *twizna-* family preserves a high vowel in
both English loss/lengthening and continental rhotic
histories; early lowering would change the predicted
timbre. De Vaan discusses alternative *zn* treatments.
Kilday's specific English borrowing proposal is not proved
and does not explain away the continental evidence
[@Orel2003, p. 414; @KlugeSeebold2011, p. 1021;
@deVaan2016, unpaginated, paragraphs “Verwante vormen” and “Zowel”;
@Kilday2024, pp. 1, 4--5; @Crist2002, pp. 3--6].

Numeral-adverb sibilants and extensions must be distinguished
rather than projected from every later rhotic. ON *beðinn*
or *beðenn* has competing lowering/analogy accounts.
OHG *irh* has inherited and borrowing analyses.
Weak-III *learn* morphology supplies possible mutation
sources but no certain complete paradigm
[@Hirt1931, p. 116; @Kroonen2013, pp. 270, 530;
@Noreen1923, pp. 326, 332; @Seebold1970, pp. 94--95;
@Fulk2018, pp. 58--59, 286; @Hill2017, p. 156, n. 30;
@Jasanoff1973, pp. 865, 868; @TarsiLaker2026, pp. 42--43].
